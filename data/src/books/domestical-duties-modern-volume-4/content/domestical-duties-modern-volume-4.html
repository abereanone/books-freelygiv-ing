The First Treatise: On Ephesians 5:21–6:9

Servants and Masters in the Apostle’s Words

The First Treatise: On Ephesians 5:21–6:9 · §§. 123–133

§. 123. Of the subjection which believing servants owe.

Because there is yet another order in the family besides those noted before, namely the order of masters and servants, the apostle also prescribes their duty to them.

As he began with wives and children in the two former orders, so here he begins with servants, who are the inferiors, for the same reasons1 given before.

The apostle is somewhat full in setting out the duties of servants, and in urging them to perform their duty; and that for two special reasons: one with respect to those whose masters were unbelievers, the other with respect to those whose masters were saints.

1. In those days, when the2 gospel was first preached to the Gentiles, there were many servants converted by its preaching whose masters did not embrace the gospel. So those servants began to imagine that, being Christians, they ought not to be subject to their masters who were unbelievers.

2. There were other servants whose masters believed the gospel as well as they did. Now because the gospel taught that “there is neither slave nor free … for you+ are all one in Christ Jesus,” they thought that they ought not to be subject to their master, who was their brother in Christ.

The apostle hints at these two wrongheaded and presumptuous notions, and expressly answers them in3 another place. And because they had taken too deep root in the minds of many servants, the apostle here in this place labors all the more earnestly to root them out, and that by pressing thoroughly on their conscience the subjection by which they are bound to their masters, as masters, whatever their masters’ disposition might be. More of this4 later.

Here, by the way, note three points.

  1. The gospel does not free inferiors from the subjection5 to men to which they are bound by the moral law.
  2. Men are ready to turn the grace of God into liberty.6
  3. As errors begin to sprout up in the church, ministers7 must take care to root them out.

§. 124. Of the meaning of the fifth verse.

Eph 6:5.

Servants, be obedient to those who are your+ masters according to the flesh, with fear and trembling, in singleness of your+ heart, as to Christ.

This title (servants) is a general title, which may be applied8 to all who by any outward legal bond or right9 owe their service to another, whatever the sex of the persons themselves, and whatever the kind of their servitude, whether more servile or more free.

Servile, as those who are born servants, or sold as servants, or taken in war, or ransomed. For in ancient times those who were taken in war and saved from death were called servants.

Free, as those who are made servants by voluntary contract, whether at will, as some menservants, journeymen and laborers, or for a certain term of years, as apprentices, clerks and the like. Therefore, whatever the birth, parentage, standing or former condition of any may have been, being

Servants, they must be subject, and do the duty of servants: the10 apostle’s indefinite title (servants) admits no exception of anyone.

The other title (masters) has as large an extent, comprising11 under it both sexes, masters and mistresses, and of these all sorts: great and lowly, rich and poor, strong and weak, believers and unbelievers, true professing Christians and the profane, superstitious, idolatrous and heretical persons, and the like; so that

No condition or disposition of the master exempts a servant12 from performing his duty to him.

Among other degrees and differences, let it most especially be noted that both sexes, mistresses as well as masters, are meant here, so that the duties enjoined to be performed to masters may correspondingly be performed to mistresses (so far as they are common to both), and that both by maidservants and also by menservants who are under mistresses. In families mistresses are as common as masters, and therefore I thought it good to make special mention of this.

Under this word (obey) are included all the duties which13 servants owe to their masters. It is the same word that was used before in the first verse, and it has as large an extent here, applied to servants, as it had there, applied to children. It shows that

The rule of servants (as servants) is the will of their master.14

This clause (according to the flesh) is referred by some to the action of obedience, as if the apostle added it to show what kind of obedience servants owe to their masters, namely a secular, bodily obedience in temporal things, as opposed to the spiritual obedience which is due to God alone.

Answer. Though a distinction may be made between the service which is due to God and that which is due to man, yet this application of the phrase in this place may give servants occasion to think that if they perform outward service to their masters, all is well, and they owe them no inward fear or honor. That is an error which the apostle here chiefly opposes.

But because this clause (according to the flesh) is directly15 joined to masters, I refer it to the persons to whom obedience is to be given, and so take it as a description of them, as if he had said, to fleshly or bodily masters.

The apostle describes masters in this way for these reasons.

1. For distinction: to show that he means masters who are of the same mold as servants, so distinguishing them from God, who is a spirit. In the same way the apostle distinguishes between fathers of our flesh and the Father of spirits.16

2. To forestall an objection: so that servants might not say, our masters are flesh and blood as we are, so why should we be subject to them? To counter that notion, the apostle expressly says that obedience is due to masters after the flesh.

3. For mitigation of their servitude: for since their masters are flesh, they have no power except over the bodies of their servants; their servants’ spirits are free from them. In this respect the apostle calls Christian servants the Lord’s freemen.17

4. For consolation in their present condition, which lasts only for a time, because their masters are flesh: whatever is according to the flesh does not last long, but has its appointed end.

5. For direction: to show in what things especially the obedience which belongs strictly to a master consists, namely in secular, outward things. For everyone must be served according to his nature: as God, being spirit, must be served in spirit, so man, being flesh, must be served in the flesh. Now this service in the flesh is not opposed to sincere and upright service, but to spiritual service. So, by implication, what some take to be the chief meaning may also be intended.

Objection. Masters may command spiritual things, namely to worship God, and in such and such a manner.

Answer. On his own authority a master cannot command such things: there must be a higher warrant for doing them than the commandment of a man.

A main point intended here is this, that

Masters are not to be lightly regarded because they are after the18 flesh: that is, weak, frail, short-lived, of the same nature as servants.

So that, on the strength of this description of masters, servants19 should not take too much heart to themselves, the apostle adds this clause (with fear and trembling, etc.), which relates to the manner of their obedience. No slavish fear is meant here, as if servants should live in continual dread, or tremble at the sight of their masters. A servant may be brought to do so by the tyranny of some master, but to do so is no Christian duty. What the apostle requires here is a duty belonging to all Christian servants toward all sorts of masters, even the mildest there are. It is therefore an awe-filled respect for a master’s authority, and a dutiful reverence for his person, that is required here; and it is opposed to sauciness, impudence, boldness, defiance, talking back, grumbling and muttering against their masters, and other such vices. To show how foul those faults are, and what great respect servants ought to bear to their masters, these two words (fear and trembling) are joined together. In20 effect they declare one and the same thing, yet for explanation’s sake they may be distinguished. For21 fear signifies a reverent respect for someone: it is what was required of wives in the previous chapter. Though the thing required of wives and of servants is in general the same, yet the particular manner and measure of a servant’s fear is far different.

Trembling22 belongs more especially to servants: it is a dread of punishment. And it is required of servants, not as if they should do all things solely for fear of punishment, but because God has put a staff into a master’s hands, servants must tremble at the power their masters have, and fear to provoke them to strike. To this purpose the apostle says to subjects, with respect to the power which a magistrate has, “be afraid, for23 he does not carry the sword in vain.”

From this learn that

The authority of a master ought to strike a servant’s heart with24 dread.

The dread which servants ought to have of their master’s power and authority makes many care for no more than to avoid their master’s displeasure. Therefore the apostle adds a further degree of a servant’s subjection, namely that25 it be in singleness of heart, that is, honest, entire, upright: for this is opposed to hypocrisy, pretense and fraud. Indeed, it must be of YOUR+ heart, not another’s. Another, in the simplicity of his heart, may think you do a thing better than you do, by putting the most charitable interpretation on everything; but if you do it in singleness of your own heart, it will in truth be as it appears to be. So that

All the services which servants perform for their masters must26 be done in truth and uprightness.

The apostle gives this direction to Christians, who have to do not only with masters according to the flesh (who only “see the outward appearance”) but also with the master of spirits,27 who “sees the heart.” And therefore he also added this clause, as to Christ, teaching servants by it that

Servants, in their obedience to their masters, must approve28 themselves to Jesus Christ as well as to their masters after the flesh.

This phrase (as to Christ) implies as much as the phrase (in the Lord) of which we spoke29 before.

§. 125. Of the meaning of the sixth verse.

Eph 6:6.

Not with eye-service, as men-pleasers, but as the servants of Christ, doing the will of God from the heart.

This verse and the one following are added as a further explanation of the last clause of the previous verse. It seems that servants, however they might in some measure perform the main duty of obedience, yet failed exceedingly in the manner of performing it. Because masters were only men, masters according to the flesh, who neither had power over the heart nor could discern its disposition, servants thought they had done their duty well if they had outwardly performed what their master required. Now, to root out this bitter weed and to reform this corrupt notion, the apostle is fuller in making clear the point of sincere and upright service. Therefore, to express his mind and meaning more fully, he first lays down the vice contrary to the sincerity named above (for contraries laid side by side bring out one30 another all the more), and then returns to declare the virtue more distinctly. From this note that

Those points are most to be urged on people in which they31 most fail.

The vice noted here as contrary to sincere service is called32 eye-service. Our English word answers the original properly and fitly, both in its derivation and in its true sense and meaning. It implies a merely outward service, only33 to satisfy the eye of man.

And it is twofold:

  • Hypocritical.
  • Fawning.

Hypocritical service is what is merely for show: when something is claimed to be done which in fact is not done; as if a servant should come from his master’s work all in a sweat, as if he had taken extraordinary pains at it, whereas he has done nothing at all, but has made himself sweat some other way, or only made a show of sweat.

Fawning service is what is indeed done, but only in the master’s presence. Such servants are those who will be very diligent and faithful in doing the things their masters see, or that will come to their notice; but otherwise, behind their master’s back, and in things which they hope will never come to his knowledge, they will be as negligent and unfaithful as if they were no servants. Yet to satisfy their masters and to humor them, they will do anything, however unlawful.

From this vice, thus exposed, note that

God requires more than what may satisfy man’s eye.34 God’s eye is a piercing eye, and can see much foulness where to man’s eye all things seem very fair. So they greatly deceive themselves who think all is well because no man can say to them, black is your eye.

Those who content themselves with doing eye-service the35 apostle here calls men-pleasers; and he gives them this title for two special reasons.

1. To show the ground of eye-service: which is that all their care is to please their master, who is a man. For they know well that man can see only the outward show, or what is done before his face.

2. To show the heinousness of that sin: for it is tainted with atheism, in that the man guilty of it has no regard for God, but prefers his master before God. He does not care whether he pleases God, so long as he pleases his master. For this is the force of that word (men-pleasers): it is spoken in opposition to God, as the apostle implies in another place, saying, “If I still pleased36 men, I would not be the servant of Christ.”

Objection. How can it be so heinous a sin to be men-pleasers,37 when the apostle advises servants to please their masters in all things?

1. Answer. The advice given there is not simply to please, but to38 please well, as the original word properly signifies, and as the King James translators have fitly rendered it.

2. Answer. That general phrase (all things) must be restricted39 to the duties of a servant, and to all the parts of obedience which he mentions there in the words directly before. Servants therefore must please their masters in all things which their masters have power to require of them, and which they are bound to do. The men-pleasing spoken of here is opposed to the pleasing of God; the pleasing of men mentioned there is subordinate to our pleasing of God. Here, to please men is to flatter them in everything, good or evil; there, to please them is to obey them conscientiously in every lawful thing. Here is condemned a seeking to please men in the first place, and that only, and wholly in everything; whereas we ought first to seek God’s approval, then the testimony of a good conscience, and after these a pleasing of men, but in, for and under God. From this, then, observe that

A servant must not give himself wholly to flattering and pleasing40 his master: for so he may in many things highly displease Almighty God.

To avoid the two sins named above, eye-service and men-pleasing, the apostle gives an excellent direction in these words, as the servants of Christ, doing the will of God from the heart. Here we may observe that servants of Christ are set against men-pleasers, and doing God’s will from the heart against eye-service.

Servants of Christ are those who know that their masters are in41 Christ’s place, bear his image, have their authority from him, and are under him; so that in serving their masters they serve42 Christ. And so far as they can serve both together, they will. But if the two turn out to be contrary masters, so that one of necessity must be left, then they will hold fast to the highest master, who is Christ. In this respect they are called the Lord’s freemen, 1 Cor 7:22. So we see how a servant may43 be no servant, if he does all things for the Lord.

From this contrast between men-pleasers and servants of Christ, note that

Those who in all things give themselves to please men are no servants of Christ.

So that we may know better who are servants of Christ,44 the apostle adds a description of them in these words (doing the will of God from the heart). Christ’s will is God’s will: for as Christ is God, the Father’s will and his are one and the same; as he is man, he orders his will wholly by his Father’s: he does not seek45 his own will, but the will of the Father who sent him.

The apostle adds this description of a servant of Christ partly as a direction to servants, to teach them how in serving their masters they may be servants of Christ (namely, by having an eye to God’s word, in which his will is revealed both for the matter and for the manner of all things they do), and partly as a motive to persuade them to be content with their place and to do their duty cheerfully, because such is the will of God.

God’s will is what must direct and settle everyone in the46 things they do: for God’s will is the rule of what is right. Everything he wills is entirely right, and nothing is right that swerves from his will.

To put a difference between Christ and other masters, and to show that he does not look (as man does) on the outward appearance, but sees the heart, the apostle adds this clause (from the heart). And it declares that

A good thing must be well done. To do what is God’s47 will, commended by his word, is in substance a good thing; to do it from the heart is the right manner of doing it. What is good and is done in the right manner is well done.

§. 126. Of the meaning of the seventh verse.

Eph 6:7.

With good will doing service, as to the Lord and not to men.

In this verse the apostle presses home yet again the point named above concerning servants’ manner of obeying their masters, and their care in it to approve themselves to their highest master. From this observe that

Matters that are needful and weighty are to be pressed again and again.48 This is a needful point, because servants fail exceedingly in it; and it is a weighty point, because all the comfort and benefit of service consists in God’s approval. But the former point is not merely and barely repeated here, but set down in such a way that other good directions are given to servants49 for their manner of obedience.

1. To serve with good will is somewhat more than with singleness of heart. For it further implies

1. A readiness and cheerfulness in doing a thing: doing it with a good mind, as the derivation of the Greek word50 shows.

2. A desire and endeavor that their masters may reap profit and benefit from their service, by which they show that they bear good will and a good mind toward their masters.

In setting down servants’ duties, the apostle uses a different word from the one in the fifth verse, namely this one (doing service),51 by which he shows that a servant’s place and duty is of a lowlier and more inferior kind than the place and duty of a child or a wife. The former word52 (obey) was common to all; this word53 (doing service) belongs only to servants, and the very title of a54 servant is derived from it. From this note that

Though wives and children are inferiors as well as servants, yet55 servants must not look for such privileges as they have. Another manner of subjection must be performed by servants.

The clause added (as to the Lord) is in effect the same56 as that in the fifth verse (as to Christ), for by the Lord he here means the Lord Christ. But it is added to answer an unspoken objection. For servants might say, You require us to serve57 our masters with good will; but what if they are hard-hearted, and do not regard our good will, but abuse our good mind? The apostle58 gives them this answer: do not look so much to men and their reward as to God and his reward; serve men in and for the Lord, even as if you+ were serving God, and so your+ service will not be in vain. The eighth verse, which follows from this as an inference, shows that this is what the apostle intends here. Learn therefore that

An eye is to be cast on God even in the duties which we perform59 to men: and that both for approval and for reward from God.

The negative clause which follows in these words (and not to men) is not to be taken absolutely (for then it would contradict the apostle’s main aim in this place) but comparatively, in relation to God; and that in two respects.

1. That service is not to be done only to men.

2. That service is not to be done to men in and for themselves. Service must be done to God as well as to men; indeed, in the service60 which we do to men, we must serve God. Men must be served for the Lord’s sake, because the Lord has commanded it, and because they bear the Lord’s image and stand in his place: in the Lord, and under the Lord.

From this full declaration of the manner of doing service61 to masters, note the difference between those servants who are servants of men and those who are servants of Christ.

  1. Those do all for the eye. These, all from the heart.
  2. Those seek to please men. These do the will of God.
  3. Those do their service discontentedly. These, cheerfully.
  4. Those do all out of self-love. These, with good will.

§. 127. Of the meaning of the eighth verse.

Eph 6:8.

Knowing that whatever good thing any man does, the same he will receive from the Lord, whether he is slave or free.

Great is the ingratitude of many masters: they will demand62 all the service that a poor servant can possibly do, but repay his pains meagerly; indeed, perhaps reward him very badly for it, giving him not sufficient food, clothing and lodging, but frowns, rebukes and blows. Now, to uphold servants in such straits, and to encourage them to do their duty whether their masters regard it or not, the apostle in this verse labors to raise their minds to God, and to show them that God regards them and will reward them sufficiently, so that

Servants’ labor will not be in vain in the Lord. To press63 this encouragement on them all the more, he sets it down as a thing granted by all, so clear that none of them can be ignorant of it (knowing), as if he had said, you+ all know64 well enough that what I now say is most true. From this note that

God’s regard for faithful servants is so well known that none who65 have any understanding can be ignorant of it.

The apostle’s argument is drawn from the general to a particular,66 and the generality is noted in the thing done (whatever) and in the person who does it (any man). But because the generality of the thing might be stretched too far, he adds this limitation (good); and because the generality of the person might be restricted too much, he adds this explanation (whether he is slave or free). This distinction is used because in those days many servants were slaves, both men and women. Now the apostle’s argument may be framed like this: Everyone, of whatever condition and rank he is, will be rewarded by God for every good thing he does, whether it is great or small. Therefore every servant will be rewarded by God for every good service.

The recompense promised is set out in a concise phrase (the same he will receive), meaning that he will receive67 a reward for the same. That phrase refers to the crop which a farmer receives from the grain he sowed, which is of the same kind as he sowed:68 the seed being wheat, the crop is of wheat; the69 seed being sown plentifully, the crop will be plentiful. To the same purpose this apostle says in another place, “Whatever a man sows,70 that he will also71 reap.” Now, to apply this: servants who by their faithful service bring honor and glory to God will receive honor and glory in return. If they ask from whom they will receive it, the apostle expressly answers, from the Lord. For it is the Lord who72 said,73 “I will honor those who honor Me.” God will not forget them, though their masters may.

From this verse, thus explained, I gather these particular observations concerning servants.

1. Servants may and ought to apply to themselves general74 promises made to Christians. Otherwise this general argument of the apostle would be to little purpose in this place.

2. A Christian may be a slave: for the apostle directs this encouragement to Christians, among whom he presupposes75 some to be slaves, setting them against free men, who were also servants.

3. Faithful service performed to men is a good thing: for the76 good things which servants especially do lie in their service.

4. As God does not accept men because they are free, so neither77 does he reject them because they are slaves. It is not the person, but the work, that he regards.

5. The faithful service of servants is like good seed sown: it78 will bring forth a good crop. The metaphor hinted at here implies as much.

6. God is honored by the faithful service of servants: this is79 hinted at by the application of God’s reward to them, for God honors none but those who honor him.

§. 128. Of the connection of masters’ duties with servants’.

Eph 6:9.

And you+ masters, do the same things to them, forbearing threatening, knowing that your+ Master also is in heaven, and there is no respect of persons with Him.

To the duties of servants the apostle joins the duties of masters, saying, AND you+ masters. From this learn that

Masters are as much bound to duty as servants. A like doctrine was noted from the connection of parents’ duties with children’s: there you+ may see this general point further enlarged,80 §. 115.

1. God’s law requires as much: for it expressly enjoins many duties on masters (as we shall see in the eighth treatise, which follows).

2. So also does the law of nature, which has tied master and servant together by a mutual and reciprocal bond, of doing good as well as of receiving good.

3. The law of nations also requires as much. For in all nations where there was ever any good government, and where wise and good laws were made, particular laws have been made about the duties of masters.

4. So too does the law of equity. One good turn deserves another; therefore the apostle says to masters, “give your+ servants what is just and equal.”81

Now let masters take note of this, and know that God, the great Lord of all, has made this relation between master and servant, and has set each of them in their separate and distinct places for the mutual good of one another, so that servants are no more for the good of masters than masters are for the good of servants. Therefore, as they look for duty, let them perform duty. If servants fail in their duty, let masters see whether they themselves are not the cause of it, by failing in theirs. Their authority will be no excuse before Christ, but a means to make their fault heavier and increase their condemnation: for the greater the talent, the more diligence is expected, and the stricter the account that will be demanded.

§. 129. Of the meaning of this phrase, Do the same things.

These two titles (masters, servants) are taken here as they were82 before in the fifth verse.

All the duties of masters are included under this phrase, do the same things, which at first sight may seem somewhat83 strange. For some may say: the things which servants must do are these, to fear, to obey, to do service, and the like; and are masters to do the same things?

Answer. 1. These words are not to be referred to the particular duties which belong only to servants, but to the general rules of equity which are common to masters as well as servants; namely, that in their own places, with singleness of heart, as to Christ, not with eye-service, as men-pleasers, but as the servants of Christ, they do the will of God from the heart.

2. These words may be referred to the eighth verse, the verse immediately before, which lays down a general rule for all men in their own places to do the good things of their places. Now then, as servants must have an eye to their places, to do the good things of them, so masters must do the same things: that is, they must have an eye to their places, to do the good things of them.

3. These words may be taken without reference to any words before, and explained as a mutual, reciprocal and proportionate duty that ought to pass between master and servant: not in the particulars, as if the same duties were to be performed by each of them, for that would overthrow the order and degrees which God has set between master and servant, cross God’s ordinance, and imply a contradiction; but in general, that duties are to be performed by each to the other. In this respect the apostle said84 before of all sorts, superiors and inferiors, “Submit to one another.” And so by this phrase the doctrine mentioned before is confirmed, that masters are as much bound to duty as servants.

None of these answers contradicts another, but all of them may well be admitted, and all of them stand well together. They all imply a common equity between masters and servants, but no equality: mutual duties, but different and distinct duties, belonging to their separate places. Compare with this text what the apostle himself has noted more plainly and fully (Col 4:1), and we shall see him explain his own meaning: for what he implies here under this phrase (the same things) he expresses there under these two words, just, equal. Of these we shall85 speak more distinctly hereafter.

The apostle purposely wraps up masters’ duties in this general phrase (the same things) to forestall a secret objection raised from the eminence and superiority of masters over servants, which makes them think that servants exist only for the use of masters, and that masters are in no way bound to their servants. But if in general masters must do the same things, then they exist for their servants’ good, as well as servants for theirs.

§. 130. Of masters forbearing threatening.

The apostle in these words (forbearing threatening) does86 not absolutely forbid all manner of threatening, but only prescribes a moderation of it; and87 the King James translators have expressed this much well in the margin beside this text. Threatening is a duty which masters ought to use as occasion requires, and that to prevent blows. But men in authority are naturally prone to lord it over their inferiors, and to think that they cannot show their authority except by harshness. For this reason the apostle warns husbands against88 bitterness, and parents against89 provoking their children to wrath. Besides, the90 Gentiles and heathen thought that they had an absolute power over servants, even of life and death; and so the Roman emperors made laws to restrain that severity, for such masters would use their servants like beasts. Now, so that Christian masters might not be of the same mind, the apostle exhorts them to forbear threatening. From this note that

Authority must be moderated and kept within bounds: otherwise91 it will be like a swelling river without banks and walls.

Threatening is put here for every kind of severity, whether in heart, look, words or actions: for it is usual in Scripture to put one instance for all of the same kind.

Forbearing implies a restraint of every kind of excess, as

1. In time and continuance: when there is nothing but continual threatening on every small and slight occasion.

2. In measure: when threatening is too fierce and violent, so that it makes the heart swell, and, as it were, fire come out of the eyes and thunder out of the mouth, and the body shake in every part.

3. In carrying it out: when every vengeance once threatened is sure to be carried out, however sorry the party who caused the threatening may be for his fault, and though he humbles himself, and promises amendment, and gives good hope of it. Woe to us, the servants of the high God, if he dealt with us like that!

Here note that men may go to excess in doing a bounden duty, and92 so turn a needful virtue into a harmful vice. Great care therefore must be taken over the manner of doing good and lawful things.

Further, for the extent of this prohibition, we are to know that under the vice forbidden the contrary virtues are commanded, such as mildness, gentleness, patience, long-suffering and the like.

§. 131. Of masters’ subjection to a greater master.

The latter part of this verse contains a reason to enforce the directions in the former part. The reason, in sum, lays down the subjection in which masters are under God: a point of which none of them could be ignorant, and therefore he sets it down in this way, knowing: for93

All men know that there is one higher than the highest on earth.94 The light of nature reveals as much; no pagan, much less a Christian, can be ignorant of it. In telling masters, when he speaks to them, that they have a master, he gives them to understand that

Those who are in authority are also under authority: masters95 have a master. For God is “Lord of lords,” master of masters. In this respect Joseph, a great governor, says, “Am I not under God?”

These two little words (even your+, or your+ also) add some96 emphasis, referring to servants, as if he had said: your+ master as well as your+ servants’ master. Some Greek copies, for greater clarity, read it like this: (both your+ and their master).97 The sense is the same whichever way we read it. It shows that in relation to God,

Masters and servants are in the same subjection, and under a98 like command. There is one master, even Christ; and all men, whoever they are, are brethren, fellow servants.

§. 132. Of God’s being in heaven.

That great master, under whom all masters on earth are, is said here to be in heaven, to commend and set forth his dignity and authority all the more, and to make masters stand in greater awe of him. To a like purpose David, having set forth God99 sitting in the heavens, draws from it this exhortation to the great rulers on earth: “Therefore be wise, O kings; be admonished, O judges of the earth. Serve the Lord with fear, and rejoice with trembling.”

Objection. This placing of God in heaven makes those who do not fear God all the more insolent and complacent: for they will be ready to think and say, “What does God know?100 Does He judge through thick darkness? Thick clouds veil Him so He does not see us as He traverses the vault of heaven.”

Answer. 1. The apostle wrote to Christian masters, who thought better of God than such atheists did.

2. The placing of God in heaven does not confine him within its bounds: for101 heaven, and the heaven of heavens, cannot contain him.102 He fills heaven and earth.103 Though heaven is his throne, yet the earth also is his footstool. But because the Lord manifests his glory most in heaven and from heaven, therefore104 by way of preeminence he is said to be in heaven; and that in three special respects.

1. To show that there is no proportion between him and earthly masters, however great they are. For as the heaven is higher than the earth, so God is more excellent, indeed infinitely more excellent, than any man. “Who is like the Lord our God,105 the One enthroned on high?” There is no such difference between masters and servants on earth.

2. To show that he has his eyes continually on all his servants: he sees everything they do, as one placed above others sees all who are under him.106 “The Lord gazes out from heaven to earth.”107 “The Lord looks down from heaven; He sees all the sons of men.”108 “The eyes of the Lord are in every place, observing the evil and the good.” So this phrase shows the very opposite of what was objected before by wicked atheists.

3. To show that he is almighty: able both to reward his faithful servants (which is why David says, “I lift up109 my eyes to You, the One enthroned in heaven”) and also to execute vengeance on those who are unfaithful to God and cruel to their servants (which is why Solomon says, “If you see oppression,”110 etc., “do not be astonished; for He who is higher than the highest regards it”).

From this placing of God (in heaven) we learn these lessons.

1. The eye of faith is needed to behold God, for heaven111 is too high for any bodily eye to pierce. But by faith Moses “saw Him who is invisible.”

2. Though masters had none on earth above them, yet there is112 one higher than they. There is a master in heaven.

3. Those who cannot get a hearing on earth still have one to appeal113 to. There is a master in heaven.

4. The command under which earthly masters stand is far114 greater than the command they have: for their commander is in heaven.

§. 133. Of God’s having no respect of persons.

The apostle further adds of God, the great master of all, that with him there is no respect of persons. The Hebrew word used to set forth this point signifies115 a face; so also does the Greek word translated here116 person: it signifies both face and person. Now we know that the face of a man is outward, and is what, of all his parts, makes him most attractive in another’s eye. It is opposed to what is inward, namely the heart. In this respect it is said that “the Lord does not see as117 man does. For man sees the outward appearance, but the Lord sees the heart.” Here, by a synecdoche, face or person is put for every outward quality, state or condition which makes one preferred before another in man’s approval, such as beauty, comeliness, stature, wealth, honor, authority and the like. Now since God does not receive or respect persons, it shows that God does not prefer any one before another for any of the outward things named above, or any others like them. Elihu plainly explains this phrase in these words: “He does not accept the person of princes, nor favor118 the rich over the poor.” The phrase is taken from those who sit on thrones of judgment, whose eyes should be blinded so that they cannot see the face or person of those who are brought before them, but only hear the cause.

This is aptly noted of God here, to counter a vain notion of many masters, who, though they know that God is their master as well as their servants’ master, yet think that God will not call them to account so strictly, but will bear with and tolerate them, because they are of a higher rank and in a better condition than servants. But by this phrase the apostle shows that

To God all are alike: he makes no difference between any.119 He will show favor to the lowliest as well as to the greatest; he will take vengeance on the greatest as well as on the lowliest.

Many good lessons may be learned from this; among others, these:

1. The poorest and lowliest there are may have as free access to God as the wealthiest and greatest, and their petition will be received as soon.

2. The great ones on earth have as great cause to fear the avenging hand of God for any sin as the lowly.

3. It befits magistrates and all in authority to conduct themselves impartially toward all who are under them: for they are in God’s place. Respect of persons is the cause of all the injustice and wrong which magistrates do.

4. It befits ministers to be faithful in all God’s house, and with an even hand to sow the seed of God’s word, and to keep themselves pure from the blood of all men: for they are God’s stewards and ambassadors, and therefore they must have no respect of persons.

A Servant’s Reverence for His Master

The Seventh Treatise: Of Servants · §§. 1–9

§. 1. An analysis of the apostle’s direction to servants.

Eph 6.

5. Servants, be obedient to those who are your+ masters according to the flesh, with fear and trembling, in singleness of your+ heart, as to Christ; 6. not with eye-service, as men-pleasers, but as the servants of Christ, doing the will of God from the heart; 7. with good will doing service, as to the Lord and not to men, 8. knowing that whatever good thing any man does, the same he will receive from the Lord, whether he is slave or free.

The third and last couple in a family are—

  • Masters.
  • Servants.

The direction that the apostle gives to servants is set down in this text.

In it he

  1. Sets out their duties.
  2. Adds motives to perform them.

I. In setting out servants’ duties he notes the kinds, the manner, and the extent of them.

1. The kinds of servants’ duties are noted in two words—

  • Obey, v. 5.
  • Serve, v. 7.

2. The manner is set down

  • Affirmatively, vv. 5, 7.
  • Negatively, vv. 6, 7.

1. Affirmatively, by showing what graces are needful; for this purpose he lists four particulars.

  1. Fear and trembling.
  2. Singleness of heart.
  3. Conscience to Christ.
  4. Good will. Under it are included—
    1. Cheerfulness.
    2. Readiness.
    3. Diligence.
    4. Faithfulness.

2. Negatively, by showing what vices are to be avoided; and these are chiefly two:

1. Eye-service, which is opposed to doing things—

  • As Christ’s servants.
  • From the heart.

2. Men-pleasing, which is opposed to doing the will of God.

3. The extent of servants’ duties is noted in these four phrases—

  1. Masters according to the flesh, v. 5.
  2. As to Christ, v. 5.
  3. As servants of Christ, v. 6.
  4. Doing God’s will, v. 6.

II. The motives the apostle uses are partly—

  • Implied.
  • Expressed.

They are implied in three ways.

  1. By declaring the place of a master (as to Christ.)
  2. By pointing out the honor of their service (as servants of Christ.)
  3. By showing the ground of servants’ subjection (God’s will.)

The motive expressed is the recompense that servants will have for their pains, fully set out in v. 8.

In it these distinct points are noted in order.

  1. The assurance of it (knowing.)
  2. The ground of it, taken from a general rule (whatever good thing any man does.)
  3. The particular application of it (whether slave or free.)
  4. The kind of it (the same he will receive.)
  5. The author and giver of it (from the Lord.)

§. 2. Of the lawfulness of a master’s place and power.

In handling servants’ duties I will proceed in the order set out above. In the first place, therefore, I will point out the kinds of servants’ duties.

To find out the kinds, I will follow the method used in handling children’s duties. Note, then:

  1. The fountain of servants’ duties.
  2. The streams that issue from it.

The fountain lies partly in the opinion and partly in the feelings of servants.

In their opinion they must be informed and fully persuaded that the120 place of a master and of a servant is lawful and warranted: that God in general ordained degrees of superiority and inferiority, of authority and subjection; and in particular gave masters the authority they have, and put servants in the subjection in which they are. Until the judgment is settled on this, neither reverence nor obedience will be given as it ought to be. For reverence has reference to eminence and superiority, and obedience to authority and power. Who will reverence or obey a man whom he takes to be his equal? This was the ground of the conspiracy of Korah, Dathan and Abiram: they thought Moses and Aaron took too much upon themselves, and lifted themselves up above the congregation of the Lord.121 Therefore I will here note the grounds of a master’s authority and a servant’s subjection.122

1. God has given an express commandment to masters to govern their servants, and to servants to be subject to their masters.123 In the fourth commandment God gives masters a charge over their servants, to see that they do no manner of work. And the angel bids Hagar humble herself under her mistress’s hands.124 And here servants are commanded to obey their masters.

2. Many directions are given both to masters and to servants, according to their different places, on how to conduct themselves toward one another. Read for this purpose the many laws that Moses prescribed to both, the many counsels that Solomon also gives to both, in his Proverbs especially, and in particular the directions of this and other apostles.

3. Saints in all ages have been set in these places, some in the places of masters and some in the places of servants; and according to the place in which God set them, they have performed their duty: masters the duties of masters, and servants the duties of servants.

4. The many parables Christ uses, taken from the power that masters have and exercise over their servants and the subjection that servants give to their masters, show that the authority of the one and the subjection of the other are things granted without question and not denied.

5. God has made many promises of reward both to masters and to servants who conscientiously perform the duties of their place, and has made many threats against the one and the other when they are negligent in them.

All these grounds are so clearly and plentifully set down in Scripture that anyone who is at all acquainted with it may know them to be so. Were there no other argument than this text which I have in hand, it would be enough to confound all who speak against them, and to move those who believe all the more125 to serve their masters according to the flesh, for the sake of their faith and their profession of it.

§. 3. Of the Anabaptists’ arguments against the authority of masters, and subjection of servants.

Contrary to this first ground of servants’ subjection is the opinion of the Anabaptists, who teach that all are alike, and that there is no difference between masters and servants. Their reasons, by which they would make a show of proving their unreasonable opinion, are these.

1. Objection. Masters are either unbelievers or Christians, and so are servants, one or the other. If masters are unbelievers and servants Christians, how unfitting it is that Christians should be subject to unbelievers! And if master and servant are both Christians, they are brothers; but brothers are equals, and neither is subject to the other.

Answer. Rule and subjection are matters of outward civil order: they tend to the outward preservation of church, commonwealth and family in this world. But faith, piety and such graces are inward matters of the soul, tending to a better life.

Since these are so different, one who is more excellent in the one may be inferior in the other. Indeed, though there is an equality in the one, namely in spiritual things, yet there may be a disparity in the other, namely in social and temporal matters. And though saints may be far inferior to unbelievers in outward condition, yet they are not a bit less glorious before God. The honor that belongs to saints, and is distinctively theirs, is inward, not visible to the carnal eye of a natural man.

2. Objection. It is against nature for one man to be a servant, especially a slave, to another.126

Answer. Even granting that it is against that absolute and perfect nature in which God at first created man, and that it came in by sin, yet it is not against that order and course of nature in which God has now settled man. God has turned many punishments of sin into binding duties, such as the subjection of the wife to the husband, and man’s eating bread by the sweat of his brow.

3. Objection. It is the prerogative of Christians to be all one;127 but the subjection of servants to masters is against that prerogative.

Answer. That prerogative is purely spiritual. For in Christ all are one as they are members of Christ, which is a spiritual body, not as they are members of a political body. A political inequality is not against a spiritual equality.

4. Objection. This subjection is against the128 liberty that Christ has purchased for us, with which he has made us free.

Answer. It is not. For that liberty is from the curse and rigor of the moral law; from the ceremonial law and its rites; from Satan, sin, death and damnation. But it is not from those degrees which God has established between man and man for the good of mankind.

5. Objection. We are expressly forbidden to be “servants of men.”129

Answer. To be a servant in that place is not merely to be in subjection130 under another and to do service for him, but to be so obsequious to a man, so bent on pleasing him, and so subject to his will, as to do whatever he wants done: to regard nothing but his pleasure, and to prefer it before God’s word and will. It is not, therefore, the thing itself, but an excess in it, that is forbidden there.

§. 4. Of a servant’s fear of his master.

The other part of that fountain from which the duties131 of servants flow lies in the feelings; and it is, in one word, fear, which is an awe-filled dread of a master: an awe because of his master’s place, a dread because of his master’s power. Awe is such a reverent esteem of his master as makes him count his master “worthy of all honor,” which Saint Paul132 expressly enjoins servants to do. Dread is such a fear of provoking his master’s wrath as makes him think over and consider every way to please him. This is what the apostle implies here under these two words, fear and trembling. In both these respects Saint Peter commands servants to be subject “with all fear.”133

This fear is so much a servant’s own, in relation to his master, that where it is lacking there is a plain denial of his master’s place and power. God implies this in this reproachful question: “If I am a master, where is your+ fear of Me?”134 That is, you+ plainly show that you+ do not count me your+ master, because in your+ heart there is no fear of me.

This fear will draw servants on to perform every duty cheerfully. The more it abounds, the more desire and striving there will be to please and to give full satisfaction (and this is a point commanded to servants, “to please them well in all things”).135 Indeed, it will gladden a servant’s heart to see his service prosper. We have a worthy pattern of this in Abraham’s servant,136 whose care to do his business as his master would have it, and his prayer for God’s help in it, and his thanks for God’s blessing on it, show the awe-filled respect he bore his master.

Again, on the other side, this fear will keep men from offending their masters (which was one reason that moved Joseph not to yield to his mistress)137 and in this respect it may prevent many harms that their masters’ offense and wrath might bring on them.

One special means to breed and preserve this fear in servants is a due consideration of the ground of their masters’138 place and power, which is God’s appointment. God has placed them in his stead, and in part given them his power: they are God’s deputies and ministers, and therefore in Scripture the title139 (Lord) is given to them in a distinctive way. What makes subjects stand in awe of lower magistrates? Is it not because they represent the king’s person, and have authority and power given to them by the king?

§. 5. Of the extremes contrary to servants’ fear of their masters.

Two extremes are contrary to this servant-like fear.

1. In the excess, a slavish fear: when they fear nothing but140 the avenging power of their master, the staff or the cudgel, as we say; so long as they may avoid that, they do not care whether their master is pleased or not. This often makes them wish that their masters had no power over them, or that they were dead. This was the fear that possessed the heart of that unprofitable servant who said to his master, “I knew that141 you are a hard man … so I was afraid.” Such servile servants will never be profitable.

2. In the defect, a slight esteem and plain contempt of142 masters. That this is contrary to fear is evident from the opposition God makes between them in these words: “If I am a master, where is your+ fear of Me, O you+ who despise My name?” As if he had said: by this you+ show that you+ do not fear me as a master, because you+ despise me. This sin of despising masters is expressly143 forbidden, and for it Hagar was harshly dealt with.144 When masters are poor, lowly, weak, aged or otherwise feeble, proud servants are prone to despise them. This shows a base mind, and proves that they respect their master’s power more than his place. The poorest and weakest have the same place and authority over servants that the richest and strongest have; all bear God’s image alike. But disdainful, proud servants show that they do not regard God’s image at all.

§. 6. Of servants’ reverence in speech.

The two main streams that issue from the fountain named above are—

  • Reverence.
  • Obedience.

Reverence is shown in

  • Speech.
  • Bearing.

In speech, by refraining from speech and by ordering it well.

Servants’ reverence in refraining from speech is shown in three ways.

1. By holding back from speaking without just cause in their master’s presence or hearing. This phrase145 (“who stand continually before you and hear your wisdom”), spoken of Solomon’s servants, shows that they were slow to speak and quick to listen in their master’s presence.

2. By refraining from replying when they observe that their master is unwilling for them to speak any more. Peter refrained in this way when his master gave him this short answer: “What is that to you?”146

3. By attending to what their masters say to them. For servants ought to show such respect to their masters when they speak to them as Samuel did to God when he said, “Speak, for Your servant is listening.”147 The titles of Lord and servant show that this speech is taken from the duty of servants. The derivation of the148 Greek word the apostle uses, translated obey, implies as much. Abraham’s149 servant showed this reverence to his master when he gave him a charge about choosing a wife for his son.

For ordering their speech well to their masters, servants must observe five cautions.

1. That they have just occasion to speak: that is, either when their masters require them to speak (as150 the disciples did), or when they see that it is needful for their masters that they should speak. In such cases speech shows reverence, as well as silence does in other cases. The general points delivered before about the reverence of151 wives to their husbands, and of152 children to their parents, may be applied to servants, and pressed on them by an argument from the less to the greater, like this: if wives (who in many things share a joint authority with their husbands) and children (who are not subject to their parents in so servile a degree as servants are to their masters) must show their inward fear of their husbands and parents by outward reverence, much more must servants show it to their masters. To show the force of this inference all the more, let it be noted that the apostle adds here another kind153 of word than he did before in either wives’ or children’s duties, namely154 trembling.

Question. In what cases may it be needful for masters that their servants speak to them?

Answer. 1. When servants know anything that may be profitable for their masters, they ought to make it known, though they are not asked; as155 that maid did who told her mistress, Naaman’s wife, that there was a prophet in Samaria who could cure him of his leprosy.

2. When masters, through ignorance, anger or some such cause, refuse to do what their servants know to be good for them, the servants ought to persuade their masters to do it; as Naaman’s servants persuaded their master to wash himself in156 the Jordan, as the prophet advised him.

3. When servants are not fully instructed in the things they ought to do for their master, they ought to ask him what is to be done, as the disciples did, who said to their master, “Where do You want us to prepare for You to eat the157 Passover?”

4. When some scruple arises in their minds about any charge their master gives them, they may inquire of their master, as Abraham’s servant did when he said, “What if the woman158 is unwilling to follow me …?”

5. When their master unjustly suspects them of any evil, they may speak to clear their own innocence, as David159 did to Saul.

A second caution for servants’ ordering their speech well is160 that the very form and manner of their speech, when they have just occasion to speak to their masters, should savor of reverence. For this purpose let these particulars be noted.

1. That servants give respectful titles to their masters.

It was out of honorable respect for their master that Naaman’s servants called him father.161 In Scripture162 one of God’s titles is given to masters, namely Lord.

2. That they use few words, no more than are needed, even when they have occasion to speak; as may be gathered from the examples cited above to show when servants might and ought to speak.

3. That all the words they speak to their master should be meek, mild and humble. Note for this purpose how the sons of the prophets framed their speech to their master.163

1. They humbly ask leave for themselves to go to the Jordan.

2. They entreat him to go, in these words: “Please come with your servants.”

3. When one of them had lost the head of his axe, as if he did not dare be so bold as to speak to his master to get it back for him, he refers the matter to his good pleasure, saying, “Oh, my master, it was borrowed!”

A third caution is that they observe a fit time to speak to their master: as when he is at leisure to hear them (thus it is noted of the disciples that when their master was alone they put their questions to him)164 or when his mind is quiet, not troubled with anger (thus when David observed that Saul’s mind was somewhat calmed toward him, by the words he used, “Is that your voice, David my son?” he then took occasion to speak to him more freely).165

A fourth caution is that they give a ready and prompt answer to their master. This is often commended in Peter: when Christ put any question to his disciples, he would answer at once and166 readily.

A fifth caution is that all their speeches and answers to their master should be true, not daring to tell them a lie. This shows a very reverent respect for a master. When Eli asked Samuel what the Lord had said to him, “Samuel told him everything and did not hide a thing from him.”167 And when David bade the woman of Tekoa not to hide from him the thing he would ask her, she told him the whole truth. There is a double bond to tie servants to this:

  1. Their conscience toward God, who is a God of truth.
  2. Their respect for their masters, with whom they ought to deal faithfully.

Lastly, a servant’s reverence ought to be shown by his speech about his master even behind his back, speaking good of him and no evil. Abraham’s servant also showed this part of reverence to his master.168 In this way they will show themselves good, true-hearted, faithful servants, and not flatterers.

§. 7. Of the vices contrary to a servant’s reverence in speech.

The offenses contrary to the reverence of servants to their masters, named above, are these.

1. Sauciness and boldness in servants, when they have no more respect for their master’s presence than for anyone else’s, but are full of chatter and loud in speech before their master, or in the room next to him, where they may be heard as well as if they were in the same room. Masters are often much offended in this way.

2. Insistence in speaking, and replying again and again, though their masters not only show their dislike of it but also expressly charge them to speak no more. Scolding maids who will have the last word over their mistress offend much in this. This goes directly against the apostle’s prohibition to servants, “not answering back.”169

3. Impatience, when they cannot bear to hear their master finish what he is saying, but either interrupt him or flounce off.

4. Defiance, when (as Solomon notes) “though they understand,170 they will not speak.” Though they know very well that it is their master’s pleasure that they should speak, indeed though they are bidden to speak, yet their stubborn pride and sullen heart will not allow them to speak, no, not even though it is most needful for their masters. If they know that their master is going on in a course very harmful to him, yet they will not tell him of it. Indeed, if their master unjustly suspects them of any evil, they will let him remain in that suspicion rather than speak to clear themselves. And if their master does not expressly appoint them from time to time what to do, they will never ask; nor, if they are in doubt about what he gives them in charge, will they inquire further to be satisfied about it. This kind of silence, in these and similar cases, shows more doggedness than dutifulness.

5. Disdain, when they171 scorn to give the title master to him who is set over them, because he is a poor and lowly man.

6. Arrogance, when their words are high and lofty against their master, claiming that they are as good as he, though for a time they are under him. Clerks, apprentices, waiting women and the like, born of gentlemen and men of good rank, are for the most part guilty of this fault. The reason is that their birth and parentage make them forget their present place and condition; or else (which is worse) make them willfully set themselves above it.

7. Muttering and grumbling on every occasion of discontent, by which it comes about that they often provoke much wrath (for “a harsh word stirs up anger”).172

8. Ill-timed interruption of their master: speaking to him when he is seriously occupied in some weighty business (as those did who told Christ of his mother and brothers173 when he was preaching), or speaking to him at an unfit time while he is angry, by which they often bring much harm on themselves.

9. Answering their master at their own leisure, letting him call, and call again and again. Job complains of this, saying, “I call for my servant, but he does not answer.”174

10. Slapping their master in the mouth with a lie, like Gehazi, who, when he had wickedly got money and clothing from Naaman, and his master asked him where he had been, said with a straight face, “Your servant did not go anywhere.”175 Let the judgment carried out on him make all servants beware of the like sin. For lying is in itself a heinous sin, yet all the more heinous when it is told to one who has authority over us and, because of that authority, stands in God’s place.

Lastly, evil talk about their master behind his back. This is a sin even though what is spoken to a master’s disgrace is true; for a master’s infirmities ought rather to be covered than revealed and laid open by a servant. How monstrous a sin it is, then, to raise slanderous reports against a master that are untrue! This was Ziba’s sin against Mephibosheth his master.176

So much for servants’ reverence in speech to their masters.

Their reverence in bearing follows.

§. 8. Of servants’ reverent behavior to their masters.

To show a servant’s reverence in bearing toward his master, three things are especially required.

  1. Dutiful gestures of respect.
  2. Respectful behavior.
  3. Modest clothing.

I. Such dutiful and submissive gestures of respect and courtesy as befit their sex and place, and that according to the most usual177 custom of the country and place where they are, servants must perform to their masters, as they have occasion to go to them, to come from them, to receive any charge from them, or to bring any message to them. Where Isaac says, in the blessing he gave Jacob, “May you be the master of your brothers, and may the sons of your mother bow178 down to you,” by that phrase, bowing down, he notes the condition of a servant, and with it implies a servant’s duty. On this ground, when the sons of the prophets saw that the spirit of Elijah rested on Elisha, taking it for a clear sign that God had made him a governor and master over them, “they went to179 meet him and bowed down to the ground before him.”

II. Corresponding to a servant’s gestures of respect must be his whole behavior180 before his master, seasoned and ordered with such modesty and humility as may show an honorable respect for his master; as:

1. To stand in his master’s presence, which testifies a readiness to perform any service his master appoints him to do. This was one thing noted and commended by the queen of Sheba in Solomon’s servants: she saw their standing,181 and said, “How blessed are these servants of yours who stand … before you!” Where it is said that she also saw their sitting, that phrase shows a seemly order they kept even when they were out of his presence, by giving and taking their right and due place.

2. By uncovering their heads in their master’s presence: in our days, and in the parts of the world where we live, this is for males a sign and token of subjection.

3. By sobriety and modesty both in expression and in the whole bearing of the body, especially when servants are in their master’s presence. For to compose the expression and the whole body soberly because of someone’s presence shows a reverent respect for that person, for whose sake that sobriety is shown.

III. The clothing servants wear must also be so fashioned182 and ordered as to declare them to be servants and under their masters; and so it will show a reverent respect for their masters. One purpose of clothing is to show a difference between superiors and inferiors, persons in authority and those under subjection. It pleased the Holy Spirit to note this particular (and their183 attire) as a point worth observing in Solomon’s servants.

§. 9. Of the faults of servants contrary to reverence in bearing.

The bearing of many servants toward their master is wholly contrary to the reverence named above:

1. Some, through rudeness and lack of good upbringing,184 come to their masters and go from them as to and from their fellows: no sign of reverence by any gesture of respect; they do not know how to give it. By this they dishonor their parents and the way they were brought up.

2. Others, if on first coming to their master they give some185 greeting, yet, through too much familiarity with their masters, all the rest of the day they will behave as if they were his fellows, scarcely uncovering their heads in their master’s presence, not bearing to stand long before him, but either sitting down or slinking away when they should be in attendance. No doubt this proverb arose from this excessive familiarity: “Good morning for all day.” This unmannerly familiarity is commonly found in servants who have poor and lowly masters. For the servants of rich and great men can be so full of courtesy that not a word will be spoken by their masters to them, or by them to their masters, without the knee being bowed with it. They can stand hour after hour before their masters and not once put on their hats. If they are walking behind their masters, their master will not turn round sooner than their hats will be off, and that as often as he turns or speaks to them. Why should rich masters have so much reverence shown to them, and poor masters none at all? Do not the poor bear God’s image as well as the rich? Does God’s word make any difference between rich and poor? Does it say, “Serve rich masters with fear and trembling”? Surely it is the corruption of man’s heart that makes this difference. Servants by nature regard their master’s outward ability more than his inward authority, and the honor that the world confers on men more than the honor that God confers. For God has given as much honor and authority to a poor master as to a rich one, because he is a master.

Servants commonly fail most in this duty of reverence toward their masters in the very place where they should most of all show it, namely in the courts of God and the assembly of God’s people, where the very angels are present to behold our seemly conduct. In this way they cause the ministry of the word to be spoken evil of, and so make their sin all the more heinous.

3. Exceedingly great is the fault of servants in their excess in clothing.186 Ordinarily there is no distinction between a man’s children and his servants; indeed, none between masters and their men, mistresses and their maids. While men and maids are provided for at their masters’ and mistresses’ expense, a difference may perhaps be made; though even then, if they can get the means in any way, they will do what they can to be as finely dressed as they can. But once they provide for themselves, they will lay out all they have on clothing rather than not be as fine as master or mistress: if not so costly, yet in show as showy and fine. New fashions are taken up as soon by servants as by masters and mistresses. What is the end of this, but to be thought as good as master or mistress? If the queen of Sheba were living now, she would wonder as much at the disorder of servants in these days as she then wondered at the seemly order of Solomon’s servants. Let these proud servants look to it: for if God has threatened to “punish … the king’s sons … who are dressed in strange apparel,”187 can servants who are so dressed expect to go scot-free?

So much for servants’ reverence. Their obedience follows.

A Servant’s Obedience to His Master

The Seventh Treatise: Of Servants · §§. 10–19

§. 10. Of servants’ obedience.

No inferiors are more bound to obedience than servants: it is their main and most distinctive function to obey their masters. It is therefore expressly mentioned here in my text (Servants, be obedient to those who are your+ masters), and all other duties are included under it.188 The reasons given to move wives and children to obey ought much more to move servants.189

Those who are of a contrary mind, who are rebellious and disdain to be under the authority of another, and are ready to say of their master, “We do not want this man to rule over us,”190 are fitter to live among Anabaptists than among orthodox Christians. For to what end is the lawfulness of authority acknowledged, if subjection is not yielded to it? Of the two, a man would do better to be blinded by error than not to obey the truth which he knows.

In handling this point of obedience, I will follow the same order as I did in setting out the obedience of wives and children, and consider:

  1. The parts of servants’ obedience, in which it consists.
  2. Its extent, how far it reaches.

The parts are two: one negative, the other affirmative.

The negative is to refrain from doing things on their own initiative, without or against their masters’ consent.

The affirmative is readily to yield to doing what their masters would have them do.

§. 11. Of servants’ refraining from doing things without their masters’ consent.

Servants ought to refrain from doing things on their own initiative without or against the consent of their masters, because while the time of their service lasts they are not their own, and the things they do ought not to be for themselves: both their persons and their actions are all their masters’, and the will of their master must be their rule and guide (in things which are not against God’s will). The rite used under the law of piercing a servant’s ear191 implied as much. David therefore, alluding to that rite, says to God (both of himself and also of Christ, whom he prefigured as a type), “my ears You have pierced,”192 and from this he infers that he would do the will of God. We have a notable pattern of this in Abraham’s servant,193 who, in a business committed to his charge, put forward such doubts as came into his head, to receive his master’s direction about them, so that he would not be forced to do something on his own initiative without particular warrant from his master.

This general point will be made clearer if it is illustrated by some particulars. Take therefore these instances, gathered out of the Scriptures, of things which servants must not do without their masters’ consent.

1. Servants must not go wherever they wish. The phrase the centurion uses (“I tell one to go, and he goes”)194 implies that unless his master tells him to go, he ought not to go.

2. They ought not to do their own business and affairs. It is noted of Jacob, when he was Laban’s servant, that though he195 had flocks of his own, yet he fed his master’s flocks and committed his own to his sons.

3. They ought not to do whatever business they please. Ahimaaz had a great desire to carry the news of Absalom’s196 death to David; yet without the leave of Joab, his captain and master, he would not do it. The good mistress provides “portions for her maidservants,” namely, the portion of work:197 so they must do what she gives them to do. The servants of the centurion each did as their master198 charged them; and the servants who had talents committed to them each looked to his own talent.

4. They ought not to marry while the time of their contract of service lasts, unless their master gives consent to it. The law of God sets out the lawful marriages of servants in this way: “If his master gives him a wife.”199 By this it is implied that if a servant marries, it must be with his master’s consent.

Objection. The apostle says, without excepting servants, “to avoid fornication, each man should have his own wife,” etc.200

Answer. He shows there what means the Lord has sanctified for everyone to avoid fornication; but he does not by this give everyone liberty to use that means rashly, against the order which God has set down.

2. That precept is given to those who are201 in their own power. For of children he says that202 parents must see what is fitting or not fitting for them.

3. What servants are to gather from it is this: if need so requires, to make their desire known to their master, and to use all the good means they can, by themselves or others, to obtain their master’s consent.

5. They ought not to dispose of their master’s goods at their own pleasure, not even for charitable uses. The steward who203 wasted his master’s goods was justly put out of office for it. Indeed, he is called unjust for disposing of some of them for his own204 future livelihood; therefore it was an act of injustice, and unlawful.

Objection. The lord commended him for it.

Answer. He commended his wisdom, not his injustice: his prudent care for the time to come, not the means of providing for himself. For his foresight in general he is commended; for the particular means, deceiving his master, he is called unjust.

6. They must not go away from their master before their contracted time has expired. When Jacob,205 after long service, had a mind to be gone, he asked leave; and because his uncle and master would not willingly let him go, he stayed on.

Objection. Afterward Jacob206 secretly stole away from his master.

Answer. 1. His contracted time was out. 2. He had an express warrant from God to go. 3. His manner of going away is not to be justified, and in that respect it is no good pattern.

§. 12. Of the unlawful liberty which servants take for themselves.

Contrary to the limits on servants’ liberty named above are these and similar wicked and licentious tricks, as follow.

1. When servants watch for their chance to go wherever they please without their master knowing it: as when their masters are busy with serious matters, or out of the house, or in bed. In this way that wicked servant Gehazi watched for his chance to run after Naaman, thinking that207 his master would not know it.

2. When, being bound to their master’s service, they do their own business and seek their own profit, and that without their master’s leave. What made Gehazi’s secret stealing away and withdrawing from his master worse was that he went to receive gifts for himself. There are many such wicked servants who, knowing certain friends of their master who will be ready to do them any kindness, will use their master’s name to borrow money or get some other favor, and never let their masters know of it.

3. When servants will choose their own work and do what pleases them best, or else do nothing at all. So where many servants are in one house together, if they are not in such positions as they like, they will mutter and grumble and do nothing well.

4. When servants, especially maidservants, who are bound, deliberately marry to free themselves; because our laws free a maid who is married from her service to master and mistress.

5. When they are free with their master’s goods, giving them away. Some think that because they belong to the house, they may give away the things of the house to charitable uses. But the pretext of charity is no excuse for injustice. Servants may let their masters or mistresses know that there are such and such things in the house fit to be given away, or that there are such and such poor people in great need; but secretly, without any consent at all, they must not give away anything of their master’s.

6. When, out of discontent, they run away from their masters. The two servants of Shimei who ran from their master208 are blamed for it by the Holy Spirit. Onesimus, who ran from his master, is sent back by Saint Paul; and Hagar is sent back by an angel.

Objection. What if master and mistress are harsh, severe and cruel?

Answer. An angel from heaven gives one answer (“Submit209 to her authority”), and an apostle gives another: “Submit yourselves+ with all fear … to the perverse. For conscience toward210 God, endure grief, suffering wrongfully.”

These faults are noted in this way so that servants, taking notice of them, may avoid them all the more carefully.

§. 13. Of servants’ obedience to their masters’ commandments.

The affirmative and active part of servants’ obedience, consisting in a ready yielding to what their masters would have done, has respect (like the same kind of obedience in children) to the commandment, instruction, reproof, and correction of their master.

I. Since a master has power to command his servants, it is211 the duty of servants to obey their master in this. The particular work which belongs to a servant by virtue of his place is to keep an eye on his master, to see what he requires of him. David describes the character of good servants in this way: “the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master.”212 That looking, as it implies an expectation of relief and help, so it also implies a readiness to receive and carry out anything their masters would have done. The centurion commends this duty213 in the example of his servants, each of whom did what their master commanded them to do. It is further commended in the examples of Abraham’s214 servant, Elijah’s215 servant, and many others. Note how far the Lord Christ demands this duty of servants:216 though a servant has been plowing all day (a laborious and wearisome work), yet when he comes home and his master commands him to prepare his supper and wait on him, he must do it. By all these proofs it is clear that if a master bids his servant come, go, do this or that, he must obey.

The contrary of this is the highest degree of disobedience,217 as when servants refuse to be at their masters’ command and to do what they charge them to do: like Ziba, who, being commanded to saddle his master’s donkey, went away and did not do it; and Job’s servants, who would not answer when they were called.218 Of all offenses this most provokes masters, for it is a plain contempt of their authority.

§. 14. Of servants’ heeding their masters’ instructions in matters of their calling.

II. As a master has power to command, so it is his duty to219 instruct his servants in the way of righteousness, and in the secular course of life in which he is to walk. The duty of servants, then, is to listen to them and obey them in both.

1. Servants who are under masters to learn their trade are bound in many ways to listen to them:

1. So that during their term they may do better service to their masters.

2. So that by learning a trade or skill in some good calling they may be better able, when the time of their service is out, to support themselves, to teach other servants who will be under them, and to do more good in the place where they will live.

3. So that in this way they may better keep a good conscience in the particular place in which God has set them.

Contrary to this is the idle, sluggish, dull disposition of many servants who, through their carelessness and unruliness in doing what they are taught, vex and grieve their masters, and indeed make them weary of instructing them. Many apprentices spend all the time of their apprenticeship without reaping any good at all: they never turn out masters of their craft. So many in the country live under good farmers, many clerks under good lawyers, many maids under good homemakers, and that for many years on end, yet through their negligence get no good at all; whereas if they had been attentive and careful, they might have learned much. These are enemies to their masters, to themselves, to the city and country where they live, and to their relatives and parents, especially if they have any living.

§. 15. Of servants’ heeding their masters’ instructions in piety.

Servants who have religious masters, who take care to220 instruct their household in the way of righteousness, ought to be obedient to their instructions. So were Joshua’s servants (or else Joshua could not have answered for them221 as he did); and the servants of that official of whom it was said that “he believed,222 and all his house”; and the servants of Lydia and of the jailer, of whom it is said, “she and her household were baptized,”223 and “he and all his house … had come to believe in God.”224 No one doubts that servants are included under these words house and household.

Now then, if the servants of these had not listened and yielded to the instructions of their master and mistress, would they have believed in him in whom their masters believed, or been baptized when their mistress was? The same may be gathered from this phrase, “the church in their house”;225 for by this it is implied that all in the house were of the same faith as their master and mistress. It is expressly noted of the226 soldier who waited continually on Cornelius that he was devout, by which it is presupposed that he listened to his master’s instructions; for it is noted of the master also that he was “devout and God-fearing.”227

The benefit that comes to servants by obeying such instructions is beyond words. Much more good may be gained by following the spiritual directions of religious masters than the clever and profitable directions of the wisest statesmen and the most skillful craftsmen or tradesmen there are. So there is a double bond to bind servants to obey this kind of instruction:

  1. The bond of duty.
  2. The bond of profit and benefit to themselves.

§. 16. Of servants’ faults contrary to obedience in matters of religion.

Though the duty named above is so needful to servants, yet of all duties it is by many least thought to be a duty, and most neglected, the very opposite being for the most part practiced, as may appear by these particulars.

1. Many servants are of so ungodly a mind that of all masters228 they will not serve those who are religious and make it a matter of conscience to instruct their servants in the way to salvation: they would rather serve profane masters, out-and-out worldlings, and even papists. Why? Anything agrees better with corrupt nature than to be kept within the bounds of religion. Few like the narrow gate and narrow way that leads to life; for the god of this world so blinds men’s eyes that they cannot see the blessing of life at the end of this way: they see only how narrow it is. Or if they should see it far off, yet they are so set on the things of the flesh and of this present evil world that they would not regard what is so far off. Now religious masters will do what they can to keep all their servants in that narrow way; but irreligious masters will allow them to follow the sway of their own corruption: to swear, to profane the Sabbath and spend that whole day in sleeping, play, eating and drinking; to go to bed without prayer, or catechizing, or reading the word of God, and so to rise again and go about their business. To these holy duties such devout masters as Cornelius was will hold their servants.

2. Others, who have been placed by their relatives under such devout229 masters, or who, not knowing the disposition of their masters beforehand, have themselves agreed with them to be their servants, after they have been in the house a while think their master’s house a prison to them, muttering and grumbling against being kept in so strictly, as they regard it. And when they are called in to any religious exercise, they will be ready to say, “I was not hired for this, and I am not bound to you in this. Set me to your work and I will do it.”

3. Some again are the more negligent and dissolute in their230 business because their masters are religious. It is a common complaint that profane, worldly masters get more service than religious and godly masters, even though the godly give better wages, food and lodging than the worldly. Many think the fault for this lies in the master; but assuredly, for the most part, the fault is the corrupt disposition of servants, who value pleasure in sin, and freedom for it, more than wages, food and lodging. By this they show themselves ungodly toward God, ungrateful to their masters and harmful to themselves. Let this perverse disposition be noted, so that the apostle’s rule, very pertinent to this point, may be better observed: “Those231 who have believing masters should not show them disrespect, but should serve them all the more, since they are beloved believers.”

§. 17. Of servants’ obedience to reproof and correction.

The same directions may serve for yielding to reproof and correction, for both may be brought under one head, and in general they agree. For reproof is a correction in words, and correction is a reproof in deed.

The obedience which servants must show their masters with respect to these is twofold:

1. To bear patiently every kind of reproof and correction.

2. To amend readily what they are justly reproved or corrected for.

For the first of these, servants have an express precept, enforced232 by many reasons, in these words: “Servants, submit yourselves+ to your+ masters with all fear, not only to those who are good and gentle, but also to the perverse. For this is thankworthy, if anyone, for conscience toward God, endures grief, suffering wrongfully. How is it to your+ credit if you+ are buffeted for your+ faults and you+ take it patiently? But if you+ do well and suffer for it, and you+ take it patiently, this is acceptable before God.” Here we see:

1. That correction is to be borne patiently by servants: if correction, then much more reproof.

2. That though correction is unjustly inflicted, yet it is to be endured patiently; therefore much more when it is deserved.

3. That buffeting, and that from a perverse master, is to be borne; therefore much more the lighter correction of a good and gentle master. Joseph patiently endured fetters,233 and iron chains, and imprisonment234 inflicted on him most unjustly, even for his faithfulness to his master. Great was the recompense God made him; and assuredly God will recompense all the wrong, pain and grief that is endured for conscience’ sake in this case. For the apostle says that this is thankworthy, that is, worthy of praise and commendation, and so worthy of recompense and reward. Again he says that it is acceptable to God, so that God will recompense it, though men may count it nothing but servility and dullness; indeed, though unjust masters may take occasion from it to trample all the more on their servants and lord it over them.

To these motives I may add the rule of the Christian faith we profess (which the apostle also hints at in these words,235 “For to this you+ were called”). For our Christian calling requires all men to “give place to wrath”;236237 to turn the other cheek when one is struck;238 to “overcome evil with good.”239 If all Christians must show such patience to all men, even their equals, how much more must servants show it to masters? The apostle commends to servants the example of Christ in this case: he was reviled, and much he suffered, and that most unjustly; and yet he bore all patiently. If servants suffer with him in this way, they also will reign with him.

§. 18. Of the extremes contrary to servants’ patient bearing of reproof and correction.

Patient bearing of rebuke and blows seems a hard saying to most servants: they cannot bear to hear it, for their mind and conduct are quite contrary to it.

1. Some, when only reproved, though justly, are ready to answer back and to trade word for word: a sin240 expressly forbidden. It seems, from the apostle’s express mention of it, that answering back has long been an evil trait in servants.

Objection. If a master unjustly reproves his servant and the servant does not answer back, he knowingly allows his master to continue in his error, and so makes himself an accessory to his master’s sin.

Answer. There is a difference between a spiteful, revengeful contradicting of what is said, and a humble, mild, reverent, timely defense of what is unjustly censured. The latter is lawful, but the former is forbidden.

2. Others scorn to be corrected, and they show this disdain in many ways, such as:

1. By muttering and saying they did not come for that. But though that was not the main end of their subjection, yet it is a means to keep them in subjection, and is therefore to be endured by them.

2. By running away, like241 Hagar.

3. By struggling and striving with their master or mistress, and grabbing the stick or rod by the end, or by holding the hands of those who correct them.

Objection. Must I let myself be wrongfully beaten when I can help myself and prevent it?

Answer. 1. Servants must not be their own judges of whether their correction is just or unjust; for men are so prone to flatter themselves and to make light of the evil things they do that, if they were not corrected until they thought it just, they would never be corrected.

2. To endure punishment (if I may use Saint Peter’s word) is thankworthy in no other case. If you are justly punished, you have only what you deserve. If you are punished by force, so that you cannot resist, necessity makes you bear it. The sturdiest thieves there are, once their arms are bound, let themselves be hanged, because they see there is no help for it. But Christ (whose242 example in this case is set before servants)243 could have freed himself, but would not. If servants endure for conscience’ sake, they will not resist, though they are able.

3. Others, if they are struck by master or mistress, will give as much as they receive: they will strike back. It is a practice unbecoming in any Christian, but most unseemly in Christian servants, who show by it contempt for God’s image and power in their masters.

4. Others are so possessed by a devil that they will seek all the revenge they can if they are corrected. So it comes about that some hot, headstrong, reckless youths do not hesitate to challenge their masters to a duel, and others, more maliciously minded, secretly try to take their masters’ lives. Many who have not the opportunity to carry out such villainies nevertheless wish their masters’ destruction in their hearts and utter the most fearful curses against them; by this they make themselves guilty of blood before God.

§. 19. Of servants’ amending what they are justly reproved or corrected for.

More than patience is required of those who are deservedly rebuked or corrected for their faults, namely244 repentance and amendment. In this way the smart and pain which servants endure will be like good medicine to them, and turn to their good. True amendment of former faults may make one a better servant than he was before he committed those faults. Witness what Saint Paul says of Onesimus: “Formerly he was useless to you, but now he has become useful both to you and to me.”245

Contrary to this is the disposition of those who, despite all rebuke and correction, keep on in their evil and wicked courses, and go on provoking their master more and more, and so make their masters add blow to blow and stroke to stroke, until they have no hope of them and are forced to put them out of doors. This comes either from a scornful, disdainful pride (for “a scorner does not listen to rebuke”)246 or from a base, servile, numb, blockish, brutish nature that is not moved by any smart or pain, like a balky nag that will not stir however much it is whipped or beaten. Solomon247 implies as much by putting a horse, a donkey and a fool together in one leash, meaning by a fool a scornful, blockish servant, to whose back a rod is what a whip is to a horse. Of such a fool he says that a hundred lashes248 do not sink into him; and again, “Though you grind a fool in a mortar with a pestle along with grain, yet his folly will not depart from him.”249

But what shall we say of those who are made worse by rebuke and correction? What else, but that shame, beggary and some disgraceful death or other is likely to befall them?

A Servant’s Faithful and Willing Service

The Seventh Treatise: Of Servants · §§. 20–34

§. 20. Of serving with trembling.

The manner in which servants ought to perform their duties is noted in four phrases:

The first of them is this, with fear and trembling.250

Fear is both a fountain from which all other duties flow, and a sauce to season them all.

Commonly the taste and savor of waters come from the fountain. Saint James implies this where he says that no fountain yields salt water and fresh:251 for if the fountain is salt, the streams flowing from it will be salt; and fresh, if the fountain is fresh. So if fear is seated in the heart of servants, all their obedience and submission will be seasoned with it. Let servants therefore learn here to show, by their manner of performing all their duties, that there is a true servant-like fear seated in their hearts. On this I need say no more than what has been252 said before.

The other word,253 trembling, added to fear, adds emphasis, showing that it is no small fear that is required of servants. It also gives them to understand that, since their masters have power to punish them, they must conduct themselves so that they do not provoke their master to wrath, but be very careful and watchful to avoid his displeasure, so that they give him no just cause of offense. This care was in that servant of David who first spotted Absalom hanging in a tree and told Joab of it:254 he so feared the displeasure of the king his master that he dared not kill Absalom, even to gain a thousand shekels of silver. The same is noted of Obadiah, who was afraid to tell his master Ahab where Elijah was, for fear that his master would think he had mocked him, if the Spirit carried Elijah away.255

This trembling fear is needful because of the little love that servants commonly bear to their masters. There are not the same motives to stir up love in servants toward their masters as in children toward their parents. Unless, therefore, they are kept within bounds through awe and dread, they will transgress exceedingly. And because this is so needful, servants must labor to nourish it, as a means to keep them from too much boldness.

Contrary to this, on the one side, is a proud despising of a master’s authority (saying, if not with their mouths, yet in their hearts, as Gaal said of Abimelech, “Who is Abimelech … that we should serve him?”256 or as those who despised the government of Christ their master, “We do not want this man to rule over us”;257 and again, “Let us break His chains and cast away His cords”).258 And on the other side, a wretched carelessness, not fearing any punishment before they feel it, like many reckless thieves who do not fear the power of the judge in the least, but recklessly say, We have but one death to pay. The authority of God himself is despised, and his avenging hand is lightly regarded, by such proud and reckless servants; so their sin is no small sin.

§. 21. Of serving with sincerity.

The second branch concerning the manner of servants’ performing their duty is in these words, in singleness of heart,259 so that everything must be performed with an honest and upright heart. “Whatever you+ do, do it heartily,” says the apostle to servants260 in another place. In this way Joseph served his master in singleness of heart: witness his refusing to lie with his mistress in261 a private room when she desired it and no one else was in the house. Happy would it be for masters to have such servants! Then they might take no more care than Potiphar did,262 but put all that they have into their servants’ hands. Nor would this rare virtue in servants be profitable only to their masters; it would also be a great comfort to the servants themselves, and bring them much peace of conscience.

Contrary to this is hypocritical service: when servants have a heart and a heart, making a show of one heart outwardly and having another, even a completely contrary heart, within them.263 Such a one was Gehazi, who “went in and stood before his master” as if he had done his master some good service, when264 he had most highly dishonored him. And such a one was Judas, who showed as fair a face to his master as any of the disciples, and yet was an arrant traitor: for when he was thought to be going out to buy provisions for his master, he went to265 betray him.

All eye-service is contrary to the singleness of heart named above: when servants are diligent so long as their master’s eye is on them,266 like little children who will do anything their mother wants them to do while her eye is on them, but nothing when her back is turned. The world is full of such eye-serving servants, who, while their masters are present, will be as busy as bees; but if the master is away, they are either idling at home, or roaming about outside, or doing nothing but quarreling, and eating, and drinking with drunkards, like that wicked servant whom Christ notes in the parable.267 Let such servants take note of the judgment denounced against him.

§. 22. Of serving for conscience’ sake.

The third branch of the manner of servants’ performing268 their duty is in these words, as to Christ, as the servants of Christ, doing the will of God, as to the Lord. All of these set forth a good conscience, or such service as is performed for conscience’ sake, or for the Lord’s sake, which is all one, because the conscience has an eye only to the Lord, to his will and to his ordinance. Though there were no other motive in the world to move servants to obey their masters, yet their conscience toward God would move them. Such was Joseph’s manner of serving his master, as the reason which he himself gives to his mistress shows: “How could I do such a great evil and sin against God?”269 The prayer which Abraham’s servant made to God, and the thanks270 which he gave him for blessing his journey, show that he served his master for the Lord’s sake. It is too plain to need proving that such was Jacob’s service to his master. This is all the more to be noted by Christian servants, because in this lies the greatest difference between believing servants and others. Others may serve with fear and trembling, in singleness of heart, and with good will; but only saints do service as to Christ, for conscience’ sake. If this is not the thing they aim at only, yet assuredly they aim at it chiefly and principally. This makes them not content themselves with doing the thing, but strive to do it in the best manner they can, so that God may best accept it. By this, as they approve themselves to God, so they do much good to their masters and bring much comfort to their own souls.

Contrary to this is the mind of most, who do all the service which271 they do from ulterior motives. They may perform much duty, and perhaps do much good to their masters; and on that account they may get good wages at their masters’ hands, and extraordinary rewards too, and live in much quiet under them; but they can look for no reward at God’s hands. So I may say to such servants what Christ said to those who did everything to have glory from men: “They already have their full reward.”272

§. 23. Of servants’ willingness to perform their duty.

The fourth branch concerning the manner of servants’ performing273 their duty is noted in this phrase, with good will. This good will of a servant toward his master has respect partly to the disposition of the servant, and so it implies willingness and cheerfulness;274 and partly to the benefit of the master, and so it implies faithfulness.

Of willingness to do the duty which belongs to a servant, Christ Jesus (who took on himself “the form of a servant”)275 has made himself a worthy pattern. “I delight to do Your will,”276 he says to him who sent him; and again, “My food is to do the will of Him who sent Me and to finish His work.”277 Does not a man eat his food willingly, with delight and cheerfulness? Even so Christ did the work of him who sent him. So cheerfully did Jacob serve his uncle Laban that seven years “seemed but a few days” to him.278

Objection. The reason for that was the love he had for Rachel.

1. Answer. This was one reason, but not the only reason. Had he not borne good will to his uncle and master as well as love to his wife, the time might have seemed tedious enough; but both meeting together made the time pass all the better.

2. Answer. If the love he had for Rachel made him do his service so cheerfully, then if servants love God, for whose sake they ought to do their service, it will be done cheerfully.

1. What the apostle applies to the giving of alms (2 Cor 9:7) may be extended to every kind of duty which God requires: God loves cheerfulness. Therefore God has no regard for the work which is not seasoned with it.

2. As cheerfulness makes God like the work the better, so it makes the work much easier to him who does it. Our common proverb notes as much: Nothing is hard to a willing mind.

3. Let279 there be cheerfulness in a servant’s mind, and he is as free as his master: for such a servant is “the Lord’s freeman” (1 Cor 7:22); and when he cannot be made free by his master, he makes his service free, after a fashion.

Have an eye to God, to his acceptance and his reward, and it will put life into your spirit. Man’s reward makes poor men glad of work and cheerful in doing their work: it is like sugar in sour wine. Tradesmen, doctors, lawyers, all sorts of men are drawn on by gain to take great pains with great willingness. Should not God’s reward for our pains make us much more willing? Surely it would, if we had such an eye of280 faith as Moses had, to see by it the reward which he saw.

Contrary to this is heaviness of spirit and discontent of mind,281 when servants do their service with a scowl, grudgingly, under compulsion (as bears are brought to the stake), and of necessity, like slaves in a galley. Such service is bound to be done badly; and even if it is done well outwardly, the doer can have little comfort in it, because God does not accept it.

§. 24. Of servants’ quickness and diligence in their service.

Servants, as a testimony of their willingness and cheerfulness, must be both quick and diligent in their service, for these are effects of willingness. Quickness has respect to the time of doing a thing; diligence, to the pains that are taken over it.

He who is quick in his service takes up no more time over282 one thing than he must, but is ready to do one thing after another, and so does much more service. It is expressly noted of Rebekah that she hurried,283 and let down her jar; and hurried and emptied her jar, and ran back to the well. And Abraham’s servant, having made what speed he could to the place he was sent to, would not eat until he had told his errand.284 After he had told it, if he had not succeeded,285 he would not have wasted time, but gone back at once; when he had carried out everything well,286 he would not linger over any courtesies, but hurried away to his master. Ahimaaz287 strove with the Cushite over which of them should be first to bring their message to their master; and Ahimaaz outran the Cushite, for he had a very willing mind to do the business. The kind of charge which Elisha gave to his servant implies all the speed he could make. It was this: “Tie up your garment … and go! If you meet anyone, do not greet him, and if anyone greets you, do not answer him.”288

He who is diligent in his service will not only be quick in289 time, but also keep at it, and employ all the labor and pains he can to carry out well what he has to do. That general charge laid on all men belongs in particular to a servant290 (“By the sweat of your brow you will eat your bread”), and so does what Solomon notes291 (“Whatever you find to do with your hands, do it with all your might”). Great was Jacob’s diligence (for he says of himself, in doing his master’s business, “The heat consumed me by day and the frost by night, and sleep fled from my eyes”).292 Great also was the diligence of those shepherds who were “keeping watch over their flocks by night,”293 and of294 those servants who by their pains doubled the talents which were committed to them. As diligence is commended to us by these and many similar examples, so it is further set forth by the many promises made to it, such as: “Diligent hands bring wealth”;295 “The work of a man’s hands returns to him”;296 “The hand of the diligent will rule”;297298 “There is profit in all labor.”

The work which servants are appointed by their master to do is to them the work of God. That is why the apostle says to servants as well as to others, “Each one should lead the life … to which God has called him.”299 But the work of God is to be done with all diligence: for “Cursed is the one who is remiss in doing the work of the Lord.”300

Contrary to this are the idleness, laziness, slothfulness and sluggishness301 of servants. These are faults far too common. Solomon speaks out much against these vices, describing them in their kind and setting down the many harms that follow from them.

He describes them like this: “The slacker says, ‘A lion is in the road!’”302 (that is, he claims some unlikely danger); “As a door turns on its hinges, so the slacker turns on his bed. The slacker buries his hand in the dish; it wearies him to bring it back to his mouth.”303 “A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest.”

These are the harms which he notes come from idleness and sloth: poverty,304 servitude,305 fruitless wishes and desires,306 hunger,307 beggary,308 death.309 And to show what little joy or comfort masters can have in lazy servants, he compares them to vinegar and smoke, which are as irritating to the teeth and eyes as anything can be: “Like vinegar”310 (he says) “to the teeth and smoke to the eyes, so is the slacker to those who send him.” And because many idlers think and say they do no harm, he further says that “whoever is slothful in his work is brother to him who is a great waster.”311 In this respect Christ calls a slothful servant a wicked312 and unprofitable servant, and gives him the portion of those who do much harm.

However lightly many servants regard idleness and sloth, the truth is that it is plain theft. For the best service that servants can do is due to their master, and they ought to be as diligent in their master’s work as if it were their own. So it is not enough to avoid the idleness of doing nothing at all; they must also take care not to be slothful in doing something: for as good not to do it at all as to be never the better for it.

I have pressed this point all the further because it is noted as a blemish in servants who profess religion that they are the laziest and most negligent, the least industrious and diligent. Much of the time which they should spend on their master’s business (which is their particular calling) they spend chattering about matters of state and church business (matters that do not belong to them). In this way they make their masters weary of their service; and because of this they are often shifted from house to house, and, like rolling stones, they gather no moss. They neither learn a skill by which they may maintain themselves and do good to others when they are on their own, nor lay up any stock or portion, as others who are diligent do. Indeed, they get such a habit of idleness that they can never shake it off again: for those who are slothful while under masters seldom turn out diligent when they work for themselves. In them Solomon’s proverb is borne out: “Mere talk leads only to poverty.”313

§. 25. Of servants’ faithfulness.

It was314 noted before that the good will here required of servants toward their masters had respect to the profit and benefit which servants might bring to their masters by their service; and in that respect it includes faithfulness under it: faithfulness (I say), by which servants discharge well the trust committed to them. Servants are expressly charged with “showing all good faithfulness,”315 and “it is required of stewards that they be found faithful.”316 The phrase which is used of Moses, “Moses was faithful as a servant,”317 shows that faithfulness belongs to a servant as, in a special way, properly his own; and318 where Christ couples these two, good and faithful, he gives us to understand that a servant’s goodness consists in his faithfulness.

Great is the benefit that will come to both master and servant from servants’ faithfulness.

Solomon notes the benefit which comes to the master in this proverb: “Like the cold of snow” (which is very welcome, refreshing319 and profitable) “in the time of harvest” (when men are sweltering with heat) “is a faithful messenger to those who send him;320 he refreshes the soul of his masters”; and again in this: “A faithful envoy is health,” that is, he brings safety to his master.

Christ notes the benefit which comes to the servant himself by his faithfulness, in this approval and reward of it: “Well done, good and faithful servant!321 You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Enter into the joy of your master!” Every servant will be called to account: if not by his master on earth, yet by his master in heaven. He will say to every one, “Give an account of your stewardship.”322 Now then, if servants have not been faithful, what other settlement can they look for than what the wicked, slothful and unprofitable servant (who hid his talent in a cloth) received, namely this: “Throw that323 unprofitable servant into the outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.” Let all unfaithful servants note this: for all unfaithfulness is contrary to faithfulness, such as negligence, deceit, thievery, treachery and similar vices. Much damage, disgrace and vexation is brought on masters by such servants; and it would have been better if they had never come into a man’s house.

But so that the point of faithfulness may be better discerned and observed, I will illustrate it in seven particulars in which it ought especially to be exercised. They are: 1. The goods, 2. The business and affairs, 3. The counsels and secrets, 4. The other servants, 5. The children, 6. The spouse, 7. The person of their master or mistress.

§. 26. Of servants’ faithfulness with regard to their masters’ goods.

Two things are required of servants to show their faithfulness with regard to their masters’ goods.324

  1. Keeping them safe.
  2. Increasing them.

1. Whatever masters commit to their servants, the servants must preserve so carefully that it is not lost, spoiled or impaired325 under their hands, whether the things are indoors or outdoors. To this purpose I may fittingly apply to servants the charge which the apostle gave to Timothy in another case:326 “Guard what has been entrusted to you.” The metaphor is taken from servants, and so it shows what their duty is. Great was Joseph’s faithfulness in this respect, so that his master “entrusted him with everything he owned.”327 So great was Jacob’s faithfulness that328 in twenty years his master’s ewes and female goats, being under his hand, did not miscarry, nor were the rams devoured; if anything was torn by beasts, he did not bring it to his master, but bore the loss of it himself. If servants spot any damage or harm done by others to their masters’ goods, they must put it right if they can, or at least make it known to their masters, so that the master may give orders for putting it right; as did the servants of the man in the parable who sowed good seed, among which weeds were sown.329

Masters who put servants in trust go about other affairs without worry, and do not look themselves to the goods which are in their servants’ keeping, as it is to be presumed they would if they did not trust their servants. There is great reason, therefore, for servants to be careful of the things so committed to them.

2. They ought further to do what lies in them to advance330 their master’s wealth and increase his stock. The little that Laban had was, by Jacob’s faithfulness in this respect, increased many times over. The talents which were committed to the two faithful servants were increased by their industry to as many more. So it is not enough for servants not to impair their master’s wealth; they must better it. For he who kept his master’s talent safe and gave him back his own was counted an unprofitable servant, and received the sentence of wasters.

Most masters take servants for their own advantage and benefit; for it is little relief to a master to provide food and lodging for many servants, and to give them wages, if he reaps no profit and benefit from them.

§. 27. Of servants’ carelessness over their masters’ goods.

Contrary to servants’ faithfulness in keeping safe their masters’ goods committed to their charge is carelessness and negligence in allowing loss and damage to come to their masters. This happens in the fields when they do not look to his fences and gates, or neglect his cattle so that they are stolen, or allow diseases to grow on them, or do not give them fodder and water in due season. It happens in the house when they leave doors or windows open, and thieves come in and take away their masters’ goods, or hook them out; or carelessly leave the fire, or let candles burn so that the house may be set on fire; or allow household goods and clothing to lie until they are moth-eaten or otherwise spoiled; or allow anything in the house to be broken; or let food lie moldering in corners, or be thrown about for dogs and cats. When Christ so increased the bread and fish with which many thousands were fed that many fragments were left, he told his disciples to gather up what remained, and gave this reason: “so that nothing will be lost.”331 The damage which may come by servants’ carelessness may be more than they can ever make good; and therefore they ought to guard against it all the more carefully.

§. 28. Of servants’ fraud.

Contrary to the other branch of servants’ faithfulness, in increasing their masters’ wealth, is every kind of theft and fraud, whether by keeping back what is due to their masters or by stealing from them what they have.332 The apostle expressly forbids servants to purloin. The word translated purloin is the same by which the fraud of Ananias is set forth, who333 kept back part of the price of a possession which they sold. So by it is forbidden not only open and manifest stealing, such as pilfering money out of their master’s purse, chest or counter, or carrying off his grain, wares or any other goods, but also putting more into the account of expenses than has been spent, or less into the account of debts than is due (like the unjust steward, who for a debt of a hundred measures of oil put down fifty, and for a hundred of334 wheat put down eighty); or spending more than is needed; or bringing bad company into the house when their masters are absent, and treating them at their masters’ cost; or concealing part of the price taken for any wares; or borrowing money in their masters’ names; or taking greater fees than their masters know of; or receiving bribes or gifts which their masters refuse (as Gehazi did);335 or hiring others to do their work at their masters’ cost; or enticing away their masters’ customers; or stealing other men’s goods committed to their masters’ trust (like tailors’ servants, who by this both greatly discredit their masters and also hinder their trade).

Objection. What if masters withhold their servants’ wages? May the servants not right themselves by secret means?

Answer. A master’s sin is no warrant for servants to answer sin with sin. The law is as open to servants as to masters; if not, God’s ears are open to their complaints: he can and will put all right, either here or hereafter.336 Note Jacob’s example. Many claim this as a just ground; but whether it is just or not, it is not safe to open this gap to deceit.

These and similar kinds of deceit are all the more heinous sins because of the trust which masters place in servants: for they violate both the bond of justice and the bond of faithfulness. If a servant embezzles from his master or defrauds him of any of his337 goods to the value of forty shillings, he is judged a felon by our statute law.

§. 29. Of servants’ faithfulness in the business which they are to carry out for their masters.

In the business which masters commit to the care of338 their servants, to be carried out by them, servants must do their best so that all may prosper under their hands, as it is noted of Joseph, “he became a prosperous man.”339 We have not only a clear proof but also an excellent direction for this point in the340 example of Abraham’s servant, who was very faithful in carrying out the business of fetching a wife for Isaac. In it let these particulars be noted.

1. He feared God: the whole conduct of that business testifies as much. There is a double bond to tie servants to this: one with respect to themselves, that in what they do they may be accepted by God; the other with respect to their masters, that their masters’ business may prosper under their hands. God prospers those who fear him in all they take in hand: witness the examples of Jacob,341 Joseph342 and David.343

2. He344 called on God to prosper his efforts. Now prayer is the best means that can possibly be used to obtain any blessing from the Lord. Without it all our efforts are in vain345 (it is in vain to rise early and stay up late, and to eat the bread of anxious toil, unless God’s blessing goes with it all).

3. He346 gave thanks when he saw the Lord beginning to prosper what he had taken in hand. Thanksgiving for the beginning of a blessing is an effectual means for the continuance of that blessing; indeed, it is also an effectual means to move God to prosper other things that we take in hand. So if servants desire to prosper in all the affairs which they undertake for their masters, they must give thanks for the first success which God has given, and so for the second, third, fourth, and for every success.

4. He took every opportunity he could.347 When he saw a young woman to inquire of, he ran to her and asked what was needful for his purpose. When he had ground to think she was the one,348 he presented gifts to her.349 As soon as he was brought to the house, he set about the matter for which he came, even before he ate anything. After matters were settled to his liking, he made no delay, but with all speed brought the young woman to Isaac. In a word, he omitted nothing that, to the utmost of his wisdom, he judged needful for that matter: he could have done no more if the matter had wholly concerned himself. I need urge this point no further than by setting this pattern before servants.

Contrary to this is the disposition of those who do not care whether the things which they do for their master prosper or not. If they have taken some pains over them, so that their master cannot say they have altogether neglected them, that is all they care about. If it does not succeed, they will say they are not in God’s place, able to make everything that is done prosper. But though the whole blessing rests in God, yet he has sanctified means for obtaining his blessing (such as faith, prayer, thanksgiving and the like); and because they do not use these, they fail in this point of faithfulness. Indeed, worse, they use the things which most hinder and keep away God’s blessing and bring a curse on all they do instead: such as profaneness, uncleanness, drunkenness and every kind of wild living; indeed, despising God’s word and holy ordinances, reproaching his saints, taking his name in vain by swearing, false swearing and blaspheming, lying, and also backbiting, pilfering and other similar vices. These irreligious and wicked servants, as they sin against God and their own souls, so they sin also against their master: it is not to a master’s profit to keep such servants.

§. 30. Of servants’ faithfulness in keeping their masters’ secrets, and concealing their infirmities.

Because of the close bond between master350 and servants, and their close and continual living together, and the many tasks which masters have for their servants, servants come to know many of their masters’ secrets. Faithfulness therefore requires them to keep these close. This holds provided that they are not such secrets as tend to the dishonor of God, or to the danger of the commonwealth and church, or even of a private person: for Jonathan351 is commended for revealing the harm which Saul had secretly intended against David.

To prove the point, note what Solomon says: “He who is of a faithful spirit keeps a matter hidden”352: namely, a secret matter. Note also how faithful Jeremiah353 was in keeping Zedekiah’s counsel: though the officials asked about it, he would not reveal it to them.

To this head belongs a faithful concealing of masters’ infirmities: for the best people there are have many, and servants who are continually under their masters’ roof waiting on them cannot help noticing many. If servants are not faithful in this, masters would be better off without servants in their houses.

Contrary to keeping masters’ secrets close is blabbing about everything servants know concerning their masters. This is too common a fault: for when servants of different households, men or maids, meet together, nearly all their talk is of their masters and mistresses; and so it comes about that all the secrets of a house are soon known about the whole town or city. Solomon calls such a one a gossip: “A gossip,” he says, “reveals a secret”; or, “He who354 reveals secrets is a gossip”: for many of Solomon’s proverbs are convertible; they can be turned either way. Now note how Solomon shows the harm that gossips do: “The words of a gossip are like wounds” (they wound a man’s precious name and reputation) “that go down into the inmost355 being,” that is, they strike, as it were, through the very heart of a man. Again, he notes that they are the cause of all strife and stir up discord between close friends;356 and in that respect he fitly likens them to wood, which is the very fuel of fire. What enemies, then, such servants are to a house! They are like treacherous spies, the most dangerous enemies there are. So we see that this is no light sin; yet it is all the more odious when the infirmities of masters and mistresses (the publishing of which may greatly damage their reputation) are made known.

§. 31. Of servants’ faithfulness in helping one another.

Where many fellow servants are together, faithfulness requires that one be helpful to another in what they can,357 as by good example, good counsel, encouragement in good courses, dissuasion from vile and wicked practices, peace and unity, and the like. The Lord Christ expressly calls such a one a faithful servant, and pronounces him blessed.358

The example and advice of an equal prevails much with another, so that a fellow servant may in this way do more good than the master himself. And if by his means he brings his fellow servants to be faithful, his own faithfulness is doubled and trebled, and his master receives a double and treble benefit by it: namely, the benefit of this good servant’s faithfulness, and the benefit of the faithfulness of all the other servants whom he has made faithful.

Contrary to this is the practice of those who by their bad example corrupt359 their fellows (like that evil servant who, when his master was away, ate and drank with the drunken, that is, caused others to be drunk with him); or by bad counsel draw one another on to evil (like the sons of Jacob, who, when they saw Joseph far off, said to one another, “Here comes that dreamer!360 Come now, let us kill him,” etc.; and like those who, when they saw their master’s son,361 said among themselves, “This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and take his inheritance”); or are always quarreling with their fellows and striking them (like that evil servant named above, whom the Lord threatens to cut off); or dissuade one another from obedience and subjection (like those who said, “Let us break Their chains362 and cast away Their cords”; and like Sheba, who said, “We have no share in David … Every man to his tent, O Israel!”).363 These faults are very common among servants; and so it comes about that there are so few good servants and so many bad. This proverb is all too true: One scabby sheep spoils a whole flock. Let there be in a large family one servant who is profane, proud, dissolute, defiant, rebellious or otherwise vicious, and all will soon be like him.

Among other parts of unfaithfulness of this kind, one of the most monstrous (which is yet far too frequent) is to entice one another to uncleanness, and for men and maids to defile one another. We showed364 before that it was unlawful for servants to marry during the time of their service without their master’s consent: how abominable, then, it is to defile one another! The sin is doubled when it is between servants: for as it is a beastly sin in itself, so, in the respect just named, it greatly dishonors their master and his house. Besides this, the maid so defiled is often unable to do her service well; indeed, many times the charge of the child falls on the master. So shame and dishonor, grief and vexation, loss and damage all meet together, the more to gall and pierce him to the very heart. Is not this, then, a great part of unfaithfulness? Does it not deserve to be severely punished, and that openly and publicly, with shame and pain too, so that others may take warning by it? Many use means to escape the avenging hand of man; but though they escape man’s hand, they will be sure to meet with God’s heavy vengeance: “God will judge the sexually immoral.” Daily experience shows what misery365 such wretches bring on themselves, and how God catches up with them, and that most fearfully.

§. 32. Of servants’ faithfulness with regard to their masters’ children.

Servants may show great faithfulness to their masters366 in their dealings with their children: such as, while the children are young and not able to look after themselves, to be tender toward them and tend them well; maids especially to handle them neatly and look after them, and to bring them up cleanly; and those who have a particular charge of them, to seek their good in everything they can and give them their due portion; and (because children are much in servants’ company) to use such speech in their hearing as may minister grace to them, and to teach them good things; and (when they grow to some ripeness of years) to bear a reverent respect toward them and esteem them their betters, because they are their master’s children. It is noted of Abraham’s servant that he called his master’s367 son master.

Children are dear to parents: they count the honor and kindness done to them as done to themselves. This kind of faithfulness must therefore be highly valued by masters in their servants; and assuredly it is a great means of working a good regard in masters toward them.

Yet many servants behave contrary to this toward368 their masters’ children, as is evident from these particulars.

1. Some are very boorish and churlish in their conduct toward their masters’ children when the parents are out of sight.

2. Others are very careless of them, and tend them in a very slovenly way, not caring how they look; by which they often bring great displeasure on themselves from their master and mistress.

3. Others get what they can from their masters’ children, and deprive them of their allowance, turning it to their own gain.

4. Others greatly corrupt their masters’ children with their filthy and corrupt talk, teaching them to swear, blaspheme and use every kind of unclean speech. So that proverb is borne out: “Evil talk369 corrupts good character.” Children often learn in their young years such bad language and behavior from wicked servants that their parents can never get them to leave it again; so that they may curse the day that such servants ever came into their house.

5. Others entice them to stage-plays, to gaming houses and other such places, which are the very bane of youth, and draw them to spend in wild living the allowance their parents give them, indeed beyond that allowance, so that they run into debt and get such a habit of spending that at length they squander their whole estate.

6. Others entice their affections, and often draw them into folly and uncleanness. This filthy kind of unfaithfulness is as much worse than that which was370 noted before between fellow servants as children are nearer and dearer to their parents than servants are to their masters.

7. Others, who dare not commit this abominable wickedness, do not hesitate to do what is little better: namely, to draw them on to be betrothed, indeed often married, to them, and that secretly, without their parents’ consent. By this, parents’ affections are often so alienated from their children that they will not acknowledge them as children, but cast them off completely. These are the fruits of this wicked kind of unfaithfulness in servants.

§. 33. Of servants’ faithfulness with regard to their master’s or mistress’s spouse.

Servants ought to be so faithful to their masters and371 mistresses that, if one of them should try to use a servant in any kind of deceit against the other, the servant ought not to yield. For example, a master might urge his maid secretly to take away jewels, silver plate, money, linen or any such thing as is in her mistress’s keeping. It makes no difference that the master has the chief power over all the goods: a secret taking of them away without the knowledge of the mistress in whose keeping they are is deceit in the servant, and a point of unfaithfulness. Much less ought any servants to be moved by their mistress secretly to take away their master’s grain, wares or any goods for her private use. Of the two, this is the greater part of unfaithfulness.

If such deceit ought not to be used about any goods, much less about the body of master or mistress. For example, if a master should entice his maid to commit folly with him, or a mistress her manservant, both their conscience toward God and their faithfulness to their master or mistress should make them utterly refuse it, and give no place to any such temptation. Joseph372 is set before us as a pattern in this; and against his mistress’s urging he gives the two reasons named above. He shows his conscience toward God in these words: “How could I do such a great evil and sin against God?” He shows his faithfulness to his master in these: “He has withheld nothing from me except you … So how could I,” etc.

To this head may be referred servants’ faithfulness in making known to their master the sin of his wife, and to their mistress the sin of her husband, especially if it is such a sin as may tend to the ruin of the family, and so that, by knowing of it, the party who is not blinded and besotted with the sin, but rather free from it, may be a means to redress it. In this way Nabal’s373 servants made known to Abigail Nabal’s churlishness toward David’s servants, by which means the harm intended against the house was averted. So if servants know that their master intends some deadly revenge against another, to tell his wife of it in time may be great faithfulness; or if they know that their mistress has planned to go away secretly from her husband, to tell him of it is a part of faithfulness. This may be applied to many other similar cases.

The contrary is yielding to masters or mistresses in any point of deceit against one another. Servants are too prone to this, because they think they will be shielded by the authority of the party who sets them to work to deceive. But no authority can be a warrant for any deceit or wickedness.

§. 34. Of servants’ faithfulness with regard to their masters’ persons.

Masters and mistresses are flesh and blood as well as servants,374 and so subject to weakness, sickness, old age and other distresses, in which they may stand in great need of servants’ help. Servants therefore must be faithful in giving them the best help they can. Saul’s servants did a part of faithfulness to their master when, as he was tormented by an evil spirit,375 they inquired after means to ease him. So did David’s servants when, as he was bedridden,376 they sought out one to care for him. It was a point of faithfulness in Naaman’s377 maid to tell her mistress of a means by which her master might be cured of his leprosy; and in his378 servants, to persuade him to use the means prescribed by the prophet.

Contrary to this is a servant’s ungrateful and inhumane leaving of his master in his time of need, as the servants of Job did. For when the hand of God lay heavy on him, and all his goods were taken from him, and his body was full of sore boils, those who dwelt in his house, and his maids, counted him a stranger; he was a foreigner in their sight; he called his servant, and he gave him no answer.379 So Ziba380 left Mephibosheth in his greatest need; and so381 the disciples fled from their master Jesus Christ. But what shall we say of those who take occasion from their master’s helplessness to murder him themselves, as Rechab382 and Baanah did; or to betray him to his enemies, as Judas383 betrayed the Lord Christ? Only this: that such treacherous servants may look for such ends.

A Servant’s Duty and Its Limits under God

The Seventh Treatise: Of Servants · §§. 35–43

§. 35. Of the means to make servants faithful.

Among many other means to make servants faithful to their master, and careful to perform their other duties sincerely, willingly, cheerfully and diligently, as has been noted before, this is one of the most general: that servants, in all things they do for their master, make their master’s case their own, and so do for him as they would for themselves, or as they would have their own servants do for them. The general rule of the Law is, “love your neighbor as yourself”;384 and “In everything, do to others as you+ would have them do to you+.”385 If every man must regard another in this way, even though he is a stranger, then much more must servants regard their master, because all that they can do is, in a special way, a debt owed to their masters. In this respect Christ says that when they have done all that is386 commanded, they have only done their duty.

I have noted this all the more because the practice of most servants is contrary to it. For while they work for their masters, they will cry out that they are overburdened and tired with work which they will easily get through when they work for themselves. Those who must be called to it again and again while they work for their master, and forced to keep at it and hold out till it is done: what pains they will take, how early they will rise, how late they will sit up, how diligent they will be for themselves! When journeymen are paid more according to the work they do, they will get through more than an apprentice who has only clothing, food and lodging. Again, many who are very lavish and wasteful with their master’s goods are very sparing, saving and provident when they come to be their own masters. What do these things show but that servants do not bear toward their masters the mind they should? They do not do for them as they would do for themselves. It is very needful, therefore, that the general rule named above be observed.

So much for the separate kinds of servants’ duties, and the manner of performing them. It remains to speak both of the extent of their duties and of the restraint of that extent.

§. 36. Of servants’ striving to make their judgment agree with their master’s.

The extent of servants’ duties is only implied in my text, under this indefinite word (Obey) (which, not being restricted to any particulars, must be extended to everything); but in other places it is expressly laid down in these general terms: “Obey … in all things,”387 “please them well in all things.”388 It is not enough, therefore, for servants to perform their duties well in some things; they must do so in all things, even in things that may be against their own mind and liking, if their master will have it so. To this end let servants note these two rules. The same extent and restraint389 that was390 noted before in setting out the duties of other inferiors is to be observed here also in the duties of servants, for in this they all agree. The same rules, therefore, that were set down before may be applied here again. I refer the reader to those places for a fuller treatment of the general points; here I will content myself with adding such particular proofs as are most proper and pertinent to servants.

1. That they labor to bring their judgment into line with their master’s judgment, and to think that fitting and good which391 he does. So the392 servant of the Levite, who thought it best to turn into one of the cities of the Jebusites to lodge there because the day was far spent, yielded to his master when he saw him to be of another mind. So did393 those servants yield to their master who at first thought it unfitting that he who had ten talents should have one more; and394 those servants also who at first thought it fitting that the tares should be pulled up from among the wheat. This rule is to be observed in the particular points dealt with before, such as the work servants do and the manner of doing it, their clothing, their allowance, indeed even the correction their master gives them, and the rest.

Contrary to this is the overweening opinion which many servants have of395 their own judgment, understanding and wisdom, thinking themselves wiser than their masters. So did Gehazi, who, setting his own understanding against his master’s wisdom, said, “Look, my master has spared this Aramean, Naaman, while not accepting what he brought. As surely as the Lord lives, I will run after him and get something from him.”396 This was also a fault in397 the sons of the prophets, who would not rest on Elisha’s judgment, but pressed him against his mind to send some men to look for the body of Elijah. This self-conceit is the cause of many harms: of discontent at the work their master appoints them and at the allowance of food and drink they have; of the great liberty they take to themselves; of pride in clothing; and of other vices noted before. If the rule named above were duly observed, many of those harms would be avoided, and much better obedience given.

§. 37. Of servants’ yielding to do such things at their master’s command as they cannot think to be most fitting.

The second rule which servants must observe is this:

Though in their judgment they cannot think that what their master wants done is fit to be done, yet at his peremptory command they must yield to doing it. It appears from Peter’s answer to Christ that he did so. For when he was commanded to let down his net for a catch, he answered like this: “Master, we have worked hard all night without catching anything”398 (here he shows that his opinion was that it would be in vain to let down their nets); “But because You say so, I will let down the nets” (here is his obedience against his opinion). The example of Joab is clearer for this purpose. When the king commanded him to number the399 people, he made it plain that he thought it a very unfitting thing to do, by this phrase: “But why does my lord the king want to do such a thing?” Yet against his judgment he yielded to the king’s peremptory command; for it is said that “the king’s word prevailed against Joab.”

Objection. This is no fit example, because Joab sinned in obeying.

Answer. Joab cannot justly be charged with sin, because it was not in itself unlawful to number the people. David’s sin was not in the act of numbering the people, but in the mind that moved him to do it. There was no just cause to do it; only pride and curiosity moved him, as may be gathered from his own reason in these words: “so that I may know their number.” To do such a thing only to know it was curiosity. But why would he know it? Surely from a proud thought that he had so many mighty men, so many captains, so many men of war.

From Joab’s example servants may learn here to give, in humility and reverence, some reasons to move their master not to press on them what they think unfitting. But if their master will not listen to their reasons, but stands on his authority, his word must prevail.

Contrary to this is the peremptoriness of those who can by no means be drawn to do anything at their master’s command which they themselves do not think most fitting to be done. Such a one is that fool whom Solomon describes in this way: “Though you400 grind a fool in a mortar with a pestle along with grain, yet his folly will not depart from him.” These fools bring much injury on their own heads by disobeying their masters, for they greatly provoke the wrath of him who has power to take vengeance on them. Nor let them expect to receive comfort in their suffering because they refuse an unfitting thing; for fitness is not a sufficient warrant against unlawfulness. To disobey in a thing which may lawfully be done is unlawful. If, therefore, the pretext is only an unfitting thing, fitness is preferred to lawfulness, and unlawfulness is counted a lesser matter than unfitness.

§. 38. Of servants’ refraining from obeying their master against God.

So that the extent of servants’ obedience is not stretched too far,401 the apostle sets down an excellent limitation of it, and that in these four phrases: as to Christ, as the servants of Christ, doing the will of God, as to the Lord. All of these show that the obedience which servants give to their master402 must be such as may stand with their obedience to Christ. So if masters command their servants ever so peremptorily to do any unlawful thing, that is, anything forbidden by God’s403 word, they must not yield to it. The midwives of the Hebrew women did well in refusing to do anything to further the murderous practices of the king of Egypt in killing all the male children of the Hebrews. It is expressly said that they “feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt had instructed”; so their disobedience in this matter was a token of their fear of God. In this case Joseph is commended for not listening to his mistress, and the servants of Saul for refusing to kill the404 Lord’s priests at their master’s command. So if a master should command his servant to kill, to steal, to swear falsely, to lie, to use false measures and weights, to go to Mass, or to do any other unlawful thing, he ought not to obey him.

Again, if masters forbid their servants to do what405 God has commanded them to do, they must do it in spite of their masters’ prohibition. The rulers of Israel forbade the apostles to preach; yet because Christ had commanded them to preach, they would not refrain.406 Nor would Daniel refrain from praying to God, though the king and nobles forbade him by express decree.407 So if a profane or popish master forbids his servant to go to church, or to hear the word, or to take the sacrament, or to live with his wife if he is married, or to make restitution of what he has gotten by fraud, or any other duty to which he is bound, in this servants must say, “We must obey God rather than men.”408 For when masters command and forbid anything against God, they go beyond their commission, and in that their authority ceases.

Contrary to this restraint are both a sycophantic pleasing of masters and a base fear of them. It is the mark of a409 sycophant to say what his master would have him say, and deny what he would have him deny, and so to do what he would have him do. Doeg, that fawning dog, killed all the Lord’s410 priests at Saul’s word; and Absalom’s servants killed Amnon at his word. For411 all the reason they had to commit that murder was this speech of their master: “Have I not commanded you+?” Servants are so prone to flatter their masters that there is no sin so horrible that they will not be ready to do it at their masters’ command. In this way what the prophet foretold long ago is proved true:412 “like master, like servant; like mistress, like maid.” It is also the mark of base, fearful servants to do nothing but what their master would have them do, and to leave undone any duty, however necessary, which he forbids them to do. What duty is more necessary than prayer? We are commanded to “pray without ceasing”;413 yet the servants of Darius were content to leave that duty undone for thirty days on end, because it was against the decree of their lord and king. The like is noted of the people in Christ’s time: they dared not openly confess414 Christ for fear of the Jews. So in our days there are many servants who dare not profess the gospel, or go to church, or read the word, or perform other holy religious duties which they know to be binding duties, for fear of their masters. Did the apostle not have just cause, then, to strike so much on this string as he has done here, expressly forbidding eye-service, man-pleasing and doing service to men, namely in opposition to God?

§. 39. Of servants’ choosing good masters.

As a just consequence following from the extent and restraint of servants’ duties named above, I may further gather these two lessons for servants.

1. It is very needful that servants choose good masters, at least if it is in their power to choose.

2. It is needful that they continue and stay under good masters, at least if they continue as servants and stay under any masters.

That these consequences justly follow, as said above, is evident. For seeing that servants are bound to obey in all things which are not against God, and must obey in nothing but what is in the Lord, it is very needful that servants be under such masters as bear the image of God in the inward disposition and grace of their heart, as well as in their outward function and place; masters who will go along with God in using their authority, commanding nothing but what a servant may do with a good conscience and without transgressing against God, and forbidding nothing that God has bound a servant to do. There will be comfort in serving such masters, and our obedience to them will be obedience to God. Such masters, therefore, must be chosen. Indeed, with such masters servants must stay (if not with the very same, yet with such as they are, of the same disposition). For if they go to others, their former comfort will be taken away, and their servitude will seem all the more miserable, the more knowledge and experience they have had of the benefit of their former liberty. (For we may well call service under masters without conscience servitude, and, in contrast to it, service under religious masters liberty.) He therefore who has a master that is faithful must, with due respect to his authority, love him as a father,415 and so stay with him.

For the choice of good masters, note what is416 recorded of many of the Egyptians and other peoples: they left their own country and went out with the Israelites. What could move them but conscience toward the true God, whom they knew Israel served? Now many of these went out as servants, as may be gathered from those many417 laws which were made on behalf of servants who were strangers, and especially those who were Egyptians. The knowledge Ruth418 had that Naomi, her mother-in-law, served the true God moved her to leave her own country and to go as a servant with Naomi.

For staying with good masters we have a worthy pattern in the twelve disciples.419 When many of Christ’s disciples in the wider sense “turned back and no longer walked with Him,” and Christ asked the twelve whether they too would go away, Peter, in the name of all the rest, answered, “Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life.”

Contrary to this is the mind and practice of many servants: they never inquire into the religious disposition of their master, nor care if he is popish or profane, so long as they may have good wages, meals and lodging. Yet by this means, if there is at least any spark of the fear of God in their heart, they expose themselves to many severe temptations, and bring themselves into many hard straits and dangerous snares. And if God opens their eyes to see the wretched condition into which they have plunged themselves, they will be forced to cry out and complain as David did when he was in foreign countries where he could not freely serve his God: “Woe to me that I dwell in Meshech, that I live among the tents of Kedar!”420

Much more contrary is the mind and disposition of those who refuse to serve religious masters and shun them most of all, or who, being in their service, are never at rest till they are out of it again. We spoke of these421 before.

So far we have dealt with servants’ duties. The reasons which the apostle gives to enforce those duties remain to be set out.

§. 40. Of the first motive, the place of masters.

The first reason the apostle uses to enforce servants’422 duties is implied in this phrase, as to Christ. It points to the place of a master, which is to be in Christ’s stead. In this respect the title Lord is given to a master. The word the apostle uses in Greek is the one which in the New Testament423 answers to that proper Hebrew name of God, Jehovah, and it sets out the absolute sovereignty of God and his power over all creatures.424 Because the Father has given this power to his Son as Mediator, God-man, he is called the425 one or only Lord. And because masters, by virtue of their office and place, bear Christ’s image and stand in his stead, through Christ’s imparting his authority to them, they are called lords, indeed even gods426 (for what a magistrate is in the commonwealth, a master is in the family).

Hence it follows that servants, in performing duty to their master, perform duty to Christ, and in rebelling against their master they rebel against Christ; as the Lord said to Samuel about the people’s rejecting his government, “it is not427 you they have rejected, but they have rejected Me as their king.” Is this not a strong motive to stir servants up to every duty, and to restrain them from rebellion? If one weighs well what Christ is, it cannot but be a weighty reason. For though masters should neither reward their good service nor punish their bad service, yet Christ will do both.

§. 41. Of the second motive, the place of servants.

The second reason is implied in this phrase, as the servants428 of Christ. It points to the place of a servant, which, though it may seem a lowly and base place, is in fact an honorable place. Men count it an honor to be servants to a king; but Christ is higher than all kings. On this ground the apostle says, “Were you a servant when you were called? Do not let it concern you,”429 and that for this reason: “he who was a servant when he was called by the Lord is the Lord’s freeman.” This reason is to be noted against the notion most people have of a servant’s place, that it is so lowly and base that there can be no honor or comfort in doing its duties. But that is a foolish and unjust notion. Look to Christ, the highest master, and there is as much honor and comfort in doing the duties of the lowest servant’s place as of the highest master’s.

§. 42. Of the third motive, God’s will.

The third reason is implied in this phrase, doing the430 will of God, which declares the ground of servants’ subjection. God in his word has plainly made it known that it is his pleasure that those who are under the authority of masters should obey them. Therefore, if servants would please God, they must obey; if they refuse to obey, they thwart the will of God. This also is a weighty motive. For God’s will is the mark which everyone ought to aim at, and the Holy Spirit urges it much as a general reason for all duty, in these and similar phrases: “This is the will of God,”431 “Such is the will of God.”432 On this ground we are exhorted to “understand”433 and to “test and approve what the will of God is.”434

There is good reason to press this reason:

1. God’s will is the very ground of goodness: things are good for this reason, that they agree with God’s will. God’s will gives goodness its very being.

2. God’s will is a rule to square all our actions by, just as the king’s statutes and proclamations are to his subjects.

3. It is a perfect rule (“the Law of the Lord is perfect”),435 so that we may be sure not to swerve if we hold close to it.

4. It is a sufficient rule: it will give everyone (and servants among others) direction how to conduct themselves in everything they take in hand, indeed in everything that belongs to them. For God’s word is given to make us “complete, fully436 equipped for every good work.”

5. It is a good warrant to justify us in all our actions, so that, going along with it, we need not care what any man can say against us. If a man is sure that he has statute law or the king’s proclamation on his side, he is bold.

From this reason, which is of such weight, I gather two propositions to add force to this motive.

1. That servants obey their masters is no optional matter but a necessary duty: not left to a servant’s will whether he will do it or not, but a thing to which he is bound; and that not only by the civil laws of men, but also by a divine institution of God. So it is not only a matter of public order but also of conscience, to be done for conscience’ sake.

2. That no created being can grant servants a dispensation, so that they should be exempted from doing their duty to their master. If any could, they would be greater than God, and their will above God’s will. Masters themselves are to be reckoned among created beings. Now because it is God’s will that servants should be in subjection, their masters cannot exempt them from it. Masters may set them free; but while they keep them as servants, they cannot exempt them from a servant’s subjection. Therefore, though masters are careless in demanding duty, let servants be conscientious in giving duty, because it is God’s will.

§. 43. Of the fourth reason, the reward of good service.

The fourth reason is plainly and fully expressed in these437 words: “Knowing that whatever good thing any man does, the438 same he will receive from the Lord, whether he is slave or free.” Its general sum is a declaration of the reward of good service. To this purpose the apostle says to servants in another place, “From the Lord you+ will receive a reward.”

This is to be noted first, to add force to the former reasons. For if anyone asks, what if masters do stand in Christ’s stead, what has that to do with the matter? Surely it has much to do with moving servants, because masters stand in the place of him who will reward what is done for his sake.

If again they ask, what benefit is it to be Christ’s servant? It may be answered: very great; for Christ will reward all his servants.

If it is further asked, what is gained by doing God’s will?

Answer. Just what all aim at: reward for service.

As this adds weight to all the other reasons, so it is in itself a weighty reason, as weighty as any can be to our corrupt nature. It is hope of reward that makes all sorts of people take the pains they do in their separate places. But having439 dealt with this general point of reward before, I will show here more distinctly what kind of reward it is with which the Lord will recompense the good service of faithful servants.

God’s reward consists of both temporal and eternal blessings.

The temporal blessings which God has bestowed in the past440 on faithful servants, and which other such servants may on that ground expect, are especially these.

1. He will move masters to show regard for such servants, as he moved441 Ahasuerus to recompense the faithfulness of Mordecai.

2. If masters fail in this, he will move strangers to recompense them, as he moved442 the jailer to favor Joseph when his master had cast him into prison, and Pharaoh443 to raise him to great dignity.

3. To draw the hearts of their masters and others more to them, he will make the things they take in hand prosper. In this way he blessed Joseph444 and Abraham’s445 servant.

4. When they work for themselves, he will bless their labors, as he blessed Jacob’s.446

5. When they come to keep servants, he will provide such servants for them as they were to their masters.447 In Egypt God blessed Joseph with a faithful servant. David, who448 risked his life to save his father’s sheep, had449 many servants who risked their lives for him.

To this purpose may be applied that proverb which Christ often uses: “with the measure you+ use, it will be measured back to you+.”450

The eternal reward is expressed in this phrase, “the reward of the inheritance.”451 For the inheritance meant is the one Christ mentions in this clause, “Come, you+ who are blessed by My Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you+ from the foundation of the world,”452 and Saint Peter in this, “an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, reserved in heaven.”453 What more can be said? What will not be undergone for this reward? How willingly do apprentices go through their apprenticeship in hope of a temporary freedom of an earthly city!454 Many years’ service was only like a few days to Jacob, because he liked his reward. But see here a greater reward, which we must surely like much better. If this is not enough to move servants to every duty, I do not know what can be enough.

The Eighth Treatise: Of Masters

A Master’s Authority Rightly Maintained

The Eighth Treatise: Of Masters · §§. 1–12

§. 1. Of the heads of masters’ duties.

Eph 6:9.

And you+ masters, do the same things to them, forbearing threatening, knowing that your+ Master also is in heaven, and there is no respect of persons with Him.

Last of all, the duties of masters are set out by the apostle, and that in this verse, where he notes:

  1. The kinds of their duties.
  2. A reason to enforce them.

The kinds are set out:

  • Generally (do the same things.)
  • Particularly (forbearing threatening.)

The reason is taken from the subjection in which masters stand to a higher master (knowing that your+ Master also.) And it is amplified:

  1. By the equal subjection of masters and servants to that master (your+ … also.)
  2. By the place where that master is (in heaven.)
  3. By his attribute, in this phrase (there is no respect of persons with Him.)

Because the separate duties of masters are only pointed at here, and wrapped up in general terms, I will (as has been done in the former treatises) set out a distinct order, by which we may better find out their duties, and handle them one after another.

All of them may be brought under these two heads:

  1. Care in choosing good servants.
  2. Conscience in using them well.

To use their servants well, masters must have an eye to their place and authority, and with respect to it they must:

  1. Wisely maintain their authority.
  2. Rightly manage it.

The right managing of their authority is noted generally in this phrase, do the same things, but more particularly in455 another place it is referred to these two branches:

  1. Justice.
  2. Equity.

Of these in order.

§. 2. Of masters choosing good servants.

The first thing that a man who takes it on himself to be a master must take care of is to receive good servants into his service. “My eyes456 shall be on the faithful of the land,” says David, “that they may dwell with me; he who walks in the way of integrity shall serve me.” What does this phrase (my eyes shall be on the faithful) imply, but that he will diligently and carefully inquire after such men? Indeed, my eyes (he says), not another man’s eyes: he would not put all the trust on others; he would himself make what test and trial he could. The phrase (my eyes) has the same emphasis where Job457 says, “I will see Him for myself; my eyes will behold Him, and not another.”

1. Servants are, of all other things (except wife and children),458 of the best and greatest use. If, then, men are careful in choosing other things well, such as houses, land, household goods, horses and all kinds of livestock (as men are very circumspect in these), should they not be much more careful in choosing servants well?

2. In this way masters will show that they seek and aim at the good of their family, and indeed, in their family, at the good of church and commonwealth; for good members of a family are the likeliest to turn out profitable to church and commonwealth.

3. When good servants are chosen, there is hope of receiving more good from them, and of doing more good to them. They will be pliable to all good admonition, teachable by all good instruction, serviceable in all things they take in hand.

Objection. A master may make a bad servant into a good one.

Answer. There is great risk and hazard in that, especially if they have grown to ripe years. No creature is easily tamed, or brought from the natural course it has taken, after it has grown up; a bough that has grown crooked for many years on end will hardly be made straight. It is not in man’s power to make a bad servant good; nor can a man expect God’s blessing on it when he does not follow a right course warranted by God.

Question. What course is fittest for the choice of servants?459

1. If they are young, see how they have been brought up from their infancy. The benefit of a good upbringing is great, and much good may be expected from it, for it is the means which God has sanctified for good.

But if they have grown to ripe years, choose such as fear God; such were those on whom David460 cast his eyes.

On this ground461 Saint Paul advised Philemon to take Onesimus back, because grace had then been worked in his heart.

1. The fear of God is the ground of all good obedience and462 faithfulness. Note the examples of the good and faithful servants who are commended in Scripture, and you+ will find that they all feared God.

2. Such servants will not only be diligent in their work, but will also faithfully call on God to prosper the things which they take in hand on their master’s behalf, as the example of Abraham’s463 servant shows.

3. God will have such regard for servants who fear his name as to bless their master’s house for their sakes. So Potiphar’s464 house was blessed for Joseph’s sake.

2. Make some trial of their fitness for the calling, work and service to which you will put them. From such servants there may be hope of profit and benefit by their service. Laban465 made trial of Jacob for a month before he made any agreement with him for a long continuance.

3. Take especially those who are of lowly and poor condition, and do not know how to support themselves except by service. In this way a double work of charity will be done, and in this way you may look for better service; for commonly such servants are the most industrious and the most obedient to their masters.

Objection. When men have taken all the care they can in their choice, they may still be deceived.

Answer. 1. Then it is much more likely that they will be deceived if they are careless in it.

2. Diligent and wise inquiry is a means of finding out the disposition and ability of servants.

3. If men are deceived after using the means, they may have the more comfort in bearing the cross, because they have not knowingly or carelessly pulled it on themselves.

§. 3. Of masters’ carelessness in choosing servants.

Contrary to this is the carelessness of those who are ready to receive anyone into their house, even atheists, papists, swearers, swaggerers, profane wretches, unclean persons and the like, against whom David protests: “No one who practices deceit shall dwell in466 my house; no one who tells lies shall stand in my presence.” Those who take in such people make their houses cages of unclean birds, seedbeds of wicked persons and unprofitable members; for one scabbed sheep is enough to infect a whole flock. It is no wonder if many injuries fall on such a family, for a man might as well bring so many snakes and adders into his house as such persons; indeed, with them God’s curse is brought in. Can any good be looked for, then? Such foolish masters regard neither themselves nor their household, no, nor church or commonwealth either. If no one would take such people in, it might be a means to make them change their ways.

Many are not only careless in choosing good servants, but even object to those who are religious, thinking it a matter of reproach to take them in. By this notion they often drive God’s blessing away from their house.

Others insist so much on a large sum of money with an apprentice, a clerk or another servant that they will take none but rich men’s children. So it comes about that this point of charity (which is a great point) is neglected: persons most unfit for service are taken in, and many times such as scorn to do service. So their masters grow weary of them; the servants lose their time and never become masters of their craft; and their parents regret giving such a sum with them.

§. 4. Of masters maintaining their authority.

After masters have chosen good servants, their duty is to use them well; and because of the difference between masters and servants this cannot be done well unless masters wisely maintain their authority. A master therefore must be able “to rule his own household well.”467 This is a duty which the apostle requires of a bishop in particular, who is master of a house, but it belongs in general to all masters of families. Women also, who are mistresses by virtue of their places, are commanded468 “to govern their households,” or to rule, and to play the part of a mistress in them. It was the469 centurion’s commendation that, having servants under him, he had them at his command.

1. In this way the image and authority of God, which a master carries, is preserved.

2. In this way a master will have much better service done. Not one servant in a thousand who is not kept under authority will do good service. A duty like this was enjoined on a husband in relation to his wife, Treatise 4, §. 4. Some of the reasons, directions and other points handled there may be applied here. Read it, therefore.

To this end three things must be observed.470

1. That masters conduct themselves worthily of their place, and of the honor which is due to them. This may best be done by making themselves a pattern of such good things as belong to them in their places. “As for me and my house, we will serve the Lord,”471 says Joshua: he would not only set them to it, but would also do it himself; he would go before them. “I will behave wisely … I will walk in my house with integrity of heart,” says472 David.

2. That masters keep their servants in awe and fear.473 Children must be “kept in subjection”; much more servants.

3. That masters do what they do in their conduct toward their servants with authority. Command, forbid, rebuke (to use474 the apostle’s phrase) “with all authority.” The manner of speech which the centurion used to his servants475 (“Go,” “Come,” “Do this”) carries the ring of authority. So the church, taking on herself the person of a mistress, uses a word of authority: “I charge you+”476 not to do this.

§. 5. Of masters causing their authority to be despised.

The failings in the defect, contrary to the duty477 and point of wisdom named above, are many, such as these:

1. When masters conduct themselves in a base and abject way before478 their servants: being frivolous in their behavior, foolish in their conduct, given to drunkenness, uncleanness, wicked company and other vices. Nabal was such a man, for his servant could say of him, “He is such a scoundrel.” David,479 though he did not give himself over to such foul sins as these, yet often failed by unseemly conduct before his servants: as when “he changed his behavior and feigned madness … he scratched on the doors of the gate and let his saliva run down his beard”;480 and again,481 when he gave such free rein to his grief for the death of his traitorous son Absalom that his people, being ashamed, stole away from him, and Joab his servant was forced to tell him of it bluntly, saying, “Today you have disgraced all your servants,” etc.

Mistresses often lose their authority by conspiring with their servants to go out, take away goods, gossip, and do other such things secretly without their husbands’ consent. In this way they make themselves slaves to their servants, not daring to do anything which may offend them, for fear that the servants should reveal to their masters such wicked pranks as their mistresses have played.

2. When masters are too lax and timid, begging and482 pleading with their servants to do such things as they ought to command and require of them; and if it is not done, their only remedy is patience, or else to do it themselves. However much this might be counted meekness and gentleness toward equals and strangers, over whom we have no authority, yet toward servants it is too base a laxness; indeed, it is a giving up of the power which God has given, and of which God will take an account.

3. When masters allow their servants to be their companions,483 playing, drinking and reveling with them, and saying (as the proverb has it) hail fellow, well met. In this way servants often take the liberty to set themselves above their master; for men are naturally prone to ambition, and if an inch is given, they will take a mile. Those who debase themselves so far in this way as to give their servants power over their own body make both themselves and their true, lawful spouse despised: themselves, because servants who have been made one flesh with them in this way will expect to keep in awe the master or mistress they have known, who will fear their revealing that sin; their spouse, because such servants will expect to be maintained and bolstered up by the master or mistress whom they have known in this way. On this ground Sarah484 was despised in the eyes of Hagar her maid.

4. When masters are overruled by their servants into doing any485 unjust or unlawful thing: as Joash,486 who was drawn into idolatry by his servants; and Zedekiah,487 who gave the prophet Jeremiah into his servants’ hands, using this base and abject speech: “The king can do nothing to stop you+.”488 In this way servants will soon become masters; and if they once come to this high pitch, to rule rather than to be ruled, they will quickly become intolerable. For489 this is one of the four things, noted by the wise man, under which “the earth … cannot bear up”: namely, “a servant who becomes king.” And490 this is one of the evils which proceed from rulers, “servants on horseback,” that is, servants advanced above their place and rank; from which it follows that “princes go on foot like servants”:491 they are cast down below their place and rank. It happens in this case between servants and masters as between scales or balances: if the weights that usually lie in one pan to keep it down are taken away, it will suddenly fly up, and so the other pan will be kept down.

§. 6. Of masters’ too great severity.

The contrary in the excess is too great severity and harshness, shown in look, speech and actions.

1. In look, when a master cannot look kindly on his servant. Jacob492 was much disheartened by the countenance of his master. Many masters greatly terrify their servants by their continually frowning brows and fiery eyes.

2. In speech, when masters cannot give their servants a good word, but if they are moved ever so little, heap on them all the reproachful names they can call to mind, little thinking of this fearful sentence: “anyone who says, ‘You fool!’ will be subject to the fire of hell.”493

In this way Saul showed a malicious and spiteful mind against Jonathan and David by the foul language he used. Some have such a perverse and crooked tongue that they can never speak plainly to their servants: if they command or forbid a thing, they will do it in such a way that their servant can hardly tell what they mean; and this not only when their servant has offended them, but in their best mood. Others are so shrewish that their tongues are seldom still: they are always scolding on every small occasion. So it comes about that their servants are no more moved by it than the doves and starlings that live continually in belfries are moved by the ringing of the bells. Such bitterness is also mixed with the scoldings of many, so that they belch out of their black mouths the most dreadful curses. What can be said of such tongues, but that they are “set on fire by hell”?494 As the other failings for which masters are reproved are to be applied to mistresses, so this one especially; for mistresses commonly offend most in shrewishness of speech.

3. In actions, when masters are too frequent and too furious495 strikers, striking their servants on every occasion, not caring how they strike. In these and other such signs of too much severity and plain arrogance, masters forget that they are men. Though for outward order a master is more excellent than a servant, yet as a man he ought to judge himself his equal. This extreme can be no good means of maintaining authority; it is plainly an abuse of it. Masters ought so496 to conduct themselves that their servants may rather reverence than dread them.

§. 7. Of masters’ commanding power limited to lawful things.497

So that a master may know better how to maintain his authority, I will set out distinctly the extent of his power, and that in these four particulars: 1. In commanding. 2. In correcting. 3. In ordering the marriage of their servants. 4. In having the disposal of their persons.

Within the bounds of these two virtues, Justice and Equity (to which all the duties that masters owe their servants will498 afterward be referred), the commanding power of masters must be confined.

  1. Justice requires two things:

    1. A restraint on masters’ commandments.
    2. An exercise of them.

The restraint is to God’s law: that a master command nothing against it,499 but only what agrees with it. Abishai would gladly have had David’s warrant to kill Saul; but David was so far from commanding him to do it that he kept him from it.

Masters are only subordinate ministers under God; they must therefore command nothing against his law. As a judge, a high sheriff and all other officers under the king must make the king’s law the ground of everything they require of the king’s subjects, so masters must make God’s law the ground of everything they require of their servants, who are also the servants of Christ. Besides, what is the point of commanding what a servant may and must refuse to do? But in no500 unlawful thing may he obey.

§. 8. Of masters presuming beyond their authority.

Contrary to this is the arrogance and presumption of many masters who make their own will the rule of their servants’ obedience. This must be a leaden rule, which may be bent this way, that way and every way, because of the flexible and changeable whims of man. That is why many most vile and horrible things are commanded: because they suit the whim of the one who commands. Absalom ordered his servants to commit a most detestable murder on his own brother, and note how he presses it: “Have I not commanded you+?”501

More presumptuous is the one who takes it on himself to be the502 great commander of all Christians, the pope of Rome, and other masters and governors of Jesuits, friars, monks and other such orders, who command those under their authority to commit treasons, raise rebellions, kill kings and do other such abominable villainies.

And to justify the pope, from whom all inferior governors receive their power and by whose will they must be ruled, papists hold these blasphemous positions about his power: “He can do all things by right, as God. He is as God, having503 on earth fullness of power. If he should draw innumerable souls of men with him into hell, yet no one may say to him, ‘What are you doing?’” Does not the apostle’s504 description of “the man of sin, the son of destruction” fit him of whom these things are said? Is he not, in the papists’ account, “as God … proclaiming himself to be God”? This is further confirmed by the power which they give him of making laws to bind the conscience, and of coining new articles of faith.

Not only popish masters, but profane ones too, go too far in this presumptuous use of their power beyond their power: such as those who order their servants to kill, to steal, to swear, to perjure themselves, to lie, to give false measures and weights, to go to Mass, to profane the Sabbath, and to commit other such sins. In all these, and in all other things like them that are against God’s law, masters have no power to command. They go beyond their commission, and will answer for it accordingly.

§. 9. Of masters commanding servants to do their duty.

2. The exercise of a master’s commanding power consists in the things which God has enjoined as binding duties. These a master must command his servants to do by virtue of his authority: such as to worship God, to sanctify his Sabbath, to be diligent and conscientious in his calling, with other such duties, which God sums up in these words: “the way of the Lord … what is right and just.” And God says that he knew that Abraham would “command … his household” to do them.505

The charge given to masters in the fourth commandment concerning servants (“neither you … nor your manservant or maidservant”)506 proves as much. Josiah507 is commended for making his people stand by the covenant of God. On this ground, if a master has servants who are papists, separatists or profane persons, he ought to command them, and make them go to the holy ordinances of God.

It is one of the chief ends for which God has given power and authority to some over others, that by their authority they may make those who are under them keep the commandments of God, just as inferior officers have authority given them to see that the king’s laws are kept.

§. 10. Of the sin of masters in allowing servants to neglect their duty.

It is contrary to the commanding power which God has given to masters to allow their servants to omit and neglect the binding duties which God has commanded them: as when a servant is so popish or profane that he refuses to go to the word, or the sacrament, or to perform any duty to which he is bound, and his master lets him alone. Though masters do those duties themselves, and though they do not hinder their servants and keep them from doing them, yet if they do not make their servants do them too, they make themselves accessories to their servants’ sin.

Objection. Every man’s conscience is free and cannot be forced; therefore masters must not compel servants to things that are against their conscience.

Answer. Though the conscience is free to a man, and out of another’s power, yet men’s outward actions are not free; and though neither faith, piety, righteousness nor any grace can be forced into men, yet they may be forced to use the means which God has sanctified for breeding and increasing them. Though they cannot be forced to have a religious and righteous heart, yet they may be forced to do religious and righteous duties. Or if a master cannot force these, yet he may and ought to do his utmost; and by this means, though he cannot free his servant from death, yet he will free his own soul from the guilt of his servant’s death.

§. 11. Of a master’s wisdom in ordering things neither commanded nor forbidden.

Equity has respect to the things which are in a master’s power to command or not to command. Such are all secular actions that concern himself, about the particulars of which God has given no direct charge that they must be absolutely performed: as to go on such an errand, to deliver such a message, to carry out such a piece of business. Concerning these things I may say of a master’s power what the apostle says of a parent’s power508 in another case: if he sees fit for it to be done, and commands his servant to do it, “he does well, he does not sin”; if he does not see fit for it to be done, and does not command it, “he also does well, he does not sin.” The centurion509 speaks of this kind of thing where he says that he tells one, “Go,” another, “Come,” and a third, “Do this.”

The mark at which masters must aim in commanding or forbidding these things must be expediency; for “not all things510 that are lawful are expedient.” Expediency depends much on circumstances, and on the consequences which may follow from doing anything; and in observing these the wisdom of the one who has power to have a thing done or not done especially appears. When David did not allow Hushai,511 his good friend and wise counselor, to go with him when he fled from Absalom, but told him to return to the city and stay there, he had an eye to the good consequence that might follow from it. And when Joab commanded the Cushite rather than Ahimaaz to512 carry the news of Absalom’s death, he had an eye to the fitness of the persons, and to the kind of message.

To apply this to our purpose: Equity requires that masters (in commanding things which God neither commands nor forbids, and which they have power to command or not to command) have an eye to their servants’ ability, sex, age, disposition, conscience and other such circumstances, so that the thing which they command is in some measure suited to them: not above their ability, not unbecoming their sex, not unfitting their age, not thwarting their disposition, not against their conscience.

§. 12. Of masters’ offense against expediency.

It is contrary to equity for a master to regard only himself and his own mind, even in things that are lawful. Paul was not513 of this mind when he said that he did not seek his own profit; no, nor did he seek his own will. Expediency and inexpediency were great motives to him to refrain from things which were otherwise lawful. Yet many pay little regard to this, as these instances show:

1. Many command things to the very utmost of their servants’ strength, if not beyond it (as Pharaoh did),514 or else dangerous things, which may bring great injury on them. David515 was touched in heart for moving his servants, by a mere wish, to fetch him water at the risk of their lives.

2. Others, against propriety, put men to do maids’ work, and maids to do men’s work.

3. Others, out of mere partiality, keep old and good servants down, and promote much younger ones over them.

4. Others will forcibly make servants do things against their natural disposition, such as handling, and even eating, things they cannot bear to touch or see.

5. Others in doubtful matters will urge and press them against their conscience.

Although in these and other such things that are not unlawful servants ought to do what they can to satisfy their master’s command, if he is peremptory in urging them, yet a master ought not to be too peremptory in pressing his authority and power.

A Master’s Correcting and Governing of His Household

The Eighth Treatise: Of Masters · §§. 13–20

§. 13. Of the power of masters to correct their servants.

The second point in which a master’s power consists is correction, which may be given by looks, words or deeds.

By a man’s look his anger and wrath against another is shown.516 In Hebrew the same word means a face and wrath, because wrath shows itself soonest in a man’s face. It is noted of Cain that he “became very angry, and his countenance fell”517 (Gen 4:5). Now the showing of a master’s wrath against his servant is a correction.

But words, whether of rebuke or of threatening, show it much more. This phrase which Solomon uses in Prov 29:19 (“a servant will not be corrected by words”) shows that there is a correction by words. And though it is put negatively, it does not imply that correction by words is not to be used with a servant, but rather that, if he is not moved by it, blows must be added to it. This is a correction by deeds, which Christ mentions in the parable of the servants who, according to the greatness of their fault, are518 to be “beaten with many blows.” It is therefore in a master’s power to correct his servant with stripes or blows. This being so, I will show:

  1. How far his power extends in this.
  2. How it is to be ordered.

§. 14. Of the restraint of masters’ power: that it does not reach to their servants’ lives.

Concerning the extent of a master’s power in correcting his servant, this question must be resolved: Whether a master has power to take away his servant’s life for any fault.

Answer. His power does not reach so far, as is evident from these reasons:

1. There is no precept, no approved example, nor any other warrant for it in God’s word. The Jews had great power over servants who were foreigners. From them they might519 buy male and female slaves; they might have them as a possession, and take them as an inheritance for their children after them, to be slaves forever. They might be put to the most toilsome, drudging, base and lowly work they had, such as drawing water, cutting wood and the like; but even so their masters520 had no power over their lives.

2. A master was not allowed to maim his servant: if he struck out an eye or a tooth of his servant unintentionally, he had to make amends, which was to let him go free.521 Much less, therefore, was he allowed to take away his servant’s life.

3. If a servant died under his master’s hand when the master was correcting him, though he did not intend willfully to murder him, that master was to be punished.522 It was not lawful, therefore, for a master knowingly to kill a servant.

4. The power of life belongs only to the public magistrate, who does all things in open, public places, so that there may be many witnesses of his just proceeding. If masters had this power, many might be put to death secretly, and no man would know for what cause, as happens in popish inquisitions.

5. The523 approved laws of men make it willful murder for a master to kill his servant knowingly, however heinous the servant’s fault. Neither the authority of the master nor what the servant deserves will exempt the master who kills his servant from the guilt and punishment of felony.

Objection. In ancient times masters had this power.

Answer. They never had it by right, though some may have exercised it. Among God’s people it was never exercised in any age of the world. The liberty that was taken was among the heathen; and yet among them, as states came to be governed with more524 and more civilized order, that usurped liberty was much restrained by the laws of magistrates; and when emperors and kings became Christians, it was taken away altogether.

Objection. If a man takes an enemy in war, he has power to kill him.

Answer. If he does not kill him in the time of the war, but spares him then, and takes him as a captive and makes him his servant, even though only a slave, he does not have power over his life.

§. 15. Of masters’ excess in correcting servants.

Those act contrary to their just and due power who in their rage stab their servants or otherwise do away with them; and so do those who beat them so mercilessly and immoderately with rod, cudgel or anything else that death follows. For there are many who, having once begun to strike, do not know when to stop, but lay on as if they were striking stocks and blocks, and not their own flesh. God foresaw that masters were prone to such cruelty, and therefore525 set a fixed number of stripes, which no one who beat another was allowed to exceed.

Among these may be counted such reckless masters as in a fit of temper will strike their servant with anything that comes to hand, whether it is heavy, jagged, hard or sharp; they do not care526 (“like a madman shooting firebrands and deadly arrows”). These things may endanger a servant’s life; if not, they may break his head, or otherwise wound, bruise and lame him.

It is beyond a master’s power by any correction to impair the life, health or strength of his servant, or in any way to disable him in his body from doing what he otherwise might have been able to do.

If masters must not take away or endanger the life of their servant, no, not even as punishment for any sin, what may we think of masters who, without any fault in their servants, cause them to be killed by putting them on some desperate venture, either to pursue their own quarrel or for some other unjust end? David527 dealt in this way with Uriah; but afterward he528 bitterly repented of this piece of injustice. At another529 time, when three of his servants had fetched him water that he longed for at the risk of their lives, though they returned safely, yet his heart struck him for his longing, and he would not drink that water, because they had risked their lives to fetch it.

But what may we say of masters who cause their servants, for the masters’ sakes, to commit felony, murder, treason, rebellion and other such things as make the public magistrate unsheathe his sword against them and cut them off? We noted this530 before as a grievous fault because of the unlawfulness of the thing; here we may further note that it is much more heinous because of the harm that follows from it, which is the loss of their servants’ lives. So in this way they make themselves accessories to a detestable sin, and guilty of the blood of their servant.

§. 16. Of masters ordering the correction they give to their servants.

2. So that masters may rightly order the correction which they give to their servants, a difference must be made between the age, sex, disposition and faults of those whom they correct.

1. Masters ought not to be as ready to strike those who are grown in years as the younger sort. Years bring understanding, and a rebuke will make a man of understanding sorrier for a fault, and more concerned to amend it, than blows;531 pain works more on the younger sort. But if, despite their years, they are defiant and will not regard words, their defiance must be beaten down with blows. “Strike a mocker,”532 says the wise man; and again, “Judgments are prepared for mockers, and beatings for the backs of fools.”

Since servants in years must in this case be corrected, it is further needful to make a difference between the kind or measure of correction given to them and to the younger sort. If they are corrected like children, they may either make light of it, or scorn it all the more. “Bruises, wounds and stripes that pierce the inmost parts are a purging medicine against evil”533 for defiant servants of ripe years.

2. If there is a master and a mistress governing a house jointly, it is fittest for the master to correct the menservants, and the mistress the maids.534 Abraham handed his maid over to Sarah in such a case. Yet if a maid should grow defiant and mannish, and turn against her mistress, the mistress being weak, sickly, with child, or otherwise unable to master her maid, the master may and must beat down the maid’s defiance and rebellion:535 so much the law of God permitted.

3. If servants are of a frank and generous disposition, willing and eager to do what belongs to them, sorry when they have committed a fault, and concerned to amend their faults, many things may be passed over in them which must be corrected in others. To this may be applied the counsel of the wise man: “Do not pay attention to every word536 that is spoken.”

4. Correction must be measured according to the greatness537 of the fault punished, and the circumstances by which the fault may justly be made heavier. “That servant who knows his master’s will but does not … follow his instructions will be beaten with many blows. But the one who unknowingly does things worthy of punishment will be beaten with few blows” (Luke 12:47, 48).

Many failings are committed daily contrary to every branch of this direction, in that masters and mistresses, in exercising this part of their power, are carried away by anger, and do what they do in this way “at their own pleasure.” So they turn a duty into sin, and by undue correction of their servants provoke God to correct them in his wrath, either here or in the world to come.

§. 17. Of masters’ power over their servants in and about their marriage.

The third point to be noted concerning a master’s power over his servant is about his servant’s marriage. Various questions are raised on this point, which I will briefly resolve.

1. Question. Do masters have power to order and arrange their servants’ marriages as they please?

Answer. No, not without the free consent of the servants themselves; for marriages must be made with the free consent of the parties who marry.

Objection. The law implies that a master has power to give538 his servant a wife, for it shows whose the children shall be who are born to the servant to whom a master has given a wife.

Answer. 1. That law is to be understood of servants who, being foreigners, were slaves, over whom masters had a more absolute power than over others.

2. The master’s power of giving did not of itself force the servant to marry the person so given, but restrained the servant from marrying anyone other than the one whom the master should give.

2. Question. Is a master’s power in the matter of marriage as great over a servant as a parent’s over a child?

Answer. No. See the reason in Treatise 5, §. 20.

3. Question. May a master deny his servant liberty to marry?

Answer. Yes, for the time for which the servant has contracted to serve his master. For that time a servant is part of his master’s goods and possessions. As slaves were a master’s possession forever, so contracted servants are his possession for the term of their contract. When God gave the devil leave to seize all that Job had,539 by virtue of that permission he seized all kinds of Job’s servants, slave and free, as well as his goods; which he could not have done if Job’s servants had not been counted as his goods.

Yet nevertheless, if servants make it known to their master that necessity requires them to marry, such regard ought to be had for the chastity even of servants that in this case I may use the phrase which the apostle uses in reference to children: “they should get married.”540

4. Question. If servants marry without their masters’ consent, is the marriage made void by it?

Answer. No. The marriage, being otherwise rightly performed,541 remains a firm marriage, though the servants have sinned in doing so, for which their master may justly punish them.

5. Question. May a master keep his servants who have married in this way without his consent from their spouses?

Answer. He may demand the whole time of their contract for service; but the power which husbands and wives have over one another’s bodies by the bond of marriage does not allow542 them to be wholly separated from one another. Besides, our law frees a maidservant from her master’s contract when she is married.

§. 18. Of masters’ severity in forcing marriages on servants, or in separating man and wife.

Those act contrary to a master’s power who force their servants to marry someone they do not like: as when a master has defiled his maid, and to cover his shame he will make one of his servants marry her.

Those also act contrary to their power who seek to separate their married servants from their spouses. Some will keep the man at their house day and night away from his wife, and likewise the wife from her husband. Others will send one of them into remote parts of the land, where the other will not know. Indeed, others will make sure to keep them apart, for they will send one of them overseas for many years on end, if not forever. These practices are against the law of marriage, and too harsh and unjust.

§. 19. Of masters’ power to have the disposal of their servants’ persons.

The fourth point to be noted about a master’s power over his servants concerns their persons, which belong so fully to a master as his own for the time of their service that he may not only keep them himself for his own service, but also pass them on, and give or sell them to another. By God’s law not only543 foreigners but also Jews might be sold as servants.

The customs and statutes of our land also permit masters544 to transfer their servants from one to another, and on their deathbeds to bequeath them to whomever they will, just like their goods and possessions.

So that this power is not abused, masters in exercising it must chiefly regard the good of their servants, and to that end pass them on to fit masters, such masters as may do them good and not evil all the time they stay with them and serve under them.

Contrary to this are those who aim merely at their own advantage, not caring to whom they pass on their servants, so long as they can make a profit by it. Some will sell them, namely when they have them overseas, to Turks and unbelievers; some to papists and other idolaters; some to profane persons; some to cruel, inhuman beasts; some to men of unlawful trades; some to men of no trade. Masters who make their servants over to such as these, or to men like them, will answer for all the wrong that is done to them.

§. 20. Of masters managing their authority well.

The same apostle in545 another place gives this charge to masters: “Give your+ servants what is just and equal.”

By doing these two things masters will manage their authority well.

Justice has respect to the place and service of servants.546

Equity has respect to their mind and manner of doing service.

All servants, in that they are servants and do their masters’ work, must have what by right belongs to servants. This is Justice.

Servants who bear a special love and liking to their masters, doing service not by constraint or with eye-service, as men-pleasers, but in singleness of heart with good will and all good faithfulness, seeking their masters’ good to the utmost of their power, must be respected and dealt with accordingly. This is Equity. As the apostle includes Justice under this phrase (do the same things) (for service, give due recompense), so more especially he includes Equity under it: for servants’ good will and more than usual respect toward their masters, let masters return good will and more than ordinary respect to servants. Saint Peter notes these two virtues under two other words, good547 and gentle: a master’s goodness relates to Justice, his gentleness to Equity.

A Master’s Care for His Servants’ Souls and Bodies

The Eighth Treatise: Of Masters · §§. 21–34

§. 21. Of masters seeking the salvation of their servants.

The justice required of masters concerns the soul, body, and livelihood of their servants.

With respect to the good of their souls, masters must seek the spiritual548 edification of their servants. When Zacchaeus first believed, Christ said, “Salvation has come to this house” (Luke 19:9). Why to this house, rather than to this person, except because he knew that Zacchaeus would do the duty of a good master and seek the salvation of his household? In this, masters must show an impartial regard to all in their house, as the holy fathers did: though they made a difference between the place of children and the condition of servants in matters of temporal goods, yet in serving God, in which eternal happiness is looked for, they provided with equal regard for all the members of their house. The proofs given for servants’ obedience to their masters’ care in this point show that this duty belongs to masters. See Treatise 7, §. 15.

The regard which masters owe to God, to themselves, to their servants, and to the church and commonwealth where they live, requires as much.

1. God has commanded as much (Deut 6:7) as this charge implies: “You shall talk of My laws when you sit at home.” And God has shown his approval of it by commending Abraham for commanding his children and household to keep the way of the Lord, Gen 18:19.

2. Masters themselves reap great benefit from a faithful discharge549 of this duty, and that both by keeping a good conscience toward God (who requires this at their hands, since he has made them prophets and priests in their house, as well as kings, and will require an account from them for all who are under their government), and also by bringing their servants to do more faithful service to them. For there is no such means to stir up servants to do every good duty as the fear of God planted in their hearts. The servant who finds true grace either first worked, or further increased, in him by his master’s means will think himself so indebted to such a master that he will never be able to make any sufficient recompense; and therefore he will strive to do what good service he can by way of thankfulness. He will not only be faithful and diligent in his business, but he will call on God to prosper his service for his master’s good, and to repay the kindness his master has done him.

3. No earthly thing that a master can do for his servant (be it a portion of money, preferment to any place of profit or reputation, or skill in a good trade and calling) can compare with the edifying of a servant in grace.

4. Servants well instructed in piety are the most likely to turn out most profitable, not only to the family, but also to the church and commonwealth where they live.

So that masters may edify their servants the better:

1. They must instruct them daily in the principles of religion550 and all the duties of piety. Wonderful is the profit that will come from a daily and constant use of religious exercises: though only a little time is spent at once, yet much knowledge will be gained by using them often. This duty is all the more necessary because public ministers cannot take such particular notice of every servant in their parishes as masters can in their families.

2. Masters must make their servants go to the public ministry of the word and the worship of God, to be further built up by it and confirmed in their faith. Masters under the law were551 commanded to let their servants eat the Passover, which was a solemn sacrament. The law which requires all males to appear552 before the Lord on the solemn feast days implies that servants also should go. This duty must especially be performed on the Lord’s day, for the charge given to masters in the fourth commandment for sanctifying that day is extended to servants in these words: “neither you, … nor your manservant or maidservant.”

3. Masters, besides instructing servants at home and making them go to church, must take an account of their progress, both by the public and by the private means of edification. Otherwise they will not know how to order their way of instructing them: when to give them milk, and when solid food.

4. To make the means more effectual and profitable, they must add prayer to instruction. Means without God’s blessing on the means are nothing. As they observe any grace worked in their servants, they must be thankful to God for it and pray for its increase.

§. 22. Of masters neglecting to edify their servants.

Contrary to this is the mind and practice of most masters. They think that if they allow their servants sufficient food, lodging and clothing, or wages according to their agreement, they have done all they need to do; and accordingly they do no more. In this they show themselves no better than the heathen. For do not the heathen do the same?

Objection. When the servant was first taken on, no more was agreed in the covenant.

Answer. There are two covenants by which a master stands bound: one with God, the other with his servant. Though his covenant with his servant requires no more than some temporal benefits, yet God’s covenant requires spiritual edification. Many masters are so greedy for their servants’ work that they are unwilling to allow any time, morning or evening, for religious exercises. They think they will thrive by their servants’ labor, and do not think of God’s blessing, which makes rich. Some go so far in this that they keep their servants from the public worship of God, even on the Lord’s day. So it comes about that servants who came to a master ignorant and profane go away, after a long stay with him, just as they came. Many who themselves make some conscience of fearing God fail greatly in this. They do not care to teach their servants the fear of God, and so they deprive themselves of much blessing and pull God’s curse on their persons and houses.

§. 23. Of allowing servants sufficient food.

Since servants do not have bodies of brass or steel,553 but of flesh and blood like all others, masters who have the benefit of their strength and of the ability of their bodies must take care to nourish and cherish them, and that both in health and in sickness.

For preserving servants’ health, regard must be had to their food, clothing, labor, rest.

A due provision of food for servants is commended in Solomon’s554 good housewife, who is said “to provide food for her household”; and in the direction he gives to householders in these words: “Let the milk of your goats be enough for your food, for the food of your household.”

The food masters provide for their servants must be, for quality, good and wholesome; for quantity, sufficient to preserve health and increase strength; for time, given in due season. It is noted that the hired servants of the father of the555 prodigal son had bread (by bread, in the phrase of Scripture, is meant every kind of needful and wholesome food; and it must be taken in that sense there, because it is set against husks, which are not very wholesome and are fitter for swine than for men); indeed, they had bread enough. It is further noted of that good steward who was, as a master, ruler over the household, that556 he gave the household their portion of food in due season.

Question. May servants be stinted in their food?

Answer. As to excess, they may and ought to be stinted, but not as to sufficiency. It is not fitting that all servants should have as much as they can devour, for then many of them would do only a little work. But it is most fitting that everyone should have as much as is needful for strength, so that he may be the better able to do and endure his work. The Greek word translated portion in the557 place quoted before implies as much.

There is a double bond to tie masters to perform this duty: one with respect to themselves, the other with respect to their servants.

Masters themselves will have the profit and benefit of their servants’ health and strength, so for their own sakes it is needful to give them sufficient food. Men who want their work done well by their beasts, or want to be carried well to the end of their journey, will take care that their beasts are well fed.

But besides this, since the health and strength of servants is spent in their masters’ business, justice requires that their health and strength should be repaired and preserved by their masters.

§. 24. Of defect and excess in allowing servants food.

There are two extremes contrary to this duty. One is that of those who are too stingy and (as we say) miserly in giving food to their servants. Sometimes this is in the quantity, when servants’ bellies are pinched too much, even so that their bodies are weakened for lack of food. The prodigal son was served like this.558 These are both unjust and harmful masters: unjust to their servants, harmful to their servants and to themselves too. Sometimes again such miserly masters offend in the quality of the food they give their servants: as when it is kept too long and has grown musty, moldy or otherwise unsavory; or when the worst kind of food is bought for cheapness’ sake, even such as is hardly fit for a man to eat. The greater the abundance of such stuff, the more loathsome it is.

Lastly, though I do not think it fitting to bind masters to set hours for their servants’ meals (Christ allows masters a greater559 liberty in the parable of a master’s treatment of his servant after he came in from his work), yet there may be a fault, as there is in many masters, in keeping their servants too long from food: not allowing them to break off their work for food’s sake, but making them wait for their supper until ten o’clock at night, when they stop work. Surely this cannot be good for the body’s health and strength.

Those masters fall into another extreme who bring up their560 servants too delicately. Solomon has expressly blamed such masters, and notes that this harm is likely to follow from it: “he will have him become his son in the end.” Such a servant will forget his place, scorn to be as a servant, and aspire to be as his master’s child, which is next to being his master’s equal.

§. 25. Of masters’ care about their servants’ clothing.

A wise care for servants’ clothing is also commended in the example of Solomon’s good housewife: “She has no fear of the snow for her household,” that is, for the coldest season in winter, “for all her household are clothed in561 double garments,” that is, with such clothing as is fit for cold weather.562

Fit and decent clothing is both a means of preserving health and also a matter of good report, tending to the reputation of a master. Yet the attitude of many is contrary to this: they do not care how tattered and ragged their servants’ clothing is, so much so that many servants have neither decent nor warm clothing.

This point especially concerns those masters who (as we say) keep their servants and provide everything for them, as in old times masters did for most servants, and as they now do for apprentices, whether male or female. If by agreement servants are to provide their own clothing, masters are not so strictly bound in this; yet they must take care that their servants fall neither into the one extreme of too shabby, slovenly or dirty clothing, nor into the other of too gaudy or too costly clothing. Masters, having authority over their servants, must keep them in order and within good bounds, and have regard to decency in this as in other things. For clothing is one of those outward signs by which the wisdom of masters and mistresses in governing their servants well is shown to the world. If therefore servants are dressed unsuitably for their place and means, all who see them will think that their masters and mistresses are of the same mind as the servants, or at least too lax and careless in their government.

§. 26. Of moderating servants’ labor.

Though labor and pains belong to a servant’s place, yet he may be put to it so hard that the health and strength of his body is impaired by it. For the right ordering of this, therefore, a duty lies on masters, and that in two things especially.

1. That they moderate well the labor they put their servants to, so that they may be able to bear it for the time and to hold out as long as their time of labor is appointed. It was a good reason which Saul gave to keep David from563 entering into combat with Goliath, because (as he thought) David was not able to fight against the Philistine, David being a youth, and Goliath a man of war.

And indeed, if David’s faith had not exceeded the strength of his body, it would have been unfitting to put David to that task.

Those who are put to things beyond their ability are likely to fail and sink under the burden of them.

It is contrary to this to put servants to such hard tasks as impair their strength, endanger their limbs and risk their lives. Saul will rise up in judgment against such masters: for he, supposing that it was too hard a task for David to undertake combat with Goliath, was unwilling to put him to it.564 David failed greatly in this when he gave orders that Uriah should be set “at the front of the fiercest battle,”565 and yet that the commander with his forces should withdraw from him. Many servants, being put to services beyond their strength, either lose their lives by it or (as we say) are never their own men again.

It is also a fault of this kind to put servants to such toiling566 work as is fitter for beasts than for men, or to oppress them with too much work, as the Egyptians oppressed the Israelites. Let God’s hearing the cry of those servants so oppressed,567 and his taking vengeance on their oppressors for it, make all masters beware of the like cruelty.

§. 27. Of providing servants with fit means for their work.

2. The other thing required of masters for the right ordering of their servants’ work is that they provide their servants with things needful and helpful for the work they are put to. Such a mind was commendable in Saul, who tried whether his568 armor might fit David when he was to go against Goliath. To list all the particulars would be an endless task: every separate trade and work has its own means of help belonging to it. This general direction may be enough to move masters to apply it to the particular works and services they put their servants to. Fit means are such a help that what may easily be done with them can hardly, if at all, be done without them.

It is contrary to this to deal with servants as the Egyptians did with the Israelites,569 demanding work from them and not providing them with the means to do it; by which they often demand impossibilities. Some will have much work done at night and not provide candlelight; and so in other particulars.

§. 28. Of allowing servants timely rest.

Breaks, ease and rest from labor at due times are as needful and necessary as food and clothing. The reason God gives for the fourth commandment shows that masters ought to allow their servants rest. It is this: “so that570 your servant may rest.”

Without breaks and rest the body cannot endure labor: it will grow weak and faint, and utterly unable to continue. But as labor wears down strength, so rest repairs it.

There are two special times of rest which servants must not be denied.

  1. The rest of the night.
  2. The rest of the Lord’s day.

The first is common to all living creatures; for it was one571 main reason why God caused the light of the sun to be withdrawn from the face of the earth, that its inhabitants might rest from their labor. “Man572 goes forth to his work and to his labor until evening.” The time of darkness is fitter for rest than for work. Let this not be taken as if no work were to be done after the light of the day is taken away (then only a little work would be done in the depth of winter, when the days are short, and then God would not have provided artificial lights); but it shows that a difference must be made between the day and the night, and that the night is given as the time in which men are chiefly to rest.

The other time of rest (the Sabbath day) is by divine573 institution. It was sanctified for man in the time of Adam’s innocence, when he stood as a public head and stock of all mankind. The very name Sabbath (which in Hebrew means rest) and the express prohibition of doing any work on that day show that it is a day of rest; and that one end of it was for servants to rest in it is evident from the reason given for it: “so that your servant may rest.” So there is a double bond to tie masters to allow their servants rest on the Lord’s day:

  1. The bond of piety to God.
  2. The bond of charity to servants: for since God thought it fitting for servants to rest one day in seven, we ought to574 think that it is needful and helpful for them.

§. 29. Of denying servants timely rest.

Many masters offend against both the times of rest named above: first, those who make their servants stay up too long at night and also rise too early in the morning, not allowing them as much time for rest and sleep as is needful to refresh their bodies and repair their strength. So it comes about that (besides the wrong done to their poor servants) their work cannot be done so well.

Objection. It is said of the good housewife named above that “her575 lamp is not extinguished at night.”

Answer. That is a figurative speech, and somewhat hyperbolic. The word night is put for a part of it. The phrase only implies watchfulness, showing that she is not, like many, in bed early in the evening and up late in the morning, but late to bed and early up. This phrase (“she rises while it is still night”),576 used before, shows that what I have noted is the true sense. If not extinguishing her lamp at night should imply sitting up all night long, how could it be said that she rises?

Question. What time may be thought sufficient to allow for sleep577 to servants?

Answer. As the same quantity of food is not to be measured out too strictly to all alike, so neither is the same length of sleep. Yet experience has shown that for sound and healthy bodies five hours is the least time that may be allowed, and seven hours is time enough for anyone.

§. 30. Of masters’ offense in keeping servants from the rest of the Lord’s day.

2. Against the rest of the Lord’s day far too many masters offend, as follows:

1. By keeping servants at their ordinary work on that day.

2. By sending them here and there on that day on many errands, such as collecting debts, or doing other services which they are unwilling to allow time for on other days.

3. By making it the day of greatest pains and labor, particularly for those who are in the kitchen or have any other business about feasts; for many commonly make the Lord’s day a day of feasting.

4. By keeping them up too late on Saturday nights, even till one o’clock. Many who make some conscience of the Lord’s day are accustomed to offend in this. So that their servants will not work on that day, they keep them up till the very moment they think the Sabbath begins. But in this they commit a double fault: one in not allowing their servants sufficient time for rest; another in making them unfit, through lack of sleep, to do the holy services of the Lord’s day.

§. 31. Of allowing servants time for recreation.

Concerning times of recreation, I may say as the apostle does in another case, “I have no command from the Lord,”578 that is, no express command, to press on the conscience of masters any set time for recreation. Yet without question it is very fitting that servants should have some times to refresh themselves in this way, for recreation rightly used is a great means to put life and add spirit, to youth especially. With work and business not only men’s bodies but their minds also are occupied and employed. In this the labor and toil of men differs from the labor of beasts; and so, for refreshing both body and mind, good and moderate recreation is needful. But I leave this point to the wise consideration of masters themselves, who can discern better by their own observation what is fittest in this respect than we can prescribe.

§. 32. Of masters’ care for their servants in sickness, and after death.

Masters’ provident care for the good of their servants is not to be limited to the time of their health, but must reach to the time of their sickness also, if it pleases God to visit them with sickness while they are in service. Therefore in this case everything needful is to be provided for them by their master: first, spiritual comfort for their soul, so that they may bear their sickness the better; then such things as may give them ease and refreshment for the present, and also, if it pleases God to add his blessing, bring about a recovery. The centurion’s care for his sick servant579 is commended in Scripture: he did as much for his servant as he could have done for his son. The king of Aram’s care580 was also commendable in this respect: he did not hold back from writing to his enemy and making himself beholden to him on behalf of his servant who was leprous.

If the servant has formerly been a faithful, diligent and581 profitable servant, recompense requires as much. For it is a most unkind and inhuman thing to deny help in sickness to the servant who in health has been profitable to his master.

But if a master takes care of his sick servant, giving582 him the best means he can for his ease and recovery, and that servant recovers through God’s blessing on the means, the servant will, if he has any spark of goodness in him, hold himself so obliged to his master that he will never be able to make sufficient recompense, and so he will be moved to use all the diligence he can for his master’s good. So a master may reap benefit for himself by this kind of kindness.

But though the sick servant has in former times583 been unprofitable, and there is little hope of future benefit from him, yet for charity’s sake, and in subjection to God’s ordering providence, by which that servant has fallen sick in his master’s house, he must be well cared for in that case. If the Samaritan584 did well in taking care of an utter stranger outside his own house, because he saw him without help, surely the master who neglects his servant who is sick in his house does very ill.

1. Question. What if he is not a servant hired by agreement for a set term, but a journeyman at will, or one who works by the day or the week?

Answer. Though a master is not so much bound to such a one, yet if he falls sick in his house, the master must not in that case thrust him out of doors, unless he knows where he may be well provided for.

2. Question. What if a master is poor and not able to provide what is needful for a sick person?

Answer. If the sick person has family and relatives who are better able, they must provide for him; if not, the church must help. But it is the master’s duty to make the sick man’s case known to his family or to the church.

If, after all good means are used for his recovery, the servant585 dies, his master must see that the last duty is performed for him in some seemly manner, which is Christian burial. In this lies a main difference between the treatment of men’s bodies, which will be raised again after death, and of beasts, which utterly perish. Jacob’s care in burying his mother’s nurse586 is expressly mentioned by way of commendation.

§. 33. Of neglect of servants in sickness, and when they are dead.

Many masters offend greatly by doing the contrary of the duties named above:

1. Some, when they see their servants beginning to be sick, will put them out of their houses and leave them to fend for themselves, like that cruel Amalekite who left his sick servant587 out in the fields. But note the vengeance of God which followed: leaving that sick servant in that condition was the occasion of the destruction of that master and all his company. By that means they were exposed to their enemies.

2. Others, when they cannot remove their sick servants from their house, will let them lie there without help and perish for lack of things needful. Many rich men who are well able to provide for them will send them to some out-of-the-way back room and take no more care for them. In this respect many of them show more kindness to a dog, or another beast that is unwell, than to a servant. An inhuman thing!

3. Others, who seem not so inhuman, deal too harshly with their servants in such a case. They will give them things needful, but all at the poor servants’ cost; and if those servants do not have ready money, they will take it out of their wages if they recover the health to do them service. Is this “to undo the heavy burdens”? Or is it not to lay burden upon588 burden?

4. Others, who go to some expense over their servants’ sickness, grumble so at their servants and fling out such discontented speeches (namely, that they took them for their work, and not to keep them in their bed; to get something by them, not to be at such cost over them; or that they make themselves sicker than they need to; they could get up if they wanted to; with many other such discontented speeches), that the poor sick servants are more grieved and troubled by their masters’ discontent than by their sickness. And they are often moved to strive beyond their strength to get up, even when death is seizing on them, and so they hasten the approach of death.

5. Many who may be attentive enough to their servants’ bodily condition in sickness589 have no regard at all for the spiritual comfort of their souls. They neither give them a word of comfort themselves, nor send for a minister or anyone else to do it, but let them lie in this respect, and die like beasts. Of all kinds of unmercifulness this is the greatest, and the most unbecoming to Christians.

6. Others, if their servants die, will hardly give them a winding sheet, but say, “Let anyone who will bury them for their clothes.”

These inhuman practices are to be noted, so that the loathing of them may make other masters more tenderhearted toward their servants.

§. 34. Of masters providing for the future livelihood of servants.

Next to the soul and body of servants, masters must have some care for their livelihood; and that not only to keep them while they are in their service, but also to strive and provide that they may support themselves and do good to others. “When shall I provide for my own household?”590 says Jacob to his master. This protest shows that this is a master’s duty.

The mind of most masters is contrary to this: in taking on servants they think of nothing but serving their own ends. So it comes about that when masters die many servants are put to very hard straits: some are forced to beg, others are driven to pilfer and steal. In this way caterpillars are bred to plague the commonwealth.

So that I may not be thought to lay the care of parents on masters, and to make servants equal with children, I will point out four particulars which will show what kind of care and provision is required of masters here with respect to their servants’ livelihood.

One is that masters accustom their servants to taking pains.

Another is that they train them in some useful calling.

A third is that they give them sufficient wages.

A fourth is that after sufficient service they allow them to provide for themselves.

A Master’s Employing and Rewarding of His Servants

The Eighth Treatise: Of Masters · §§. 35–45

§. 35. Of employing servants well.

The charge which the master gave to his servants when he was going away (“Conduct business with this until I return”)591 shows that masters must keep their servants exercised and employed in some business or other. This is also implied in this part of a good mistress’s commendation, “she gives a portion to her maidservants,”592 meaning a portion of work. As there is never an idle member in a natural body, but every one is employed, so it should be in a family.

1. Servants thus accustomed to hard work while they are in subjection will be more industrious when they are their own masters. Indeed, they will both more willingly undertake, and more easily carry through, matters which require pains and diligence. Practice makes perfect.

2. In this way masters themselves, and others afterward, will receive more profit and greater benefit from them.

3. In this way many temptations will be avoided and evils prevented. Continual employment is to the corrupt nature of man like a running stream which carries away all the mud and filth in a brook, so that none settles there.

Contrary to this is sheltering idlers in one’s593 house, giving food and drink and lodging to such as do no work at all. The apostle expressly commanded, “If594 anyone is unwilling to work, he shall not eat.”

These are not only a prey to the devil, but also instruments of the devil, by which he works much harm. For these are the ones most ready to reveal the secrets of a house, to gossip about everything that is done, and to fall in with any company; and, not content to do no good themselves, they disturb, interrupt and draw away those who are busy at their work.

§. 36. Of exercising servants in a calling.

Though it is a good thing to keep a servant always occupied and employed, yet for the servant’s benefit it is further needful that his employment is about some settled matter, in which he may also exercise himself when he has left service. This especially concerns those who have taken595 apprentices. They must teach them their trade.

  1. For that end apprentices are bound to masters.
  2. The terms of the agreement on the master’s part require as much.
  3. The good likely to come from it to the master himself, his apprentice and others will repay the pains.

Other servants also must be kept to a work which may be a means of maintenance: in a great house, to duties about that house; in the country, to farming; in legal offices, to some employment there; and so in other callings.

Contrary to this is masters’ begrudging their apprentices the secrets of their trade, and employing them time after time on messages and errands and such things as serve only the master’s present need, but cannot profit the servants in time to come. These masters are like old, full-grown, broad trees, which keep all the sunshine from the shrubs that grow under them, and so keep them from growing.

Objection. Apprentices will be like ivy to the trees they cling to, soon overtopping them and soaking all the life out of them. They will hinder their master’s trade and draw away all his customers if they become too expert in his trade.

Answer. 1. This is only a surmise. It implies that masters who fear what is alleged do not deal as honestly and faithfully with their customers as they should. Otherwise, how could they suspect that wise buyers would leave one of whom they have had long and good experience to go to a beginner?

2. Daily experience shows that God by his providence so orders men’s affairs that masters who from time to time train up and send out many apprentices, well practiced and skillful in their trade, hold their own, and indeed increase in their own dealings and in the gain they get by them; and yet at the same time their apprentices also do well. Why should any masters so distrust God’s providence as to be afraid to make their apprentices skillful in their trade?

3. When masters give up trading, by death or otherwise, how will trades be continued if masters are so envious and distrustful? What if their own masters had dealt with them like that? And what if all masters dealt like that? For what one does in such a case, he must suppose that all may do.

Those mistresses also offend in this way who keep their maids at drudgery for many years on end, and never teach them, nor give them the means or the leisure to learn the skills of household management, things by which they may earn a better living for themselves.

Such masters and mistresses use their servants like beasts, only to serve their own ends, without any regard for the servants’ good. In this they pervert the main end of the relation between master and servant, which is a mutual and reciprocal good passing from the one to the other.

§. 37. Of appointing to every servant his particular function.

For the better exercising of servants in some calling, let these596 directions be noted.

1. That masters assign their servants their own distinct work, so that they may know what to do and in what to exercise themselves; and if there are many servants in one house, that they set each one his distinct function. The phrase noted before of the good mistress (“she gives a portion to her maidservants”)597 proves this much. This was one thing which the Queen of Sheba observed and marveled at in Solomon’s house, “the standing of598 his servants,” namely, each one in his own place, at his own task. To this purpose it is noted that the great master gave to599 each of his servants their distinct talents. It is like this in the natural body: every member is not only employed, but also employed600 in its own function: the eye in seeing, the ear in hearing, the foot in standing or walking, and so with the rest. It is like this also in Christ’s mystical body: one has the spirit of wisdom, another of knowledge, another of faith, and so with other gifts. So also it ought to be in a family where there are many servants.

This is an especially good means to make each one more diligent and faithful. For when each one has his particular work, they know that they in particular are to give an account of it. So if it is not done, or done badly, they will bear all the blame; if it is done, and done well, they will have all the praise.

2. In this way each one will be made skillful and expert in something, by constantly attending to it and exercising themselves in it.

§. 38. Of disorder in families through masters’ negligence.

The great disorder in many families is contrary to this. For there are many masters who, having a number of servants, expect everything to be done well, and yet appoint no particular place or work to anyone, but think and say that everyone should be eager to do everything. And when they find601 things not done to their liking, they fret and fume, and complain that they keep so many servants and yet nothing is done well. Such servants are not free from all blame; but surely the masters have the greatest cause to complain of themselves, and of their own disorder in governing. For their negligence in appointing no set work to their servants is the cause of everything being neglected. Where many things to be done are left to many servants, one will decline this, and another that, and say it does not belong to them. So the saying proves true that what is spoken to all is spoken to none, and that general charges are no charges.

§. 39. Of masters overseeing the ways of their servants.

The second direction is that masters exercise oversight over their servants, and keep an eye on them, to see how they spend their time and carry out the business committed to them. The good mistress “watches over the ways of her household.”602 This is noted of Boaz, that he went to his field to see what his reapers were doing.

The eye of a master is a great motive to make a servant diligent and faithful; for in this way he knows that both his diligence and his negligence will be seen, and dealt with accordingly. From this arose the proverb, The eye of the master makes the horse603 fat. The notion the wicked servant had, that his master was delaying his coming and so could not see what he did, made him as unfaithful as he was.

If a master’s position allows it, it is needful that he is present with his servants and goes before them as a good example. It is recorded of the good mistress that “she rises … and gives a604 portion to her maidservants”: she is with them herself; she “works with eager hands.” This note of difference is set between a provident, thriving master and a dissolute, careless master: this man says to his servants, You+ go, but that man says, Let us go; or, as it is put, Go, sirs, and Let’s go, sirs.

Contrary to this is letting everything go (as we say) at sixes and605 sevens, and never seeing what servants are doing. How can such masters expect their servants to be diligent in doing what serves their masters’ good, when they themselves are negligent in overseeing what serves their own good? Masters who give themselves to gambling, and to following their pastimes all day long, offend greatly in this. So also do mistresses who spend all the morning lying in bed and dressing themselves (a custom quite contrary to what is noted of the good mistress),606 and at noon, when they come out of their room, scold and quarrel because things are not further along.

§. 40. Of stirring up servants to their duty by both fair and foul means.

The third direction is that masters use what means they can to urge and stir up their servants to be diligent and faithful: in the first place exhortation, admonition, persuasion, promises of reward and other fair means like these. Such were the means which Saul used to make his servants faithful to him, when he said, “Will the son of Jesse give all of you+ fields and vineyards,”607 etc. If what he expected from them had been good, this way of dealing with them would have been commendable. But if fair means do not prevail, then masters may and ought to608 rebuke, threaten and correct their servants.

And if servants are brazen in sinning, and neither fair nor foul means will reclaim them, they must then be thrust out of doors. Note what the master in the parable said to his servant: “You cannot be steward any longer.”609 To the same purpose David also said, “No one who practices deceit shall dwell in my house; no one who tells lies shall stand in my presence.”

When Ishmael became a scoffer at God’s word, Abraham thrust610 him and his mother out of doors, and that by God’s appointment. The parable of cutting down the fruitless fig tree611 may fitly be applied to this point, and the reason also given there (“Why should it take up the ground?”). Why should incorrigible servants take up the room of good servants? Indeed, why should they remain to infect and pervert other servants?

Objection. This is so far from helping servants in their livelihood that it may turn out to be their utter ruin.

Answer. 1. Then they have no one justly to blame but themselves. Justice (in case of necessity) must take its course, though through the wickedness of man some harm follows from it.

2. Carrying this out on some may make others look to themselves more carefully.

3. It may make those who are thrust out more dutiful under another master, or more diligent in another course of life.

Contrary to this is the course of those who keep no course or order612 in drawing their servants on to their duty, but begin with what should come last. Indeed, they use only the remedy which should not be used unless no other remedy will serve, which is to turn their servants out of doors on every small occasion. Many use no instruction, persuasion, admonition, rebuke or threatening when their servants have offended, but only this thunderbolt hurled at them: Be gone, get out of the house. If this were recognized as a fault, servants might stay longer in a house than they ordinarily do, and master and servant might reap more good from each other; and indeed the secrets of a house would be better kept. For it is the frequent chopping and changing of servants that causes everything done privately in houses to be broadcast up and down.

§. 41. Of paying servants their wages.

A third thing required of masters with regard to their servants’ livelihood is to give them their just wages. Even this is included in the general precept, “Pay everyone what you+ owe him,”613 and more particularly expressed in the example of614 the master who hired laborers into his vineyard, and at the end of their work gave every one of them his wages. Indeed, there is an express615 law to this purpose.

Objection. These places concern laborers hired by the day.

Answer. Servants are in the same rank, and the ground for both is the same, for both work for wages. Indeed, the argument follows all the more strongly from the less to the greater, like this: if a laborer and servant for a day must have his wages justly paid, much more a servant and laborer for a year, and for years.

1. A master’s agreement requires as much. If there were no other bond, yet the bond by which he voluntarily binds himself ties him to it in conscience. Among the fruits of the Spirit Saint Paul616 counts “faithfulness,” meaning by it trustworthiness in keeping promises and agreements.

2. Common fairness and justice require as much, for wages are as due for labor as money for goods. Christ takes it as an undeniable principle that “the worker is worthy of his wages”;617 so also does618 the apostle.

This duty is to be performed to those who are hired for wages. Of those who are provided with everything by their master, I spoke619 before.

In giving servants their wages these three things are to be620 observed.

1. That sufficient wages are allowed: at least so much as may serve to provide such necessities as are fitting for a servant. For there is great reason that he who works should live by his work.

2. That they are given in due season.621 God would not have the laborer’s pay left unpaid one night after it was due. The time agreed by a servant for his wages is the due time: he expects it then; he plans his uses of it for that time; at that time therefore it ought to be given him.

3. That they are paid in full, according to the agreement. The master’s promise and the servant’s need require as much.

§. 42. Of masters’ injustice about their servants’ wages.

Injustice contrary to the duty named above, of paying servants their wages duly, is committed in many ways.

1. When masters withhold their servants’ wages altogether:622 this is a crying sin, which enters into the ears of God.

2. When they make their servants ask for their wages again and again, even until they are ashamed; indeed, make them stay and wait for it until they are forced to sigh to God, or else to pilfer and steal to supply their needs. Though these masters have no intention of utterly defrauding their servants of their due, yet putting them off and delaying payment puts servants, who for the most part live only from hand to mouth, into great straits. The Lord knew this well, and therefore623 expressly forbade the withholding of a servant’s wages one night. This therefore is a matter not only of unkindness, but also of injustice.

3. When masters alter and change the agreed wages, and seek to reduce them, as624 hard-hearted Laban did: by this they show that they begrudge their servants’ welfare and seek only themselves.

§. 43. Of allowing servants to provide for themselves.

A fourth thing required of masters with regard to their servants’ livelihood is that, after sufficient service has been done, they allow their servants to provide for themselves. Jacob asked this of Laban as a most fair and reasonable thing, saying, “when shall I also provide for my own household?”625 (as was626 noted before in general). This concerns especially such servants as have been with masters a long time as apprentices, and have spent their time, labor and pains solely and wholly for their masters’ good.627 God’s law made express provision for such, that after certain years of service they should go free. So also do the laws of our land, especially the ordinances of London.

Here lies a main difference between servants (who are under subjection, and kept to work for their own good) and beasts (which are only for man’s service and good).

Those therefore who are of a contrary mind, keeping servants628 as long as they possibly can, even all their lives, at hard labor and in servitude, deserve to be served by beasts rather than by men and women. This severity of masters toward their servants is particularly noted to be629 one of the causes of God’s great indignation against the Jews, by which he was provoked to give them over as captives to their enemies.

§. 44. Of kindness to be shown to good servants.

As masters must give what is just to all servants, so they must give what is630 equal to those who deserve it. That is, they must be of a corresponding mind and disposition toward good, upright, loving, kind, faithful servants, who do not stand so much on what is demanded of them as on what they are able to do for their masters, and for that reason, as they see occasion, often do much more than is demanded or expected. Goodness calls for goodness, good will for good will; and this is to do the same things. This kind of Equity consists in these and similar particulars.

1. Masters must think well of such good servants, and hold them in high regard. Abram631 counted his old, good, faithful servant as his child, and until he had a child thought of making him his heir. Great was the esteem which Potiphar632 had for Joseph, when he raised him from a slave and put him “in charge of his household,” Gen 39:4. Why is the title father given to masters (2 Kgs 5:13), and the title sons to servants (Josh 7:19 and 1 Sam 24:16), but to show that servants should bear a childlike affection toward their masters, and that masters should bear a fatherly affection toward such servants?

2. Masters must take notice of the goodness and kindness of such servants, and show that they do, both by giving them due praise and by a good reward. Both are noted in the pattern of that great master who said, “Well done, good633 and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things.” In this way those good servants will be all the more encouraged to hold on, and others will be moved to imitate them. The apostle gives this encouragement to all under authority: “do what is right, and634 you will have his praise.” This phrase implies that governors ought to praise those who do well.

3. If such servants are accused of some heinous crime, masters must not rashly believe it, but rather sift and examine the matter thoroughly. Here Potiphar635 failed greatly, and by that means lost such a servant as he could never get again. If a good servant happens to slip and commit a fault, his master ought in wisdom either to take no notice of it, or to pass it over with some mild admonition, and not deal with him as with a wicked, graceless servant.

4. When such servants depart (their agreed term having expired), their masters must not send them away “empty-handed,”636 but help them in their marriage (as Moses’ master did)637 or in their setting up in business, as the great master did who put his wise and faithful servant “in charge of all his possessions.”638

§. 45. Of unkind dealing with good servants.

Those who are of a contrary mind are unworthy of good and kind servants; and many masters are of that mind:

1. Some make no difference between servants, but think of bad and good all alike. They think that the best servants only do their duty, and that therefore no extraordinary regard is to be shown them. But it is a point of wisdom at times to count a duty as a kindness, especially when good will of heart is joined with the outward performance of duty.

2. Others think it good policy to take no notice of any servant’s extraordinary faithfulness and diligence, to praise and reward it, for fear it should puff them up too much. But there is much more fear of servants fainting and growing weary of doing good if they have no encouragement, than of their growing insolent through encouragement.

3. Others will be more ready to check and rebuke such servants for every slip, and for failing in anything, than other servants, because the others care less for their rebuke. In this they show a lack of wisdom in managing their authority well.

4. Others, when their servants are about to leave, or to marry, or to set up in business, will look for some occasion or other to fall out with them, on purpose to send them away empty-handed. Many will show a pleasant face to profitable servants until the time of reward comes, and then begin to frown, as Laban639 did. Indeed, some masters are so far from seeking the prosperity of faithful, wise, diligent, skillful servants that they will hinder640 them all they can, and keep them down, fearing that as their servants rise, they themselves will decline and fall. These are both unkind and ungrateful masters. Would masters want to be dealt with like this by their superiors? You ought to live with your inferior as you would have your superior live with you.

A Master’s Own Master in Heaven

The Eighth Treatise: Of Masters · §§. 46–49

§. 46. Of the subjection under which masters are.

Eph 6:9.

Knowing that your+ Master also is in heaven, and there is no respect of persons with Him.

In general the apostle gives only one reason to stir masters up to do their duties, but it is so laid down that it includes other forceful reasons under it.

The principal reason is taken from the subjection masters are under.

The other reasons are taken from the description of the authority under which masters are; for it is such an authority that:

1. In relation to it there is no difference between master and servant.

2. It far surpasses all dignities on earth.

3. It is swayed by no outward respect for anything.

The first reason, which declares the subjection of masters in that they have a master over them, reminds them of the account they are to make, and the reckoning they are to give, of the right use of their authority, and of their conduct toward those who are under them. For they are only like stewards over fellow servants; every one of them therefore will hear this charge: “Give an account of your stewardship.”641

In this respect this reason is both a spur and a curb to masters.

It is a spur, to prick them forward to perform conscientiously all the duties which are required of them; for they have a master who will take notice of it and reward them for it. As they approve and reward the good service which their servants do, so much more will their master approve and reward them if they do well. Do masters therefore expect their servants to perform their duty? Then let them perform theirs, for the same reason holds for both. Let this be applied to all the particular duties mentioned before.

It is also a curb, to restrain masters from doing anything to their servants but what they will be able to justify to their own master. With this curb God held in the Israelites, saying, “You are not to rule over your servant harshly, but you shall fear your God.” Joseph642 was held in by it when he said, “I fear God. So do this and you+ will live.”643 And Nehemiah, when he said, “The governors before me had heavily burdened the people … but I did not do this because of my fear of God.”644 And Job, when he said, “If I have rejected the cause of my manservant … what will I do when God rises to judge? How will I answer when called to account?”645 Think of this, O masters, when you+ are about to demand anything of your+ servants that is not lawful or fitting; when you+ are incensed and stirred up in anger to strike your+ servants unjustly or cruelly; when you+ withhold from them anything that is their due; when you+ lay more on them than they are able to bear; when in any way you+ wrong or oppress them. Think, and say to yourselves+, Can this be justified? How will we be able to hold up our heads before our master when he calls us to account? What stronger motive to do every duty? What stronger restraint from all injustice and severity? The notion many have that they are free, under no one, and to give no account, makes them both negligent of their own duty and insolent toward others; as Pharaoh, who said, “Who is the Lord that I should obey His voice?”646 and Sennacherib, who said, “Will your+ God deliver you+ from my hand?”647 and Nebuchadnezzar, who said, “What god will be able to deliver you+ from my hands?”648 Note the outcome of this insolence. Pharaoh, after many extraordinary plagues laid on him and his people,649 was drowned in the Red Sea with all his army. Sennacherib,650 after his army was destroyed, was killed by his own sons. Nebuchadnezzar651 became a very beast. But to let these abominable blasphemers pass, there are many masters who, though they do not utter such detestable blasphemies with their mouths, yet by their conduct toward their servants show themselves to be little better minded, in that they make their own will a rule for their servants, and will have them do things that are unfitting and unlawful, using their servants as slaves, or rather as beasts. Let all such masters know that they have a master.

§. 47. Of the equality between masters and servants in relation to God.

The second reason, in this particle also (your+ Master also), declares an equality between masters and servants in relation to God. As God is the master of servants, so he is the master of masters also. As servants are the Lord’s freemen,652 so masters are the Lord’s servants. In this respect those who are made rulers, and those who are under them, are called fellow servants.653 For however great the difference between master and servant in outward dignity, yet as the servants of God they are in a like condition, and in many things may be counted equal; especially if both are of the same faith, and so brethren in Christ. This is another spur, and a curb too.

A spur, in that God will accept all the more kindly the goodness which masters do to their servants, because it is done to God’s servants.

A curb, in that servants will be heard before God as well as masters, for he is the master of both.

Many think that all the kindness shown to servants is lost, because servants are so lowly that they are able to make no repayment. But their master is able.

Others think their servants can never take any revenge on them, and so use them as they please. But the master of servants, who is also the master of masters, can take vengeance, even such as will make masters sorely repent all the wrong they have done.

If masters duly weighed this point, that, though there is some difference between them and their servants in outward government, yet before God they are like fellow servants, would they be over-harsh and cruel? Would they not be kind and gentle?

§. 48. Of God’s being in heaven, how it is a motive to stir up masters to have due regard for their servants.

The third reason (taken from the place of God, in heaven) declares the surpassing excellence of that great master who is the common master of masters and of servants, and it gives an edge to the earlier motives.

1. It shows that though the wrong which masters do to their servants is within the walls of their house, so that no mortal eye can see it, yet God who is in heaven sees it; and though servants cannot be admitted into the courts of men to make their complaint, yet heaven is open to them: they may have access to that great master who is in heaven whenever they will. What made the wicked steward654 deal harshly with his fellows was the notion he had of his master’s absence. But those who know that this great master is in heaven can have no such notion of him; for they know655 that as heaven is over every place, so “the eyes of the Lord are in every place, observing the evil and the good”: he sees all the good and all the evil that masters do to their servants.

2. It shows that the kindness which the Lord will repay, and the vengeance which he will inflict, are infinitely greater than the good or evil that masters can do to their servants, even as heaven is higher than earth, and as he who is in heaven is greater than those who are on earth. Do you, therefore, who are a master on earth, gladden or grieve the soul of your servant? God in heaven can much more make your soul glad or sad. Do you therefore desire the favor, or fear the frowns, of your master in heaven? Show favor to your servant on earth, and forbear threatening. Remember the infinite disparity between your mastership and God’s, and this will move you all the more to deal with your servant as you would have God deal with you.

3. It shows that there is much more reason for us to take notice of our servants, of their pains, of their diligence and of their faithfulness, than for God to take notice of ours; and less reason for us to scorn or neglect our servants than for God to scorn or neglect us. For there is a far greater difference between God and us than between us and our servants. We and our656 servants are all of the earth, of the same mold, nature and disposition, subject to the same passions and to the same dissolution. (The heathen observed as much.) But God is in heaven, eternal, unchangeable, in every way surpassingly glorious. How then can we expect to be regarded by this master if we do not regard our servants? O masters, in all your+ dealings with your+ servants, remember your+ Master is in heaven.

§. 49. Of God’s impartial respect toward all.

The fourth reason (in these words, and there is no respect of persons with Him) declares God’s just and equal way of proceeding with all men, of whatever rank and degree. God will do the same things to all sorts of masters that they do to their servants. The apostle calls masters to consider this impartial justice of God, both because of the outward power which they have over their servants, and also because for the most part masters are backed by the power and authority of magistrates on earth, who in matters of dispute between master and servant are ordinarily partial, having more respect for masters than for servants.

But let masters here learn to cast off all such foolish notions and vain hopes. Though they are higher in place, and have more wealth and better connections than their servants, and though men who have carnal eyes may be much moved by this to respect them, yet God will not go a hair’s breadth from justice for the whole world. If the greatest man that ever was in the world had a servant who was the lowliest that ever was, and a case between that master and that servant came before God, God would not lean in the least toward that master more than toward the servant. If the greatest abuse the lowliest, they will not escape. Therefore, O masters, give no just cause of complaint to any servant.

The apostle’s way of setting down these points, marked by this word (knowing), implies that ignorance of God, of the authority which he has over masters, of the equality between masters and servants in relation to God, and of God’s heavenly excellence and impartial respect toward all, makes masters abuse their power, by neglecting all duty, and by lording it over their servants and tyrannizing over them. Therefore you+ who have been ignorant of these points until now, learn them now; and you+ who know them, call them often to mind, and do what becomes good masters, “knowing that your+ Master also is in heaven, and there is no respect of persons with Him.”
