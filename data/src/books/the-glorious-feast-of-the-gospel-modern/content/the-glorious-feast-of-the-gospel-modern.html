To The Reader

So much has been written lately about the times that spiritual discourses are now almost out of season. Men’s minds are so hurried up and down that, we fear, they are far too unsettled to think seriously, as they ought, about their eternal concerns. Alas! Christians have lost much of their communion with Christ and his saints — the heaven upon earth — while they have woefully disputed away the life of religion and the power of godliness and drained the spirit out of them, turning them into dry and sapless controversies about the government of church and state. So, to recover your spiritual relish for savory practical truths, we are publishing these sermons of that excellent man of God, of precious memory. In them you are presented:

  1. With an invitation to a great and wonderful feast, the marriage feast of the Lamb. A feast to wonder at indeed, in which Jesus Christ, the eternal Son of God, is the bridegroom, and where every believer who has “clothed himself with the Lord Jesus” (Rom 13:14), “the wedding clothes” (Matt 22:11), is not only the guest but the spouse of Christ, and the bride at this wedding supper. Here Jesus Christ is the master of the feast, and the food and provision too. He is “the Lamb of God” (John 1:29), “the ram caught in the thicket” (Gen 22:13). He is the “fattened calf” (Luke 15:23). When he was sacrificed, “wisdom killed her beasts” (Prov 9:2). At his death, “the oxen and fattened cattle have been killed” (Matt 22:4). Ἀληθῶς βρῶσις καὶ ἀληθῶς πόσις. His flesh is “real food,” and his blood is “real drink” (John 6:55). And so that you may be fully delighted at this feast, Christ is “a rose of Sharon,” “a lily of the valley” (Song 2:1). He is “a sachet of myrrh” (Song 1:13), “a cluster of henna blossoms” (Song 1:14); his name is “perfume poured out” (Song 1:3), and his love is “more delightful than wine” (Song 1:2). In Christ “everything is ready” (Matt 22:4), for “Christ is all and is in all” (Col 3:11). And great is the feast that Christ makes for believers, for it is the marriage feast that the great King makes “for his son” (Matt 22:2); the great design and aim of the gospel is to exalt the Lord Jesus Christ and to give “Him the name above all names” (Phil 2:9). Great is the company that is invited (Luke 14:16), Jews and Gentiles. God keeps open house: “Come, all you+ who are thirsty” (Isa 55:1), and “whoever desires, let him come and take the water of life freely” (Rev 22:17). Great is the fare that is provided. Every guest here has Asher’s portion, “royal delicacies” and “bread of fatness” (Gen 49:20). Here is wine, all excellent, the best, “wines on the lees well refined” (Isa 25:6). Here are “fat things,” indeed, “fat things full of marrow.” Here is “the hidden manna” (Rev 2:17), “the water of life” (Rev 22:17), and the fruit of “the tree of life in the Paradise of God” (Rev 2:7). All that is at this feast is of the best, indeed, the best of the best. Here is variety and plenty too; here is “bread enough and to spare” (Luke 15:17). The feasts of Caligula and Heliogabalus, who ransacked earth, air, and sea to furnish their tables, were nothing to this. And above all, here is welcome for every hungry, thirsty soul; he who bids you come will bid you welcome; “he will not say, ‘Eat,’ when his heart is not with you” (Prov 23:7). The invitation is free, the preparation great, and the hospitality at this feast, suiting the magnificence of the great King, is full and bountiful. All this is treated at length in these excellent sermons, which are therefore deservedly entitled The Marriage Feast between Christ and his Church. We read of a philosopher who had prepared an excellent treatise on happiness and presented it to a great king, and the king answered him, “Keep your book to yourself; I am not at leisure now.” Here is an excellent treasure put into your hand; do not answer us, “I am not at leisure now.” Oh, do not let Christ stand “knocking at your heart, who will come and sup with you” (Rev 3:20), and bring his food with him. Oh, do not let a “deluded heart” any longer “lead you astray” to “feed on ashes” (Isa 44:20); feed no longer on “the pods the pigs were eating” (Luke 15:16), while you may be filled and satisfied with “bread in your Father’s house” (Luke 15:17).

    But this is not all. If you will kindly read this book through, you will find that many other useful, timely, and excellent subjects are handled in it besides the marriage feast.

  2. Jesus Christ has not only provided a feast, but because he wants all those for whom it is provided to come to it (and only those who believe do come), he takes away the veil of ignorance and unbelief from their hearts. And here you will find that this skillful preacher has excellently discussed what this veil is, how by nature it lies on all, and how it is removed only by the Spirit of Christ. And if the Lord has “destroyed this covering from your heart,” we do not doubt that the truth of this heavenly doctrine will shine comfortingly into your soul.

  3. To make his bounty and mercy appear further in this feast, Jesus Christ has given his guests “the bread of life,” and has secured them from the fear of death. They need not fear. There is no mors in ollâ (death in the pot) at this feast. We may feast without fear. Jesus Christ by his “tasting of death has swallowed it up in victory” (1 Cor 15:54). The feast Christ makes for his people is not like the one Dionysius the tyrant is said to have once made for his flatterer Damocles: he set him at a princely table, but hung a drawn sword by a small thread over his head. But Christ wants us to triumph over the king of fears, who was slain by the death of Christ; and by that death we are delivered from the bondage of the fear of death (Heb 2:14, 15).

    At other feasts in old times they used to have a death’s head served in among the other dishes, to remind them of their mortality in the midst of all their mirth (a practice of the heathen that condemns the ranting jollity of some loose professing Christians in these times). Κατῆλθεν εἰς θάνατον ἀθάνατος, καὶ τῷ θανάτῳ καθεῖλε θάνατον [The immortal went down into death, and by death destroyed death]. But here Christ serves in death’s head, as David did “the head of Goliath” (1 Sam 17:54), the head of a slain and conquered death. Our Samson by his own death “has destroyed death, and has so ransomed us from the power of the grave, and has redeemed us from death” (Hos 13:14), and from the slavish fear of it. All this is handled at length in these following sermons for your comfort and joy, so that you may triumph in his love, through whom you are more than a conqueror.

  4. Because “it is a merry heart that makes a continual feast” (Prov 15:15), and so that this feast might be a feast day indeed to your soul, Christ here promises “to wipe away all tears from the faces of his people” (Isa 25:8). The gospel has comforts enough to gladden the hearts of the saints and people of God. “The light of God’s countenance” will refresh them with “inexpressible and glorious joy” (1 Pet 1:8), in the midst “of the valley of the shadow of death” (Ps 23:4). A truly godly person can weep for his sins, however much the world smiles on him; and however much he is afflicted in the world, yet he can and will “rejoice in the God of his salvation” (Hab 3:18). In these sermons you have this gospel promise sweetly opened and applied, and in them you will find directions on when and for what to mourn and weep, and the blessedness of all true mourners, whose “grief will turn to joy” (John 16:20).

  5. In these sermons you will further find that, though Jesus Christ regards his people highly and welcomes them bountifully, yet they meet with nothing but coarse treatment in the world, which is accustomed to revile them as “fools” and “madmen,” as “seditious rebels,” “troublers of Israel,” “proud and hypocritical persons.” But blessed are those who do not “stumble over the stumbling stone” (Rom 9:32), who wear “the reproaches of Christ as their crown,” and “by doing good … silence the ignorance of foolish men” (1 Pet 2:15). For though the world loads them with all its revilings, “the Spirit of glory … rests on” them (1 Pet 4:14), and in due time he will roll away their reproach, and “bring forth their righteousness like the dawn, their justice like the noonday sun” (Ps 37:6).

  6. And because a Christian here has more in hope than in hand, more in what is to come than in possession, “walks by faith” rather than by sense, and lives by the word of God, and not “on bread alone” (Matt 4:4), you have here, Christian reader, a sweet discourse on the precious promises of Christ, which he has left us here to ease the hunger of the soul till we come to that feast of feasts in heaven. He left them so that by this glimpse we might know in part the greatness of “the glory to be revealed” (1 Pet 5:1), and so that the first fruits might be a pledge of the harvest, and the Spirit, “the pledge” (Eph 1:14), a guarantee of that full reward we will have in heaven, where we will be brimful of those “pleasures” that are at God’s “right hand” forever (Ps 16:11). Christ has given us promises to uphold our faith and hope till faith is perfected in full enjoyment and hope ends in vision, till Jesus Christ, who is here the object of our faith, is the reward of our faith forever.

  7. Now because the comfort of the promises is grounded in the faithfulness of him who has promised, this godly and learned man has strongly asserted the divine authority of the holy Scriptures, proving that they are θεόπνευστοι [breathed out by God], that they are the very word of God, that they are αὐτόπιστοι [worthy of belief in themselves] and ἀξιόπιστοι, worthy of all acceptance and belief for their own sakes: a truth very fitting for these times, as an antidote to guard you against the poisonous errors of blasphemous anti-scripturists.

  8. Lastly, because God often takes a long time to perform the promise, you will find the doctrine of waiting on God excellently handled here: a duty that we earnestly commend to your practice, as suited to these sad times. Say, oh, say with the church, “In the path of Your judgments, O Lord, we have waited for You” (Isa 26:8); and with the prophet, “I will wait for the Lord, who is hiding His face from the house of Jacob. I will look for Him” (Isa 8:17). And rest assured that “none of the descendants of Jacob will seek him in vain” (Isa 45:19); he will not “disappoint their hope, nor make the faces of those who wait for him ashamed” (Isa 49:23).

Thus we have given you a short view of the whole, a brief sum of the treasure these sermons contain. We need say nothing of the author; his former labors “speak sufficiently for him in the gates” (Prov 31:31); his memory is highly honored among the godly and learned. He who enjoys the glory of heaven does not need the praises of men on earth. If anyone should doubt these sermons, as if they were not truly his whose name they bear, let him only observe the style, and the excellent and spiritual matter contained in them, and he will, we hope, be fully satisfied. Besides, there are many ear-witnesses still living who can clear them of any shadow of imposture. They come out without any alteration, except only that some repetitions (which the pulpit bore well) are omitted here. May the Lord make these, and all the other labors of his servants, profitable to his church. And may the Lord so “destroy the veil” from your heart that you may believe, and by faith come to this feast, the joy and comfort of which may swallow up all the slavish fear of death, dry up your tears, and roll away all reproach. And may the Lord give you a waiting heart, to rest your soul on the name of the Lord, to believe his word and his faithful promises, so that in due time you may “rejoice in the God of your salvation.” This is the earnest prayer of

Arthur Jackson.

James Nalton.

Will Taylor.

London, April 19, 1650.

The First Sermon

“On this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make for all people a feast of fat things, a feast of wines on the lees, of fat things full of marrow, of wines on the lees well refined.—Isa 25:6.”

In the previous chapter the holy prophet had spoken of the miseries and desolation of the church in many heavy, sad, and doleful expressions, such as “the vine withers; the earth is defiled by its people, because they have transgressed the laws, overstepped the decrees, and broken the everlasting covenant; therefore a curse has consumed the earth, and those who dwell in it no longer drink wine with a song.” Here you+ see that all sweetness and rejoicing of heart has departed from them. Yet even in the midst of all these miseries God, the God of comforts, makes sweet and gracious promises to his church, to raise it out of its mournful state and condition. And therefore the prophet, in the first part of this chapter, speaks of blessing God for the destruction of his enemies and for his great love for his church. And when he had spoken of the ruin of the enemy, he at once breaks out with thanksgiving, breathing out abundant praises to his God. For it is the custom of holy men, guided by the prompting of the blessed Spirit of God, to praise his name on all occasions, but especially for benefits to his church: not out of ill will at the destruction of the adversaries, but at the execution of divine justice, for the fulfilling of the truth of his promise; as in the first verse of this chapter: “O Lord, You are my God! I will exalt You; I will praise Your name. For You have worked wonders—plans formed long ago—in faithfulness and truth.” When the things that were promised of old were brought to pass, the church was always ready to give God the glory of his truth. Therefore, do not rejoice when your enemies fall; but when the enemies of the Lord are brought to desolation, then we may, indeed we ought to, sing “Hallelujah” to him who lives forever and ever.

I will now come to the very words of my text: “On this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make for all people a feast of fat things.” These words are prophetic, and cannot be perfectly fulfilled all at once, but they will be fulfilled by degrees. The promise of “a new heaven and a new earth” (2 Pet 3:13) will be fulfilled. The conversion of the Jews, and the bringing in of the fullness of the Gentiles, will be brought to pass by degrees. All the promises that God has ever made will be accomplished before the second coming of Christ to judgment. God has made his peace with us in the gospel of peace; and when all these promises are fulfilled, then all imperfection will be done away, and we will never be removed from our Rock, but our joy will then be full. Indeed, even in this life we have some degrees of perfection. We have grace, and the means of grace, the ordinances of Christ, and a testimony of everlasting glory. “On this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make a feast.”

In these words you+ have a glorious and royal feast set down. The place where this feast is to be kept is “Mount Zion”; the feast-maker is “the Lord of Hosts”; the parties invited are “all people”; what comes of it, and the provision for the feast, are “fat things” and “wine” of the best: a feast of the best of the best, a feast of the fat and of the marrow, a feast of “wine on the lees well refined.”

Here you+ may see that God veils heavenly things under earthly things, and stoops so low that he enters the inner man by way of the outward man. For our powers of understanding are so weak and narrow that we cannot become acquainted with spiritual things except by the inward working of the Spirit of the Almighty.

This “mountain” is the place where this feast is made, namely “Mount Zion,” which is a type and figure of the church, called in Scripture “the holy mountain.” For as mountains are raised high above the earth, so the church of God is raised in excellence and dignity above all sorts of mankind.

Observation 1. The church is raised as far above all men as men are above beasts. This mountain is above all mountains. “The mountain of the Lord” is above every other mountain. “You, O mountain, will stand immovable,” when all other mountains will smoke if they are only touched. This is the mountain of mountains. The church of God is most excellent in glory and dignity, as you+ may see at the end of the previous chapter, where the glory of the church outshines every other glory: “The moon,” says the prophet, “will be confounded and the sun will be ashamed; for the Lord of Hosts will reign on Mount Zion and in Jerusalem, and before His elders with great glory.” So the brightness of the church will outshine the glory of the sun and of the moon. Thus you+ see that the church of God is a mountain.

Reason. First, because God has established it on a stronger foundation than all the rest of the world. It is founded on the goodness and power and truth of God. Mountains of bronze and iron are not so firm as this mountain. For what sustains the church but the word of God? And being built on his word and truth, it may very well be called a mountain, for it will be like Mount Zion, which will never be removed (Ps 125:1). It may be moved, but never removed. So, because of its firmness and stability, it may rightly be called a mountain.

Observation 2. Again, we may here speak in some sort of the visibility of the church. But here the papists will raise a quarrel. When they hear of this mount, they at once apply it to their church. Their church, they say, is a mount; so says the Scripture.

I answer, first, we grant that in some sort their church is a mount (though not this mount), for Babylon is built on seven hills; but if this proves her a church, it is an antichristian church. Secondly, the universal Protestant church has always had a being, though at times invisible. The apostle, writing to the Romans, exhorts them “not to be high-minded, but to fear”; for, he says, “if God has broken off the natural branches, perhaps he will break you+ off also” (Rom 11:20, 21). And indeed, for their pride and haughtiness of mind, they are broken off at this day. Christ, who “walks among the seven golden lampstands” (Rev 2:1), never said that the church of Smyrna or Ephesus would always remain a visible church to the eyes of the world, and they did not; for to this very day they lie under bondage and slavery to the Turks. The mount has always been visible, though not always equally gloriously visible. For there will be a time when the church will flee into the wilderness (Rev 12:6). Where, then, will the glorious visibility of the church be? There is a time when all will follow the beast. The papists themselves confess that in Antichrist’s time the church will scarcely be visible. The essence of a thing and the quality of a thing may differ. The church is a church, and visible, but not always equally and alike gloriously visible. Yet those who had spiritual eyes, and looked on things through the spectacles of the Scripture, could always declare that the church was visible. For from the beginning of the world the church has always had luster enough to delight and draw the elect, and so it will have to the end of the world, though at times the church may have a mist before it, as Augustine says: “It is no wonder that you cannot see a mountain, for you have no eyes.” But the papists have seen this mountain. As they have always been bloody persecutors of the church, they have seen enough to put them to shame. For we have nothing in our church that they do not have as well; only ours is refined and freed from idolatry. We have two sacraments, they have seven. We have Scripture, they have traditions, which they make equal with it. We have the Scriptures pure, they corrupt. So our church was in the midst of theirs, as a sound and purer part in a corrupt body.

This mountain is the church. “The Lamb” is “standing on Mount Zion, and with Him 144,000 who had … His Father’s name written on their foreheads” (Rev 14:1). Christ stands in the church, and standing on Mount Zion he is accompanied by those whom his Father gave him before the world was. Therefore those who belong to this holy mountain are Christ’s. “And on this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make for all people a feast.” And this feast is a royal feast, a marriage feast, in which the joy and comfort of God’s people are set out by what is most comforting among men. The founder of the feast is “the Lord of Hosts.” It is only he who is able to prepare a table in the wilderness, who is mighty and able to feast his church with a spiritual and holy banquet. We all live at his table for the feeding of our bodies, but much more for our souls. He can make a feast for the whole man, for he is Lord of the conscience; and he is the one to spread a table for the whole world. Indeed, more: if there were so many, he could furnish a table for ten thousand worlds. He is the God of all spiritual comforts, and “the God of all comfort.” He is infinite and can never be drawn dry, for he is the fountain of eternal life. All graces and comforts in Scripture are called the comforts and graces of the Holy Spirit, because God is the giver of them by his Spirit. Who can take away the wound of a guilty conscience but he who has set the conscience in the hearts of men? If he pleases, he can take away the burden of a grieved conscience and give it new and solid comforts in its place. He knows all the windings and turnings of the soul, where all the pain and grief lies; and he cannot fail to know it, because he alone is above the soul. He is therefore the fittest to make the soul a feast. He alone can do it, and he will do it.

““On this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make a feast.” Why is he called “the Lord of Hosts”?”

It is a usual term to set forth the glory of God, to make his power and the greatness of his majesty known among the children of men.

““He will make for all people a feast.””

Those who are invited to this glorious feast are “all people.” None excepted, none excluded, who will come in to Christ! Some of all sorts, of all nations, of all languages! This relates to the time of the gospel. The church at first had its being in particular families, but afterward was more enlarged. The church at the first was of the daughters of men and the sons of God. The children of the church mingled with a generation of corrupt persons who would keep within no bounds; but after Abraham’s time there was another generation of the church, so that it was a little more enlarged. Then there was a third generation, a divided generation, made up of Jews and Gentiles. So when Christ came into the world, the bounds of the church began to enlarge more and more, so that now it is in this happy condition: “Come to Me, all you+ who are weary and burdened” (Matt 11:28). Both Jews and Gentiles, all are invited, whoever they are; “nothing is now unclean” (Acts 10:15). Christ has come, and has made “for all people a feast of fat things.” It must be a feast, and of fat things, for all the world will be the better for it. The Jews will be converted, and the fullness of the Gentiles will come in. And yet it is no harm to any particular man, because the things you+ are to taste are spiritual. Go to all the good things in the world: the more one has of them, the less another must have, because they are earthly, and so finite. But in spiritual things all may have the whole, and every man in particular. Every man enjoys the light of the sun in particular, and all enjoy it too. So the whole church, and only the church, enjoys the benefit and comfort of this feast; but under the name of this church come all the elect, both Jews and Gentiles. And therefore it must be the Lord of Hosts who can make such a feast as this, a feast for all people. No other is able to do it.

This feast is “a feast of fat things, full of marrow, and of wine on the lees well refined,” the best that can be imagined, the best of the best. A feast is promised, a spiritual feast. The special graces and favors of God are compared to a feast made up of the best things, full of all varieties and excellences; and the chief dish, which is all in all, is Christ, and all the gracious benefits we can in any way expect from him by promise. All other favors and blessings, whatever they are, are only Christ dished out, if I may put it that way, in various offices and attributes. He is the source of comfort, the principle of grace and holiness. All is included in Christ. Ask him and you+ will receive, even the forgiveness of your+ sins, peace of conscience, and the communion of saints. Ask Christ, as one invested with all privileges for the good of others. But this is through his death. He is the feast itself. He is dished out into promises. Do you+ have a promise of the pardon of sins? It is from Christ.

Would you have peace of conscience? It is from Christ. Justification and redemption? It is from Christ. The love of God is conveyed to us by Christ; indeed, all the good that we have is only Christ parceled out.

“Now I will show why Christ, with his benefits, prerogatives, graces, and comforts, is compared to a feast.”

First. In respect of the choiceness of the things. In a feast all things are of the best; so are the things we have in Christ. Whatever favors we have by Christ, they are choice ones. They are the best of everything. Pardon for sin is a pardon of pardon. The title we have to heaven through him is a sure title. The joy we have by him is the joy of all joys. The liberty and freedom from sin that he purchased for us by his death is perfect freedom. The riches of grace we have by him are the only lasting and durable riches. Take anything you+ can: if we have it by Christ, it is of the best. All worldly excellences and honors are only shadows compared with the high excellences and honor we have in Christ. No joy, no comfort, no peace, no riches, no inheritance can be compared with the joy, peace, and inheritance that we have in Christ. Whatever we have by him, we have it in a glorious manner. And therefore he is compared to fat, to “fat things full of marrow,” to “wine,” to “wine on the lees,” which preserves its freshness; the best wine of all, which is not changed from vessel to vessel, but keeps its strength. And indeed, the strength and vigor of all flows from Jesus Christ in covenant with us.

“The love of Christ is the best love, and he himself is incomparably the best, and has favors and blessings of the choicest kind.”

Second. Again, as in a feast there is variety besides choiceness, so in Christ there is variety to answer all our needs. Are we foolish? He is wisdom. Do we have guilt in our consciences? He is righteousness, and this righteousness is imputed to us. Are we defiled? He is sanctification. Are we in misery? He is our redemption. If there are a thousand kinds of evils in us, there are a thousand ways to remedy them by Jesus Christ. Therefore the good things we have by Christ are compared to all the benefits we have in this world. In Christ is choice and variety. Are we weak? He is food to feed us, so that we may be strong. He will refresh us. He is the best of foods. He is marrow. So, are our spirits faint? He is wine. Thus we have in Christ what supplies all our needs. He is variety.

There is a plant among the Indians called coquus [the coco palm]; its fruit serves for food and drink, to comfort and refresh the body. It yields that from which the people make clothing to clothe themselves, and also what is medicinal, very good against the disorders of the body. And if God will infuse so much power into a poor plant, what power may we expect to be in Christ himself? He feeds our souls to all eternity, puts on us the robes of righteousness, and heals the disorders of our souls. There is variety in him for all our needs whatever. He is food, medicine, and clothing to clothe us; and when we are clothed with him, we may stand with boldness before the majesty of God. He is all in all. He is variety, and all. There is something in Christ to answer all the necessities of God’s people, and not only so, but to their full contentment in everything.

Third. Again, as there is variety in a feast, so there is sufficiency, full sufficiency. “We have seen … the one and only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth” (John 1:14). And being full of grace, he is wise and able to furnish this heavenly banquet with enough of all sorts of provisions fit for the soul to feed on. There is abundance of grace, and excellence, and sufficiency in Christ. And it must be so, because he is a Savior of God’s own sending. “Do not work for food that perishes, but for the food … which the Son of God will give you+. For on Him God the Father has placed His seal” (John 6:27); that is, he is sent out for this purpose, to “feed the church of God” (Acts 20:28). As there is an all-sufficiency in God, so there is in Christ, who by the sacrifice of himself was able to give satisfaction to divine justice. Therefore he says, “My flesh is real food, and My blood is real drink” (John 6:55); that is, spiritually, to the soul, he is real food, and can satisfy God’s justice. If we consider him as God alone, he is “a consuming fire” (Heb 12:29); or as man alone, he can do nothing; but considered as God-man, he is real food and real drink. And now the soul is content with what divine justice is content with. Though our consciences are large, yet God is larger and above our consciences. Therefore, as there is variety of excellence, so there is sufficiency and fullness in Christ. What he did, he did to the full. He is a Savior, and he fills up that name to the full. His pardon for sin is a full pardon; his merits for us are full merits; his satisfaction to divine justice is a full satisfaction; his redemption of our souls and bodies is a full redemption. Thus all he did was full.

Fourth. A feast is for company. It is convivium [a living together]. There is conversation at it. So Cicero prefers the Latin name convivium to the Greek name συμπόσιον [a drinking together]. And this feast is not for one. We are all invited to it. The excellence of Christ’s feast consists in the communion of saints; for the spirits of all who take part in it must agree with one another. Love is the best and chief dish at this feast. The more we partake of the sweetness of Christ, the more we love one another. Christ by his Spirit so works in the hearts of the children of men that, if a thousand are brought together from a thousand different nations, within a little while you+ will have them all acquainted with one another. If they are good, there is agreement of spirit, and a sympathy between them. There is a kinship in Christ. He is the true Isaac. The death of Christ and the blood of Christ are the ground of all union and joy and comfort of every kind. The blood of Christ sprinkled on the conscience will bring that peace of conscience which will be a continual feast to the soul. This feast must be wonderfully comforting, for we do not feast with those who are like ourselves, but we feast with God the Father, and the Holy Spirit, sent by Christ, procured by the death of Christ. The angels attend us at this feast; therefore it must be joyful. No joy is comparable to the joy of a feast. This is not just any feast. This is a marriage feast, at which we are betrothed to Christ. Now of all feasts, marriage feasts are the most sumptuous. This is a marriage feast for the King’s Son, for Christ himself; and therefore of necessity it must be full of all choice varieties, of the sweetest things, of the most excellent things, and of the quintessence of things. Here is all the joy that belongs to a feast. Here it is, to be had with Christ. What acquaintance can be more glorious than the one between Jesus Christ and a Christian soul? When we have hope of better things to come, then we find the sweetness of this communion. No harmony in the world can be so sweet as the harmony kept between Christ and the soul. When we have this, and are made one with God in Christ, our joy must then be inexpressible. Once the contract is made between the soul and Christ, there cannot fail to be abundant joy. When the soul is joined with Christ by faith, it cannot help finding its solace in a perpetual jubilee and a perpetual feast, in some degree.

Fifth. Again, for a feast you+ have the choicest garments, as at the marriage of the Lamb, “fine linen, bright and pure” (Rev 19:8), which is the righteousness of the saints. When God sees these robes on us and the Spirit of Christ in us, then there is a robe of righteousness imputed, and a garment of holiness, with which our souls are clothed. So this is a feast that must have wonderfully glorious attire; and when this marriage is consummated, we are sure to have a garment of glory put on us.

Sixth. This was signified in old times among the Jews.

  1. In the feast of the Passover (not to name all the resemblances, but only one or two). The lamb for the Passover, you+ know, was chosen out of the flock from among the rest four days before the time appointed for that feast. So Christ is the true Paschal Lamb, chosen by God before the foundation of the world was laid, to be slain for us.

  2. Again, manna was a type of Christ. It came from heaven to feed the hungry bodies of the Israelites in the wilderness. In the same way Christ came, sent from God the Father, to be the eternal food and upholder of the souls and bodies of every one of us. Manna was white and sweet; so is Christ, white in righteousness and holiness, and also sweet to delight the soul. Manna fell on the tents in the night; and Christ came when darkness was spread over all the world. God gave manna freely from heaven; so Christ was a free gift, and he freely gave himself to death, even to the cursed death of the cross, for us. All, both poor and rich, gathered manna. Christ is a common food for king and subject. All share in Christ. Neither Jew nor Gentile is shut out, but all may come and buy freely without money. Of this manna, he who had least had enough. So here, he who has least of Christ will still have enough, though he takes him with a trembling hand, for Christ is his. Whoever has the least grace, if it is true and sound, has grace enough to bring him to eternal life. The Jews wondered at the manna, saying, “What is it?” So one of Christ’s names is “Wonderful” (Isa 9:6). Grace and favor from Christ is true spiritual manna to the soul. Manna fell in the wilderness: even so we must remain in the wilderness of this wretched world until we come to heaven. Christ is manna to us, and very sweet as he is conveyed to us in his word and sacraments. When the Israelites came into the land of Canaan the manna ceased, not before. So when we come to heaven, the elect’s purchased possession, we will have another kind of manna for our souls. We will not feed on Christ there as in the sacrament; no, we will see him “face to face, and know as we are known” (1 Cor 13:12). In the wilderness of this world it is fitting for God to convey this heavenly manna to the soul in whatever way he pleases. Manna could not fall until the Israelites had used up all the provision they brought with them out of Egypt; and we cannot taste that heavenly manna of our Father until our souls are drawn away from all dependence on the world and from carnal delights.

    Then, indeed, manna will be sweet and precious. What is this heavenly manna, what are Christ and his Father, what are the word and sacraments, to a depraved, vicious heart, stuffed full of earthly vanities? Alas! it loathes all these. As none tasted manna but those who came out of Egypt, so none will taste Christ but those who are not of the world, who have come out of Egypt, out of sin and darkness. Manna fell only around the tents of Israel, and in no other part of the world but there, so that none might have the privilege of eating it but God’s own special, chosen people. Christ falls on the tents of the righteous, and none will taste this blessed spiritual food but those who are the Israel of God, who are of the church, who feel the burden of sin and groan under it. Oh! the very taste of this heavenly manna is sweet to their souls, and to none but them. Thus you+ see the feast that Christ makes for us on Mount Zion, and that this manna typifies Christ with all his benefits.

  3. Again, the hard rock in the wilderness, when it was struck with the rod of Moses, at once gushed out water in abundance, which preserved the life of the Israelites. So Christ, the rock of our salvation, the strength of his church, the rock and fortress of all his saints: when his precious side was gored with the bloody lance on the cross, the blood gushed out in such a manner and such abundance that by its shedding our souls are preserved alive. He is both manna and the rock of water. Manna had all in it, so had the rock; and all necessities are plentifully supplied by Christ. The church of God has always had bread to satisfy spiritual hunger. It never lacked necessary comforts. It is said (Rev 12:6), “When the church fled into the wilderness, God fed her there,” alluding to the children of Israel fed by manna. The Jews did not lack in the wilderness, nor has the church of God ever lacked comfort, even in the midst of the persecution and oppression of all her enemies. When Elijah was in the wilderness, he was fed (1 Kgs 17:4, 6). The church of God will be fed not only in her body but in her soul, for Christ has hidden manna for his elect. This typifies the exceeding joy of the church, the hidden manna, “what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no heart has imagined” (1 Cor 2:9); no heart of man can conceive of those joys that the church of God will have when the marriage is consummated. Joy in the Holy Spirit and peace of conscience are hidden from the world, and sometimes from God’s people themselves, though they will enjoy them hereafter.

  4. All the former feasts in times past were only types of this. The Feast of Tabernacles, the feast of the Passover, the spiritual manna, and all other holy feasts were only to signify and show forth this feast by Christ. But there is this difference between the type and the thing signified. In the type, the Passover lamb was quite eaten up; but this Passover, Christ, who was slain for sin, can never be eaten up. We feed on him with our souls. He cannot be consumed like the Passover lamb, or like manna, which was gone when the sun rose. Indeed, the manna that was laid up for a remembrance before the ark came to nothing, but Christ is in heaven forevermore for the soul to feed on. Though these were resemblances, yet they failed, as it is fitting that resemblances should fail, that is, come short of the body of the thing itself. Thus you+ see that the spiritual comforts of a Christian may well and fitly be compared to a feast.

  5. Thus you+ see that God provides a feast and invites all. In the sacrament you+ have a feast, a feast of varieties, not only bread, but wine—to show the variety and fullness of comfort in Christ. He intends full comfort. As for our adversaries the papists, they have dry feasts. They give the people the bread, but the wine they keep for themselves. But God in Christ intends full comfort for us. Whatever Christ did, it was full. His merits are complete, and his joy was full. He is fullness itself; and therefore whatever comes from him must be, as he himself is, both full and sweet. He intends full consolation for us.

Use. Therefore we ought to be prepared to partake of this feast in such a way that we may have full joy and full comfort; for there is enough in Christ to satisfy all the hungry souls in the world, and he himself is present at this heavenly banquet. All fullness dwells in him (Col 1:19), from which “we have all received grace upon grace.” Therefore,

Use 1. Let us labor to have large hearts: for as our faith grows more and more, so we will carry away more comfort and more strength from this holy feast. As with the poor widow: if her vessels had not failed, the oil would not have ceased; if there had been more vessels, there would have been more oil (2 Kgs 4:6). Our souls are like these vessels. Let us therefore labor, and make it our great business, to have large souls, souls able to drink in this spiritual oil of gladness; for we will carry as much comfort away from Christ as we bring faith to him. Because the favors of God in Christ are infinite, the more we draw from him, the more glory we give him. But if they were finite, we would offend his bounty; he might soon be drawn dry, and so send us away with a comfortless answer, that he was not able to relieve us. But Christ is infinite, and the more we have from him, the more we may have. “Whoever has will be given more” (Matt 13:12). The more often we go to Christ, the more honor and glory we bring him. This is a banquet to the full.

We have now come to the banquet, and Christ is the founder of it; indeed, he is the feast itself. He is the author of it, and he it is on whom we feed.

Use 2. Let us labor not to be narrow receivers of the sacrament, but to suck in abundance from Christ with a great deal of delight, so that we may come together not for the worse but for the better, considering how great a measure of strength and grace is required as very necessary for maintaining spiritual life.

The Second Sermon

“On this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make for all people a feast of fat things, a feast of wines on the lees, of fat things full of marrow, of wines on the lees well refined.—Isa 25:6.”

I have shown that Christ and his benefits are compared to a feast, and in what respects they are fitly likened to a feast, and have urged that we should prepare for it, first by getting large hearts. Now, in the second place, if we are to have comfort at this feast, we must labor for a spiritual appetite; for to what end and purpose is a man at a feast who has no stomach? I will therefore show what means we are to use to get eager stomachs and holy appetites for this feast.

(1.) The appetite is raised with sour things, such as anguish of spirit and mournfulness of heart for sin. If we are ever to relish Christ rightly, we must labor to have a lively sense of our sins. We must do as the Jews did at the Passover. They ate it with sour herbs, so that they might have keener stomachs. So must we. We must cast our eyes into our own hearts and consider what vile wretches we are, how full of sin and vanity; and this will be like the sour herbs with the Paschal Lamb. We must join the sweet benefits and privileges that we have in Christ with the consideration of our own wretched and miserable condition, and then this heavenly ordinance cannot help being sweet and comforting to our souls. I beg you+, enter into your+ own souls, and consider seriously what guilt you+ lie under, and this will whet your+ appetite. “A full stomach despises the honeycomb” (Prov 27:7); but in this appetite there is a sense of emptiness, and from that sense of emptiness pain, and from pain an earnest desire for satisfaction. So it is in spiritual things. We need Christ, and all the spiritual comforts that flow from him. There is an emptiness in us, and we see a need every day to feed on the mercies of God in Christ. There is an emptiness in our souls, and there must be a sense of that emptiness, and pain from that sense, which must stir up a strong effort to pursue what we desire. Then Christ is indeed sweet, when we find our souls hungering and thirsting after him.

(2.) Again, if we want the appetite of spirit that is fit for this feast, we must purge our souls from the corruptions of flesh and spirit, “perfecting holiness in the fear of God” (2 Cor 7:1). We must cleanse our souls from the lusts and passions that daily cling to them. All undigested matter must be taken away, so that the edge of the stomach is not blunted: for while these earthly, carnal corruptions lie on the soul, we can expect no spiritual appetite for heavenly things. Let us therefore examine ourselves to see what filth lies on our souls and what corrupt inclinations are there, so that they may be purged, and our desires may be carried fully after Christ in the sacrament.

(3.) Another means to get an appetite is to consider thoroughly what is required of a Christian to keep up the trade of Christianity well. It is a different kind of thing from what we take it for, to keep up communion with God, to perform holy duties in a holy manner, to bear the yoke as a Christian should. A great deal of strength is required here; and because corruptions will mix themselves into our best performances, there must be a great deal of mercy from God to pardon them. And where does all this come from but from the death of our blessed Savior Jesus Christ? For his sake, God has a forbearing eye. Now, if we consider what a degree of spiritual strength and vigor we should have to go through with these duties, this would sharpen our stomachs and spiritual appetites to furnish ourselves with grace from Christ to go through with these holy services. There must be an exercise of all the duties of Christianity, which is a state that must be kept up at a great deal of cost and labor. A man can do no service acceptable to God except by grace; and grace must feed the soul with fruitful knowledge in the power of faith. And when the soul feels a necessity of grace, oh! then, beloved, it hungers and earnestly thirsts after the love of God in Christ. Every trade needs a great deal of knowledge. Then surely the calling of Christianity needs a great deal. A Christian must expect much both in prosperity and adversity, as the apostle says, “I have learned to be in need and to abound, to be in honor and to be in disgrace, and I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me” (Phil 4:12). Now, because so much goes out for the upkeep of Christianity, we must also bring in much grace, and faith, and love, and holiness, or else we will never be able to uphold this condition. Where there is an exercise of Christianity, there will be an appetite for heaven; that is our best calling. For when we have done all that we can, what we must have comfort from is Christianity. Therefore, labor with all labor to be holy and able Christians. All other callings are only for this present life; but the one that is for eternity is this calling of Christianity. And this is only to fit us here in this world for an everlasting condition of glory in the world to come.

(4.) Again, if we would have a desire and appetite for heavenly things, we must labor to get acquainted, and constantly keep company, with those who are good. The old proverb is, “Company will make a man start eating,” especially the company of those who are better than ourselves. Out of sheer emulation, men will do as others do. When men live among those whose hearts are framed this way, they must keep level with them. Keeping company with those who have a good relish for spiritual things, and show forth grace in their lives, sets an appetite on our desires, to desire the same things that they do. So St Paul writes to the Gentiles to stir up the emulation of the Jews. Therefore take this also as a means of getting a spiritual appetite. To go on.

(5.) The next thing that may stir up our desires to get an appetite for the best things is to consider seriously that we cannot tell how long we have to live, or how long we may enjoy the benefit of the means of grace. Those who sit at table and talk away the greatest part of dinner time need to eat all the faster at the end, the more they have neglected eating before. We cannot tell how long we may enjoy this spiritual feast that God makes for us. Therefore, be stirred up to get spiritual appetites; for we do not know how long God will spread a table for us. We do not know how long we will enjoy our lives; and if we are suddenly overtaken, we may suffer a spiritual famine, a famine of the soul, if we have nothing beforehand to comfort us; and of all famines, a spiritual famine is the most grievous, the most fearful. Therefore do as Joseph did, and be wise. In the seven years of plenty he gathered for the seven years of famine that were to come on the land of Egypt, Gen 41:36. Alas! if we have nothing laid up beforehand, what will our end be? We will lie open to God’s wrath and anger. Nothing can support our souls in the evil time. Therefore, if you+ want comfort on that day from the things you+ will most need, labor to get a good appetite. For to perish and starve at a feast is a shame; to famish in the liberty of the gospel and plenty of spiritual food is shameful and dishonorable. So you+ see, beloved, not to enlarge on the point, how you+ may get an appetite fit for such a holy feast: first, by getting a sense of sin; secondly, by seeing a necessity of Christ; thirdly, by purging out the lusts that lie on the soul; fourthly, by keeping company with those who are spiritually minded; and lastly, by considering the time to come.

Use 3. It is not enough to have a stomach, but we must have a spiritual disposition of soul toward heavenly things, as we have toward outward things. Labor to have a taste for good things, and a taste that distinguishes heavenly things from other things. God is the God of nature, and has given us five senses; and as he has given us sense to perceive, so he has provided the creatures with varieties of excellences, suited to all our several senses. He does not want objects in the creatures without a sense, or a sense in man without objects. He has given man senses, and a variety of senses, and given fit and proportionate objects for those senses. The soul also has her sense. Wherever there is life, there is sense. God, having given spiritual life to the soul, maintains that life with spiritual food. In a feast there is sight, and the eye is fed there not only with rich furnishings but with a variety of dainties; the ear likewise and the smell are satisfied, the one with music, the other with sweet scents. So in this feast there is something to delight both the ear and the smell of the soul: the one with hearing the gracious promises of Jesus Christ, and the other in receiving the sweet fragrance of that sacrifice that was offered up once for all. Nothing is so sweet to the soul as the blessings of Christ. He is sweet in the word, as the vessel that conveys him into our souls. So you+ see that in this feast all the senses, the sight, the smell, the taste, and hearing, are satisfied, and great care is taken in the provision for the feast that our outward man may be pleased. And will the Lord of Hosts make a feast, and not content the whole man? He is for our sight, if we have spiritual eyes to see; for the ear, if we have ears to hear. All the senses are exercised here. Why is it that carnal men cannot relish a pardon for sin, and justification, and sanctification, and holiness, or go boldly to God? They may have good, sweet notions of these, but they have no spiritual taste or relish of them, and all because they lack spiritual life. None but a Christian can have a spiritual taste that matches a spiritual life. Taste is a kind of feeling, one of the most necessary senses; and a Christian cannot be without relish and feeling. Indeed, it is the very being of a Christian to have a taste of spiritual things. Of all the senses, taste applies itself most closely to its object. The other senses fetch their objects from far off; but taste comes close up to its object, and therefore it is the most necessary. Every life is maintained by taste. “Taste and see that the Lord is good” (Ps 34:8).

Now, taste does two things: it relishes what is good, and dislikes the contrary. There must be a spiritual taste to discern differences. There can be no spiritual taste unless it knows what is good and profitable for the soul, and what is not. Because God does not want our tastes wronged, you+ see what course he takes. First, the eye sees what things we taste, and if the eye is displeased, so also is the smell. So God lays spiritual things before us, the knowledge of good and bad, and gives us many warnings, and all because he does not want us to taste things hurtful to the soul, or poison instead of food. Now, when we have tasted what is good, let us take care that it is not only a taste, so that we do not fall into the sin against the Holy Spirit.

Use 4. Again, besides taste, we must digest what we taste, and digest it thoroughly, in our understandings. When we grasp that a thing is true and good, it must be digested thoroughly into the affections. Love for the best things must be above every other love; yet this must be digested. Men often have sweet notions, but, alas! they are only notions; they do not digest them into their affections. It is the last digestion that nourishes; and when any spiritual truths are understood thoroughly, then spiritual strength comes in; and with that the soul comes and sucks in the power that nourishes it. So it is in the soul: with digestion comes nourishment.

Again, there must be a faculty to retain what we have received, so that it may be digested. Many love to hear, but they do not digest. If there is nothing in the soul, nothing can be drawn out; and therefore we must learn to retain necessary truths, so that when the occasion comes they may pass from the memory into the heart. Though indeed they are not in their proper place when they are only in the memory, yet if they are there, they can easily be brought down into the soul.

Use 5. Then we must labor to walk in the strength of spiritual things. For what is the use of this feast but to cherish both soul and spirit? The use of the spiritual things we have through Christ is to cherish and enliven. It gives us strength, so that we may walk in the strength of Christ, as Elijah walked forty days in the strength of his food, 1 Kgs 19:8. And consider that, though in our consciences and conditions we go through a variety of changes, yet in Christ we have several comforts suited to all our several conditions. If our sins trouble us, we should watch over ourselves, so that we are not cast down too much, but feed on spiritual things by considering the pardon for sin in the blood of Christ. This is the great issue of all that Christ has traced out in the forgiveness of sins. He is not, he cannot be, divided. Where he pardons sins, he sanctifies; where he sanctifies, he writes his law in their hearts. So there is a chain of spiritual favors. Where the first link is, all the rest follow. Where forgiveness of sin is, there is the Spirit, and that Spirit sanctifies, and comforts, and is a down payment of everlasting life. Therefore, feed especially on the favors of God, and get forgiveness of sins, and then all the rest of the chain of grace and spiritual life will follow.

Sometimes we stand in need of present grace and comfort, and we are undone if comforts and grace are not at hand, never considering the promises that are to come, such as that promise of Christ, “I will be with you to the end of the world; do not fear” (Matt 28:20). No temptation will befall us but we will have a way out of it, and it will work together for the good of all those who fear God. This is aqua vitae [water of life] to the soul of man. Therefore we should always remember to get the gracious promises of Christ and his Holy Spirit into our souls; for when all other comforts fail, then the comforts of the Spirit come in, who will be with us and uphold us in all extremities. If we had nothing in this world to comfort our spirits, yet let us rejoice in hope of the glory to come. “Our life is hidden with Christ” (Col 3:3). We have “the hidden manna” (Rev 2:17). “In him we rejoice in hope of glory” (Rom 5:2). And the way to maintain a Christian, holy life is to make use of all the privileges of Christianity, and of the promises that convey these privileges to our souls.

Now, so that we may do this better, let us observe continually what it is that hinders us from feeding on spiritual things as we should. Whatever it is, we must labor constantly to remove it.

Now, what must follow after this feast? Why! spiritual cheerfulness.

If we find this in our Christian duties, it is a sign we have fed on spiritual things. The nature of a spiritual feast is to empty the soul of sin, and to fill it full of gracious thoughts and actions. In place of that, it moderates all things. It makes us use the world as if we did not use it. When we can do this, we may certainly know that our souls have tasted abundant benefit from this feast.

A man who has no spiritual joy is usually drowned in the pleasures of the world, drowned in riches and honors; and these are like strong drink taken without measure, which, instead of cheering the spirits, exhausts and kills them. He who has the joy of heaven here by faith is mortified to all other base delights; “he sets his mind only on the things above, where Christ is” (Col 3:1). And therefore the exhortation, or rather command, “Seek the things that are above,” has this promise fittingly attached to it, “and then all other things will be cast in upon you+” (Matt 6:33): riches and honors in the world; and if not those, at least as much as is necessary, and the mortification of our sins and the lusts of the flesh.

Again, if we have fed on spiritual things for our souls, we will be thankful. When the man who has tasted how good and gracious the Lord has been to him in this world, and how full of joy and comfort he will be to him in another world, considers this, his soul cannot help being thankful to God.

Here we see how to make this spiritual food that Christ provides for us fit for our souls. And if there is such joy as we have said there is in spiritual things, what further use should we make of them but to labor from this to justify the ways of godliness against our own false and carnal hearts, and against the slanderous charges of the world? When our hearts are ready to be false to us, and hanker after the satisfactions of the world, and are ready to say that the best contentment they can enjoy is in the things below, let us answer our base and false arguing hearts, that the ways of wisdom, the ways that God directs us to, they alone are the ways of pleasure. And religion is what makes the hearts of the children of men joyful; and “only a good conscience makes a continual feast” (Prov 15:15), as long as a man lives. But especially at the hour of death, when all the comforts of the world cease, then conscience stands as our friend.

Objection. But the world’s objection is that, of all kinds of men in the world, those who profess religion are the most melancholy.

Answer. But if it is so, it is because they are not religious enough. Their sins are continually before their eyes. They have pardon for sin, and freedom from the guilt of sin, but do not know it. They have good things, and do not know them. And so, with regard to spiritual comforts, God’s people may have spiritual joy and inward consolation, and yet not know it. There may be a time when they are sad and droop, and that is when they think God does not look kindly on them. But the true mark of a Christian is to be cheerful, and no one else can be truly cheerful and joyful. Joy is usurped by others. There is no comfort in them that can be called real. All the joy of a carnal man is only, as it were, the joy of a traitor. He may come to the sacraments, and feast with the rest of God’s people, but what mirth or joy can he have as long as the Master of the feast frowns on him? Where Christ is not, there God is not reconciled. There is no joy like the joy of the man who is assured of the love of God in Christ. A man may sometimes, through ignorance, lack the joy that belongs to him. “Rejoice, you+ righteous, and be glad” (Ps 33:1). It belongs to those who are in Christ and to the righteous to rejoice, for joy is all their portion. They alone can justify the ways of God against every reproach. But the eyes of carnal men are so held in blindness that they can see no joy, no comfort in this course. It is said of Augustine that before his conversion he was afraid to become a Christian in earnest, for fear he would have to go without all the joys and pleasures that the world then offered him; but after he was converted, he could cry, “Lord, I have stayed away from you too long,” and too long delayed coming in to taste the sweetness of Jesus Christ.

Take a Christian at his worst, and he is better than another man taken at his best. The worst condition of God’s children far surpasses the very best condition of people without grace. In the end, things will turn out for the good of the man who is a member of Christ, a child of God, an heir of heaven. The evil of evils is taken away from him. Take him at his worst, and he is an heir of heaven; but take the wicked man at his best, and he is not a child of God. He is a stranger to God, like a branch cut off, and as miserable a wretch as Belshazzar ever was in the midst of his cups, trembling and quaking with fear and astonishment when he saw the writing on the wall (Dan 5:24). When a man sees the wrath of God hanging over his head, then even if he were at the greatest feast in the world, among people full of mirth and jollity, the sight of vengeance ready to seize him cannot help dampening all his joy and all his carnal pleasures. And so only a Christian has a true title to this feast.

I beg you+, let us labor earnestly to have our part and portion in the things above. But what are those to do who do not yet see that they have any share in Jesus Christ? Why! let them not be discouraged, for all are compelled to come into this feast, both blind and lame. The servants are sent to bring them in. God invites the most wretched people of all. All who are aware of their sins are called to come in to this feast; and God requires that awareness of us, or else we can have no appetite to taste this feast. God says, “Come, all” (Isa 55:1). Yes, but, says the poor sinful soul, I have no grace at all! Why! come all the same, “buy without money”; the feast is free. “God’s thoughts are not as your thoughts are”; “but as heaven is high above the earth, so his thoughts are above your thoughts” (Isa 55:8, 9). Poor wretch! You think you have led a wicked life, and so you have! Yes, but now come in; God has invited you, and he will not always be inviting you. Therefore come in, and study the excellences of Christ. When people like these see that they need mercy, and grace, and reconciliation, and must either have it or else be damned forever, then they are earnest to seek the favor and love of God in Christ; then they rouse themselves to get peace of conscience and joy in the Holy Spirit; then they see that salvation is founded only on Christ, and see all the other excellences belonging to Christianity. And so such a man always goes well provided with grace and holiness in this life, so that he may not lose his part in glory in the life to come. Think of this and pray for it, as they did in the gospel: “Lord, always give us this bread” (John 6:34). Here is hope that you may be saved, because you are invited to come in. What is the ministry of the gospel for, if not to plead with you to be reconciled? Oh! let this work on our souls when we hear of the excellences of these things! And along with them, let us consider how necessary it is for us to obtain them, and that we must have them or else be damned eternally. We must do as the lepers did, who said to one another, “Why just sit here until we die? If we say, ‘Let us go into the city,’ we will die there from the famine in the city; but if we sit here, we will also die.” Now, what did they do? “They said to one another, Let us go over to the camp of the Arameans; there is food to feed us” (2 Kgs 7:3, 4). So the soul says, If I go into the city of the world, I will be starved there; if I sit still, I will also perish. What should I do now? I will venture on Jesus Christ; he has food that endures to eternal life, and if I perish there, I perish. If I do not have Christ I must die; the wrath of God hangs over my head, and I cannot escape. Alas! poor soul, now that you see your wretchedness, cast yourself on him, and come in. If you venture, you can only die! So venture; throw yourself on God’s mercy, for he is gracious and full of compassion.

Let those who have given themselves up to Christ seek to honor God and Christ by taking the comforts that are allotted to them. When any man invites us to a feast, he knows that if we respect him we will eat heartily. God has bestowed his Son on us, and will he not with him give us all things? So let us not dishonor the bounty of our good God, but come in, and labor to have our hearts more and more enlarged as we consider the excellence of these eternal comforts. The fullness of Christ is able to satisfy the soul, though it were a thousand times larger than it is. If it were possible for us to get the capacity of angels, it would still not be enough to show the fullness of pleasures that are provided for a Christian. So let us labor with all our strength to open our hearts to receive these joys, for we cannot honor God more than by receiving thankfully from his bounty what he freely offers. To taste plentifully, in the covenant of grace, of these riches, and joy, and hope of things to come, glorious above all that we are able to think of: this, I say, is the way to honor God under the gospel of hope. With things that are infinite, the more we take, the more we may take, and the more we honor him who gives. So let us look deeply into our particular sins; there is no danger of despair. Think of all your needs, and of all your sins; however many they are, there is more to be had in Christ than you can lack. The soul that thinks itself full of needs is the richest soul, and the soul that sees no lack at all, no need of grace or Christ, is always sent away empty. So grieve for your sins, and then rejoice that you have grieved, and go to God for the supply of all your needs. The seeds of joy and of comfort are sown in tears and grief in this world; but we know that we will reap in joy in the world to come.

Remember this: we have we know not what to go through in this valley of tears. That speech of Barzillai was good and excellent, who, being invited to the court by David himself, answered, “I have now grown old, I am not fit for the court, for my senses are decayed and gone” (2 Sam 19:35). Even so the time will come when our sense for relishing earthly pleasures will be utterly lost. We are sure to go to our graves, and we do not know what particular trouble we may meet with and go through in this world, if we live to a full age. Alas! what are all the comforts here to the comforts of eternity? When our days on earth are spent, then comes the eternity of pleasure or everlasting sorrow. Oh then, if, when we leave all behind us, we have the joy of the Holy Spirit in our hearts, it will lift us above all the suggestions of sin or Satan, and bring us cheerfully up to the judgment seat of Christ. Labor therefore to have a spiritual relish of soul, to grow in grace and the comforts of the Holy Spirit; for the time will come when we will wish that we had had more than we have. Everyone will repent of looseness and slackness in the ways of holiness. Therefore let us labor earnestly to be good stewards for our souls for the time to come.

The Third Sermon

“On this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make for all people a feast. And He will destroy on this mountain the face of the covering cast over all people, and the veil that is spread over all nations.—Isa 25:6, 7.”

I have spoken before of the feast that God makes for his church, especially in the latter times. It was especially fulfilled at the first coming of Christ, when the Gentiles came in; but the consummation and perfection of all will be at the day of judgment. Then God will spread a table for his own to all eternity.

We have spoken before at length of how spiritual good things are set out by this comparison of a feast. God sets out spiritual things by outward ones, because we cannot grasp them any other way; the best things in grace by the best and sweetest things in nature. And so God enters into our souls through our senses, as we see in the sacrament.

But we have spoken at length of this. Our care must be to have a special taste, a spiritual appetite to relish this feast that God provides. By nature we are without taste for it. We do not relish spiritual and heavenly things; we do not savor the things of God. And the Spirit of God must alter our savor and taste, as he does. Wherever there is spiritual life, there is a spiritual relish of heavenly truths.

Now let me add this further: though the feast is made by God, yet we must bring something to it. Christ feasts with us, as you+ have it in Rev 3:20. He sups with us. It is not that we have grace from ourselves, or can bring anything; he brings his own provision with him when he sups with us. Yet by the covenant of grace, by which he enters into terms of friendship with us, we must sup with him; we must have grace to welcome him, though it is at his own cost. Yet we must have something. He does not require us to pay our debts, but he gives us the means to pay them. Secretly he bids us come, but he gives a secret messenger to draw us; he certainly sends his Spirit. Certainly he will have us bring something when we come to feast, but it is of his own giving. What we are to bring is humble and empty souls, taking delight in the sense of our unworthiness; and the spirit of faith to believe his promises. It pleases him when we can honor him with a spirit of faith, and then with a spirit of love, and with new obedience springing from a spirit of faith and love. These are the things Christ requires us to have. Our souls must be supplied with these things, so that Christ may delight to dwell with us. And therefore it is a good thing to plead earnestly with God: “Lord, I desire that you would dwell in me, and prepare my soul as a fit temple”; grant me the graces you delight in, and delight to dwell in. So we may beg God for his Holy Spirit to supply our souls, so that he may dwell and delight in us.

But we have spoken at length of the former verse. I will now speak of the next one that follows.

“And He will destroy on this mountain the face of the covering cast over all people, and the veil that is spread over all nations. He will swallow up death in victory, and the Lord will wipe away tears from all faces, and the rebukes of His people He will take away from the earth; for the Lord has spoken it.”

These depend on one another, being the several courses of the feast. He promises a feast in the sixth verse. And what are its several courses? He will destroy on this mountain, this church, the face of the covering cast over all people. He will take away the veil of ignorance and unbelief, so that they may have a special sight of heavenly things. Without that sight they cannot relish heavenly things; they can take no joy at this feast.

And then, because there can be no feast where the greatest enemy is in force and power, he swallows up death in victory. Death keeps us in fear all our lifetime. That which swallows up all kings and monarchs, the terror of the world, death, will be and is swallowed up by our head, Christ, and will be swallowed up by us in victory. In the meantime we are subject to many sorrows which cause tears; for tears are only drops that fall from that cloud of sorrow. And sorrow we always have in this world, either from our sins or our miseries, or from sympathy, in tears of that kind. Well, the time will come when tears will be wiped away, and the cause of tears: all sorrow for our own sins, for our own misery, and for sympathizing with the times in which we live. Our time for this will be hereafter, at the day of the resurrection, when all tears will be wiped from our eyes. God will perform that office of a mother, to wipe the children’s eyes, or of a nurse, to take away all cause of grief whatever; otherwise it could not be a perfect feast.

Yes, but there are reproaches cast on religion and on religious people! Religion goes under a veil of reproach, and the best things are not seen in their own colors; nor are the worst things. They go under masks here.

But the time will come when the rebukes of his people will be taken away. The good things, as they are best, so will they be known to be so; and sin and base courses, as they are bad and as they come from hell, so they will be known to be. Everything will appear in its own colors; things will not go masked any longer. And what is the seal of all this? The seal of it is, “The mouth of the Lord has spoken it.” Truth itself has spoken it, and therefore it must necessarily be. Jehovah, who can give being to all things, has said it. We have heard why the church is called a mountain. He will destroy, or swallow up, as the word may signify, the face of covering, or the covering of the face; the veil, which is the covering of the face, and which that term always particularly expresses; the veil that is spread over all nations.

God will take away the spiritual veil that covers the souls of his people, which is between them and divine truths. It alludes to Exodus 34:34, 35, to Moses when he came down from the mount. He had a veil, for the people could not look at him. He had a glory put on his face, so that they could not look on him with a direct eye; and therefore he had to put a veil over his face, to show that the Jews could not see, as Paul interprets it (2 Cor 3:15): “To this day,” he says, “when Moses is read, a veil covers their hearts.” They could not see that “the law was a guardian to lead to Christ” (Gal 3:24), the ceremonial law and the moral law. God had a blessed end, by the curse of the law, to bring them to Christ. They rested in the veil; their sight ended at the veil; they could not see through to its end and purpose. Nevertheless, when they turn to the Lord, the veil will be taken away.

  1. From the words, consider first of all that by nature there is a veil of ignorance on the soul.
  2. Secondly, God takes away his veil; and only God by his Spirit can do it.
  3. Thirdly, this is only in his church. And where this veil of ignorance is taken off, there is feasting with God, and spiritual joy, and delight of the best kind; and where it is not taken off, there is none of it. First of all, by nature there is a veil of covering over all men’s spirits. To understand this better, let us explain the word “veil” a little. There is a veil either on the things themselves that are to be seen, or on the soul that should see them.

(1.) The veil on the things themselves is when they are hidden altogether, or in part; when we know part and are ignorant of part. And this veil on the things arises from the weak grasp of them: when they are not set out in clear expressions, but in obscure words or in types; when we see them only in types or obscure phrases, which sometimes hide the sight of the thing itself. The manner of speech sometimes casts a veil on things. For our Savior Christ spoke in parables, which were like the cloud, dark on the one side and light on the other: dark toward the Egyptians, light toward the Israelites. So some expressions of Scripture have a light side, which only the godly see, and a dark side, which other men of good intelligence, such as natural men, do not see.

(2.) Again, there is a veil on the soul and on the sight. If the things are veiled, or the sight is veiled, there is no sight. Now the soul is veiled when we are ignorant and unbelieving: when we are ignorant of what is spoken and revealed, or when we know its terms and yet do not believe it.

(3.) Now, this veil of ignorance and unbelief continues in all unregenerate men until grace takes away the veil. Besides, before a thing can be seen, not only must the object be made clear, and the eyesight too, but there must be lumen deferens, a light to carry the object to the eye. If that is lacking, we cannot see. So the Egyptians, in the three days of darkness, had their eyes, but they lacked light to show them the object, and therefore they could not go near one another. It is the light, and not the sight. If there is sight and no light to carry and convey the object, we cannot say there is sight. What answers to this veil is the veil of Scripture, by which heavenly things are set out in a mystery. A mystery is when something is openly shown and something hidden.

Things concealed, as in the sacrament, are mysteries. We see the bread, we see the wine, but under the bread and wine other things are meant: the breaking of the body of Christ and the shedding of his blood, and in these the love and mercy of God in Christ, in giving him to death for us, and Christ’s love in giving himself to satisfy divine justice. These are the things meant, which only the soul sees and grasps. And so all things in the church are indeed mysteries: the incarnation of Christ; the union of both natures; that Christ should save the world in such a way as he did; that he should bring us to glory by shame, to life by death, to blessing and happiness by being a curse for us. It is a mystery to bring opposite out of opposite: that so glorious a person as God should be covered with our weak and sinful nature. It was a mystery; the Jews stumbled at it. Light came, and the darkness could not comprehend the light. And as Christ was a mystery himself, so the church is a mystery. That God should delight so much in a company of poor men, the refuse of the world, to make them temples of his Holy Spirit and heirs of heaven, men who were scorned by the world: this is a mystery. So everything is mystical: the head, the members, the body, the church, and every particular point of religion. There is a mystery in repentance. No man knows what sorrow for sin is except the truly gracious person. No man knows what it is to believe except the man who has a heart to believe. No man knows what peace of conscience and joy of the Holy Spirit are except those who feel them. So that is a mystery. And therefore “the mystery of godliness is great,” says the apostle (1 Tim 3:16). Not only the points themselves, but even the practice of religion is a mystery too. Repentance and faith, and new obedience and love, and the comforts of religion, are all mysteries. There is a veil over them in all these points, so that a carnal man cannot see them.

You+ see, then, in what sense there is a veil on the things, and in what sense there is a veil on men’s hearts: that is, either the things themselves are hidden, or, if the things are open, men lack the sight and light of knowledge, and they lack faith to believe. Beloved, we live in times when the object is clear to us; the things themselves are made clear. Who does not know what Christ is, and the notion of the incarnation, and of union with him? We know them in notion. They are opened and revealed to us very clearly, all the articles of faith and mysteries of religion, so that there is no obscurity in the object. The things are clear, especially in these places of knowledge. But yet, for all that, there is a veil on the soul. The soul of every man who is not graciously worked on by the Spirit of God has a veil of ignorance and unbelief.

First of all, of ignorance. There is a veil of ignorance in many, and in all men by nature a veil of ignorance of spiritual things. For unless they are revealed, they can never be known even by the angels themselves. The angels themselves do not know the gospel until it is opened, and therefore they are continually students of it; and the best men in the world know nothing in the gospel beyond what is revealed. But there is a veil of ignorance on those who know these things in notion only, because they do not know them as they should know them. They do not know them in propria specie [in their own proper form], spiritual and heavenly things as spiritual and heavenly things. They do not know spiritual things as spiritual things; they have a human knowledge of spiritual things. Those who lack grace know the grammar of the Scripture and of divinity, and they know how to reason as the schoolmen do, from one thing to another, and to argue. They know the logic and rhetoric of the Scripture, but they get stuck in the style. There is something they are ignorant of; that is, they do not have an eye of knowledge, as we call it. They do not see the things themselves, but only see things by another person’s spirit, and they have no light of their own. And so no man by nature, except the children of God, knows what original sin is, what corruption of nature is, nor knows sin in its own odious colors, as filthy and as dangerous as it is. That it draws the curse and vengeance of God on it is not known except by the Spirit revealing the odiousness of sin, so that the soul may see it as Christ did when he suffered for it, and as God does. A gracious man sees it as God sees it, because by the Spirit of God he sees its filthiness and odiousness, and the danger it brings with it.

Second. And so in any points of religion, a man by nature does not see them spiritually, as they are and as God sees them, but he sees them by a human light. He sees heavenly things by a human light, in notion only, and only to talk of them. He does not see intuitively into the things themselves. He sees them sub aliena specie, under another representation than their own. Only a godly man sees spiritual things as the Spirit of God sees them, and sees them as they are: he knows sin as it is, knows grace as it is, and knows faith, what it is to believe, what it is to have peace of conscience and the pardon of sins. He knows these things in some sense intuitively, though not as he will when he sees these things in heaven, when he sees face to face. There is a great difference in it. He sees them intuitively compared with the knowledge of other men, though he sees only as in a glass compared with the knowledge he will have in heaven. As St Paul says, “For we see but as in a glass.” But he who sees in a glass sees more life than he who sees the dead picture of a man. So, though we see heavenly things only in a glass, yet we see them better than those who see them in a dead notion. Though it is nothing to the knowledge we will have in heaven, yet it is incomparably above the knowledge of any carnal, natural man on the earth.

Third. Again, by nature men have veils of ignorance over the most divine things: over spiritual things, such as union, and the communion between Christ and us, and the mystery of regeneration in the new creature, such as joy in the Holy Spirit and inward peace of conscience. I will not name the particulars to dwell on them, but give you+ only an instance. Though they know the notion of these things, yet they are altogether ignorant of them. Their knowledge is merely an outward light. The gracious man’s is a light rooted in the soul. It is not like the light of the moon, which receives light from the sun, but it is a light rooted and incorporated into the soul by the Spirit, as the light of the sun is. It is in the soul. It is not only on the soul, but in the soul. The heart sees and feels and knows divine truths. There is a power and force in the sight and knowledge of a gracious man; there is none in the knowledge of a carnal man. The light of a candle has light in it, but no force at all goes with it; but the light of the sun and the light of the stars have a special power: they have heat with them, and they have an influence of a special kind on the bodies below, working together with the light. So it is with heavenly understanding and knowledge. It actually conveys light. But with the light there is a blessed and gracious influence; there is heat and efficacy with that light. But though a carnal man knows the whole body of divinity, yet it is merely a light without heat, a light without influence. It is not from experience. A blind man, if he is a scholar, can talk of colors and describe them better than one who has his eyes but is not a scholar. But he who has his eyes can judge of colors a great deal better. Often a scholar can tell you+ about foreign countries from books better than one who has traveled, yet the traveler who has been there can tell of them more distinctly. So one who is experienced in that kind of work, though a stranger, can measure another man’s ground better than the owner himself. He can tell you+ there are so many acres here. But he who possesses them knows their goodness and their worth, and cultivates them for his own good. And so it is with many. They can measure the points of religion, and define and divide them. Yes, but the poor Christian can taste them, feel them, relish them and put them to use. His knowledge is a knowledge with a share in the things, but other men’s knowledge is a knowledge with no share or experience at all. So there is by nature a veil of ignorance on the heart of every natural man.

Christianity is a mystery. Until conversion there is a mystery in every point of religion. None know what repentance is except a repentant sinner. All the books in the world cannot inform the heart what sin is or what sorrow is. A sick man knows what a disease is better than all the physicians, for he feels it. No man knows what faith is except the true believer. There is a mystery also in love. Godliness is called a mystery, not only for its notional part, but for its practical part. Why do men not take more delight in the transcendent things of religion, which may enrapture angels? Alas! there is a veil over their souls, so that they do not know them, or do not know them by experience. They have no taste or feeling of them.

And so there is a veil of unbelief. There is no man without grace who truly believes what he knows; he believes only in general, he believes things only so far as they do not cross his lusts. But when particular truths are pressed on a carnal man, his lusts overbear all his knowledge, and a secret scorn rises in his heart, by which he derides those truths and goes against them; and it makes him think that certainly these things are not true, and therefore he does not believe them. If a man by nature believed the truths he says he knows, he would not go directly against them. But the ground of this is that a mist of sinful lusts is raised out of the soul which darkens the soul, so that at that time the soul is atheistic and full of unbelief. For there is no sin that ignorance and unbelief do not breathe into the soul and make way for; for if a man knew what he was doing, and understood that God saw him, and the danger of it, he would never sin.

There is no sin without an error in judgment; there is a veil of ignorance and unbelief. What creature will run into a pit when it sees it open? What creature, even the dullest, will run into the fire? A man will not run into a danger that is open to the eye of the soul, if there is not a veil of ignorance, or at least of unbelief, on the soul at that time. All sin presupposes error.

And this should make us hate sin the more. Whenever we sin, especially against our conscience, there is atheism in the soul at that time, and there is unbelief. We do not believe truth itself. There is no sinner who does not call truth into question. When he sins, he denies it or questions it; and therefore there is a veil over every man’s heart by nature, through ignorance and unbelief. The truths themselves are clear. God is clear, and the gospel is light, mens, lux [mind, light]; you+ know, they know things in the object, but in us there is darkness in our understandings. And therefore the Scripture says not only that we are dark, but that we are “darkness” itself (Eph 5:8). The clouds that arise are like the mists that come between our souls and divine things, arising from our own hearts; and the love of sinful things raises such a cloud that we do not know, or else do not believe, what is spoken. To proceed.

Observation 2. God alone can reveal and take away the veil of ignorance and unbelief from the soul. I will speak especially of this veil.

Reason 1. The reason is this: the soul is by nature so unsuited to heavenly light and heavenly truths that, unless God opens the eye of the soul and puts a new eye into it, it can never know or discern heavenly things. There must be an eye suited to the light, or there will never be any sight of it. Now God can create a new spiritual eye to discern spiritual things, which a natural eye cannot. Who can see things invisible? Divine things are invisible to natural eyes. The two are not suited to each other. He who is to reveal these and take away the veil must create a new light within as well as a light without. Now God, and only God, who created light out of darkness, can create light in the soul: “Let there be light.” He alone can create a spiritual eye, to see the things that to nature are invisible.

There are four things in sight:

  1. The object to be seen.
  2. The light that conveys it.
  3. The organ that receives it.
  4. And the light of the eye to meet the light without.

So it is in the soul. Together with divine truths, there must be light to reveal them; for light is the first visible thing, which reveals itself and all other things. And then there must be a light in the soul to judge of them, and this light must be suited to them. A carnal, base spirit judges spiritual things carnally, like himself, because he has no light in his own spirit. The things are spiritual; his eye is carnal. He has no light in his eye suited to the object, and therefore he cannot judge of them; for the Scripture says plainly, “they are spiritually discerned” (1 Cor 2:14). Therefore a carnal person has carnal conceptions of spiritual things, as a holy man spiritualizes things by a spiritual conception of them. There are degrees of discerning things. The highest degree is to see things “face to face,” as they are in heaven. The next to that is to see them in a mirror; for there I see the motion and true image of a man, though not so clearly as when I see him face to face. Therefore we soon forget the image of it in a mirror; we keep a more fixed image of the other, because there is more reality in it. We see things put into water, and that is less; but then there is a sight of a man in pictures, which is less than the rest, because we do not see the motion. It is just so: a carnal man scarcely sees the dead likeness of things. In Moses’s time they saw things as in water, dimly as it were, though truly; but we see things in a mirror of truth as clearly as we possibly can in this world. In heaven we will see face to face; we will see him as he is. And then will be the joy of this excellent feast, and the consummation of all the sweet promises, of which here we can have only a taste.

Reason 2. So that is the first reason why God alone takes away the veil, which arises from the unsuitableness between the soul and divine truths.

There is nothing in the heart of man but what is contrary to divine light. Even natural knowledge is contrary to it. Natural conscience checks only for gross sins, not for spiritual sins. Obedience and a respectable life make a man full of pride, and arm him against self-denial and against the righteousness of Christ and justification. There is nothing in the soul, without grace, that does not rise against the soul in divine things. Reason 3. Again, there is such a disproportion between the soul, full as it is of sin and guilt, and heavenly things, which are so great, that the heart of man will not believe unless God convinces the soul that God is so good and gracious that, though these things are great and excellent, yet he will bestow them on our souls. And therefore he sends the Spirit, who overpowers the soul, though it is full of the fear and guilt that sin incurs. However unworthy we are, he will magnify his grace to poor sinners; and without that the soul will never believe, so infinite is the disproportion between the soul and the things, between the sinful soul and the Spirit. So God must overpower the soul to make it believe. The Scripture is full of this. As we are by nature ignorant and full of unbelief, so God alone can overpower the soul and take away the veil of ignorance.

Reason 4. All the angels in heaven, and all the creatures in the world, the most skillful men in the world, cannot bring light into the soul; they cannot bring light into the heart. They can speak of divine things, but they understand them little. But to bring light into the heart, so that the heart may taste them and obey by believing: this they cannot do. And therefore all God’s children are theodidactoi, taught by God. God alone has the privilege of teaching the heart, of bending and bowing the heart to believe. So God alone, by his Spirit, takes away the veil of ignorance and unbelief.

Observation 3. Now, the third thing is that to have the veil taken off belongs only to the church and to the children of God. “On this mountain,” says the Scripture, “the veil of all faces will be swallowed up or taken away.”

I partly showed in the former point that to have the veil taken off belongs only to God’s children. There is a veil in all things. Either the things are hidden from men, as among the Gentiles, or, if the things are revealed, there is a veil on the heart. Their lusts raise up a cloud, and until God subdues them by the Holy Spirit they are dark, indeed darkness itself. Only Goshen had light when all Egypt was in darkness; so there is light only in the church, and all other parts of the world are in darkness. And among men in the church there is darkness on the souls of unregenerate men, who are not sanctified and subdued by the Spirit of God. And all godly men are full of light; indeed, they are “lights in the world” (Phil 2:15). As wicked men are darkness, so gracious men are made lights of the world by the Spirit of God, from him who is the true light, Christ himself.

It belongs to the church alone to know the greatest good and the greatest evil. It is found nowhere but in the church, who are the people of God. Only God’s elect can know the greatest evil, that is, sin, which the Spirit of God reveals; and the greatest good, that is, God’s mercy in Christ, and sanctifying grace. The same Spirit does both. As light reveals foul things as well as fair, so the same Spirit of God reveals the loathsomeness of sin and the sweetness of grace. Where the one is not, there is never the other; where there is no true, deep discerning of sin, there is never any knowledge of grace; it is found only in the church. Those who have the spirit of illumination have sanctification as well.

We will make use of all these together. You+ see, then, what we are by nature, and that God’s grace must take away the veil; and this is from all those within the church, and in the church from those whom God is pleased to sanctify.

Observation. In the fourth place, where this veil is taken off from anyone, there is spiritual joy and feasting with it, as here he joins them both together.

“I will make a feast of fat things, and will take away the veil” (verse 7).

Reason. The reason for the connection of this is that the same Spirit who is a Spirit of revelations is a Spirit of comfort; and the same Spirit who is the Spirit of comfort is a Spirit of revelation. All the sweetness that the soul relishes comes from light, and all light that is spiritual conveys sweetness, both together. Beloved, there is a marvelous sweetness in divine truths. In Christ is all marrow; and in religion there is forgiveness of sins, and inward peace, and joy, and grace fitting us to be like Christ, and for heaven. They are incredibly sweet; they are all marrow. Yes, but they are so only to those who know them. Now God’s Spirit, who reveals these things to us, breeds a taste in the soul. The Spirit of illumination to God’s children is a Spirit of sanctification as well; and that sanctification alters the taste and relish of the will and affections, so that with the revealing of these things there is a taste and relish of them. It is sapida scientia, a savory knowledge, that they have. And therefore where he makes a feast, he takes away the veil; and where he takes away the veil, he makes a feast. What a wonderful satisfaction the soul has, when the veil is taken off, to see God reconciled in Christ! to see sin pardoned! to see the beginnings of grace, which will be finished and accomplished in glory! to discern that “peace which passes understanding” (Phil 4:7). What a marvelous sweetness there is in these things!

They cannot be spiritually revealed to the knowledge without a feast in the soul, in which the soul delights itself; so both of these go together.

And therefore we should not rest in a revealing that does not bring a savor with it to the soul. Undoubtedly, the knowledge that is not by divine revelation of heavenly truths has no solace and comfort for the soul.

We see how these depend on one another. Then let us make this use of all of them:

Use 1. Since there is a veil over all men by nature, the work of ignorance and unbelief; and since God alone takes it away by his Holy Spirit; and since only those who are godly and sanctified have it taken off; and since, where this is done, there is a spiritual feast, joy and comfort and strength: then let us labor to have this veil taken off. Let us labor to have the eyes of our understanding enlightened, to have our hearts subdued to believe; let us take note of our natural condition. We are drowned and wrapped in darkness, the best of us all. Knowing what we are by nature does not bring us to heaven, nor does just any knowledge; there must be a revelation, a taking away of the veil. How many content themselves with the common light of education, and knowledge handed down! They were brought up this way and catechized, and sat under such a ministry! But how little spiritual knowledge of spiritual things is cared for! And yet this is necessary to salvation. There is good reason to press this, that we must not rest in common knowledge. If religion is not known effectually, it is like lightning, which does not direct a man in his way, but dazzles him and puts him quite out of his way. Many have flashes of knowledge that affect them a little; but this affection is soon gone, and does not direct them at all in the ways of life. And therefore labor that the will and affections may be subject. Ask God for a heart of flesh (2 Cor 3:3), a heart that yields to the truth. We know that truths which reach only the ear will harden, for no one is harder than a common, formal Christian. A man would do better to fall into the hands of papists than into the hands of a formal, hypocritical Christian. Why? They pride themselves on their profession of religion. There were no persecutors worse than the scribes and Pharisees, who stood in their own light. They were more cruel than Pilate. And therefore, if we are informed but not truly transformed, to love the truth we know and hate the evil we know, it makes us worse.

And then it enrages men all the more. The more they know, the more they are enraged. When truths are pressed which men do not intend to obey, they fret against the ordinance, and cast stones, as it were, in the face of truth. When medicine stirs up an infection but is not strong enough to carry it away, it endangers the body; and where light is not strong enough to dispel corruption when it stirs corruption up, it enrages it. When men know truth and are not molded into it, they first rage against it, and then little by little fall from it and become extreme enemies to it. It is a dangerous thing, therefore, to rest in naked knowledge. Beg God, then, to take away the veil of ignorance and unbelief, so that light and life may go together; then we will be fit to feast with the Lord.

Means. Now, to have true saving knowledge, first, we must meekly attend God’s ordinances, which are sanctified for this purpose: to let light into the soul.

  1. If we want to know sin and our state by nature, and how to come out of it, then along with this revelation there must come a heavenly strength into the soul, a heavenly taste and relish. And therefore attend the ordinances, and labor for a humble soul, empty of ourselves; and do not expect to break into heavenly things by strength of natural abilities. God must reveal; God must take away the veil, and only by his Holy Spirit in the ordinance. The veil is taken away from the object in the opening of truths; but the veil must be taken away from the object, and from the heart too. There must be knowledge of the object, as well as an object. The object must be sanctified and fitted to the persons, or divine truths will never be understood divinely, nor spiritual truths spiritually. Labor to be emptied of yourselves+. As far as we are emptied of our self-conceit and our own understanding, so far we are filled in divine things. As far as we are emptied of ourselves, so far we are filled with the Spirit of God, and knowledge, and grace. As a vessel is fit to be filled with more liquid in the measure that it is emptied, so in the measure that we grow in self-denial and humility, we are also filled with knowledge. He will teach a humble soul that does not stand in its own light what it is to repent, to believe, to love; what it is to be patient under the cross; what it is to live holily and die with comfort. The Spirit of God will teach a humble, self-denying soul all these things. And therefore labor for a humble, empty soul, and labor not to cast ourselves too much into the sins and fashions of the times, as the apostle says: “Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your+ mind” (Rom 12:2).

    1. When a man casts himself into the mold of the times, and is set on living as the rest do, he will never understand the secrets of God and the good pleasure of God; for the world must be condemned. The world goes the broad way. And therefore we must not consider what others do, but what God teaches us to do.

    2. And add to this: let us labor to practice what we know. “But he who does the will of My Father will know of every teaching whether it is from God or not” (John 7:17). We must do, and we will know.

      Question. But can we do before we know?

      Answer. The meaning is this: that first we have upbringing and education, and some light of the Spirit, which turns it at once into practice by obedience to that knowledge. And then you+ will know more. He who does these things will know all. Those who practice what they already know will know. “Whoever has will be given more” (Matt 13:12); that is, to him who has some knowledge and puts into practice what he has, God will increase the talent of his knowledge; he will know more and more, until God reveals himself fully in the world to come.

    3. And therefore let us be faithful to ourselves, and true to the knowledge we have; let us love it and put it into practice. When divine truths are revealed, let the heart love them, or God may give us up to believe lies. Many have been given up to this sin. Because they give way to their own proud, scornful hearts when truths are revealed, they do not know the love of the truth. God knows what a jewel the truth is; and since they despise it, God gives them up to believe lies. And take care: let us practice what we know, and love what we know, and receive it with a loving affection. A loving affection is the casket of this jewel. If we do not receive it in love, it leaves its place among us; and when it is gone, God will remove us into darkness.

      And remember, it is God who takes away the veil of ignorance and unbelief. And therefore make this use of it.

  2. To make our studies and private rooms places of prayer; not to come to divine truths to out-wrestle their excellence with our own wits, but to pray to God, as you+ have it in Ps 119:18, “Open my eyes, and reveal Your truth.” And St Paul prays for the “spirit of revelation” (Eph 1:17). And so let us ask God to reveal and take away the veil from us, that he would open divine truths to our souls; that, since he has the key of David, and “what He opens no one can shut” (Rev 3:7), he would open our understandings to conceive things, and our hearts to believe. He alone has the key of the soul. We can shut our souls, but we cannot open them again. So we can shut our hearts to divine truths; we can do this by nature; but we cannot open them without the help of the Spirit. He can open our understandings, as he did the disciples’. He can open our hearts to believe; he can do it, and he will do it. If we seek him, he will not turn away the humble desires of those who fear him. And therefore, as for heavenly light and heavenly revelation, all the teaching of the men of the world cannot give it. If we know no more than we can have from books and from men who teach us, we will never come to heaven; we must have God teach the heart as well as the brain. He must teach not only the truths themselves, as they are revealed, but the love of them, the faith in them, the practice of them. And he alone can do this; he alone can teach the heart; he alone can reveal the bent of the heart, and Satan’s wiles that cast a cloud on the understanding. The Spirit alone can do it. And therefore in all our efforts let us labor to get knowledge, and join holiness and divine grace with it, and pray to God that he would reveal the mystery of salvation to us.

Question. But how will we know whether we have this heavenly light and revelation or not, whether the veil is still on our hearts or not? I will not be long on the point.

Answer 1. We may know it by this. The apostle Peter, to express the might of God’s power, says, “He has called us out of darkness into His marvelous light” (1 Pet 2:9). In the soul that has the veil taken from it, there is a marveling at the goodness of God, a wondering at the things of faith. And the soul sets such a price on divine things that all is “dung and dross” in comparison with the excellent knowledge of Jesus Christ (Phil 3:8). Why is it that you will reveal yourself to us, and not to the world? the soul asks, as one wondering at the goodness of God. What are we? What am I, that God should reveal these things to me and not to the world, when many perish in darkness and the shadow of death? Though they hear of divine things, yet they, teaching rebellion and unbelief, are not molded to them, and so perish eternally. There is a secret wonder at the goodness of God to the poor soul, and a wondering at spiritual things. “Oh, how sweet is Your law!” says David (Ps 119:103). And teach me the wonders of your law; and joy inexpressible and glorious, and peace that passes understanding (Phil 4:7): these things are high to the soul.

Answer 2. By the taste of what they have, they wonder at that little, and at what they look for, and are carried on with desire always further and further, which is a further evidence. Those who have any spiritual knowledge are carried on to grow more and more, and to enter further and further into the kingdom. Where there is not a desire always, until they come to the full measure that is to be had in Jesus Christ, there is no knowledge at all. Certainly a gracious soul, once it sees, always desires to feel the power and strength of Christ in it; as Paul counted all things dung in comparison with this knowledge: to know himself in Christ, and to feel the power of Christ’s death in dying to sin, and the power of his resurrection in raising him to newness of life. It was St Paul’s aim always to walk toward the high prize of God’s calling; and where that is not, no grace is begun.

Answer 3. And again, where divine light is and the veil is taken away, it is through the sanctified means; for God works by his own instruments and means, and such souls are able to justify all the ways of wisdom. “Wisdom is justified by her children” (Matt 11:19). By experience they are able to say that the word is the word. I have found it casting me down and raising me up, and searching the hidden corners of my heart. I have found God’s ordinances powerful, the word and sacrament. I have found my hope, faith, strength and spiritual comfort, and therefore I can justify them; for I have found, tasted and relished these things, which work on the soul what Christ worked on the body. I find my eyes, I find my deaf ears, opened. I can hear with a different relish from before. I find a life and quickening to good things, though it is weak. I had no life at all toward them before. I find a relish which I did not know before. So there are spiritual senses by which I am able to justify that these are the things of God. So those who have divine truths can justify all the ordinances of God by their own experience. As Peter answered when Christ asked him, Will you+ also go away? Go away! said Peter; “To whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life” (John 6:68). I have found your words efficacious to comfort and strengthen and raise up, and should I depart from you, who have the words of eternal life? And so take a soul that the Spirit of God has worked on. Ask whether such souls will be careless about the means of salvation, and not pray, or hear, or receive the sacrament. By these, eternal life is conveyed to me. God has let in comfort and strength and joy through these, and should I leave these things? No; I will not. “To whom shall I go? You have the words of eternal life.” Are we able to justify these things by the sweetness we have found in them? Then certainly God has shone on the soul, and together with strength and light has conveyed sweetness to the soul.

Answer 4. A godly man sees things with life; his sight works on him. It is a transforming sight. As the apostle says, “We all behold the glory of God, and are changed” (2 Cor 3:18). Sight of light and life go together with a Christian; as Christ says, he is “the light of the world” (John 9:5), and “the life of the world” (John 6:51). First light, for life comes with light, and light conveys life. All grace is dropped into the will through the understanding; and wherever Christ is life, he is light, because true knowledge is a transforming knowledge. But if religion is not known effectually, it hardens and makes men worse.

By God’s good providence we have now come to further business: to partake of these mysteries. Yet it should be the desire of our souls that our eyes may be opened, that in these divine and precious mysteries he would reveal hidden love, which is not seen with the eyes of the body; that they may see and taste and relish his love and goodness in Jesus Christ; that as the outward man is refreshed with the elements, so the inward man may be refreshed with his Spirit; that they may be effectual to us; that we may justify the course God takes so far as to come to them in love and with joy.

The Fourth Sermon

“He will destroy on this mountain the face of the covering cast over all people, and the veil that is spread over all nations. He will swallow up death in victory.—Isa 25:7, 8.”

We have already spoken at length of the spiritual and eternal favors of God, set out in the previous verse, “On this mountain the Lord of Hosts will make a feast of fat things.” While our soul is in the body, it is guided a great deal by our imagination. So spiritual things are presented through outward things, and conveyed to the soul that way; only we must remember that there is far greater excellence in the things themselves than in their representation. For what are all banquets, fatness with marrow, wine on the lees, compared with the joy and sweetness of religion, begun here and completed in the world to come?

In Christ there is nothing but marrow and sweetness in religion, which may refresh a man in the lowest condition, if he can only have a taste of it.

Now because the spiritual things of Christ do us no good as long as they are hidden, the Holy Spirit therefore sets down a promise, “that God will take away the covering cast on all people, and the veil spread over all nations.”

But there are some things that will dampen all mirth. Now here is security against them, so that our joy may be complete; and this is in the next verse, to which I now come: “He will swallow up death in victory; He will wipe away tears from all faces.” Having spoken of a great feast before, an excellent feast, the prophet here sets out the courses of that feast. What is it that comes with it?

First of all, there will be light to reveal the excellence of the feast. The veil is taken away, and we are given knowledge to know divine things in a spiritual way.

Then the fear of death, which would dampen any feast, is taken away. “He will swallow up death in victory, and wipe away all tears,” that is, all sorrow. The effect is put for the cause. This is an excellent promise, an excellent course in this spiritual banquet. Suppose a man were seated at a feast furnished with all delicacies, royally attended, his clothes fitting the occasion, but with a sword hung over his head ready to fall on him: it would put such a damper on his spirit that it would spoil the joy of the feast. So when we hear of spiritual excellencies, and yet death, and hell, and damnation are coming along, alas! where is the comfort you speak of? And therefore, to make the feast more perfect, there is not only light and knowledge, but also the removal of whatever may dampen the feast. So this must come in to comfort all the rest. “He will swallow up death in victory, and wipe away tears from all faces.” Death is here represented to us under the word victory as a combatant, as one we must fight with, a captain.

And then here is the victory over him: Christ overcomes him, and overcomes him gloriously. It is not only a conquest, but a swallowing of him up. Usually God treats all sorts of enemies in their own kind. He causes those who spoil to be spoiled, those who swallow up to be swallowed up. So death, the great swallower, will be swallowed up.

Beloved, death is the great king of kings, and the emperor of emperors, the great captain and ruling king of the world; for no king has such dominion as death has. It spreads its government and victory over all nations. He is impartial, though a tyrant: since as a tyrant he spares none, he is impartial in this. He subdues young and old, poor and rich. He levels scepters and spades together. He levels all. There is no difference between the dust of an emperor and that of the humblest man. He is a tyrant who rules over all. And so there is this fairness in him: he spares none.

He has continued from the beginning of the world to this time; but he is a tyrant brought in by ourselves, Rom 5:12. Sin let in death. It opened the door. Death is no creature of God’s making. Satan brought in sin, and sin brought in death. So we ourselves are accessories to the powerful stroke of this prevailing tyrant. And therefore sin is called the cause of death. Sin brought in death, and arms death. The weapon death fights with, and with which it causes great terror, is sin. The cause is armed with the power of the wrath of God for sin, the fear of hell, and damnation. So with wrath, and hell, and damnation arming it, sin brings a sting of its own, and puts venom into death. All cares, and fears, and sorrows, and sicknesses are lesser and petty deaths, harbingers of death itself; but the attendants that follow this great king are worst of all, as in Rev 6:8, “I looked and saw a pale horse. Its rider’s name was Death, and hell followed close behind.” What would death be, if it were not for the pit and dungeon that follows it? So death is attended with hell, and hell with eternity. Therefore here is a strange kind of prevailing. There is no victory where there is no enemy, and therefore death must be an enemy; indeed, it is the worst enemy, and the last enemy. Death is not placed in the front ranks, but it is placed last for the greatest advantage, and is a great enemy. What does death do? It deprives us of all comfort, pleasure, communion with one another in this life, callings, and whatever else is comforting. The grave is the house of oblivion. Death is terrible in itself, even to nature, as Augustine says, where it is not swallowed up by Christ; for it is an evil in itself, and, as I said, armed with a sting of sin, after which follows hell.

Now this death is swallowed up. When the Scripture makes death a person, it is not unfitting for us to speak as the Scripture does. The Scripture makes death a person, and puts a kind of triumphing spirit in God’s children over death. “Where, O death, is your sting? Where, O grave, is your victory?” (1 Cor 15:55). Death is the greatest swallower, and yet it is swallowed up by Christ. Death has swallowed up all, and when it has swallowed them up, it keeps them. It keeps the dust of kings, subjects, great and small, until the general day of judgment, when death itself will be swallowed up. It is therefore like those Solomon speaks of, who cry, “Give, give” (Prov 30:15), and, like the grave, it is never satisfied. Yet this death is swallowed up in victory.

But how does death come to be swallowed up? Christ will swallow up death in victory, for himself and his own.

Reason. First of all, because sin brought in death, our Savior Christ became sin, a sacrifice to his father’s justice for sin. He was made sin for us, he was made a curse for us, to take away the curse due to us; and once sin is taken away, what has death to do with us, and hell, and damnation, the attendants on death? Nothing at all. Therefore, Col 2:14, 15, on the cross Christ nailed the law, and sin, and the devil. There he reigned over principalities and powers, which were only executioners let loose because of our sins. And since God has been satisfied for sin, the devil has nothing to do with us but to exercise us, and that only for our good. So he has swallowed up death, because by his death he has taken away sin, and so the power of Satan, whose power comes by sin. And therefore it is excellently set down in Heb 2:14, “He too shared in their flesh and blood, so that through death He might destroy him who holds the power of death, that is, the devil.” So Christ by death overthrew Satan, who had the power of death, because by death he took away sin, the sins of all, and bore our sins on the cross, and was made sin for us, he who knew no sin. He is ours, if we believe. For Christ is given to a particular man when he believes. Beloved, Christ on the cross triumphed over all our spiritual enemies, sin, and death, and all. It was a kingdom of patience. You+ know there is a double kingdom of Christ: a kingdom of patience, and a kingdom of power.

  1. Christ on the cross, suffering the punishment due to sin, overcame the law, and the devil, and sin, which is the kingdom of patience.
  2. The kingdom of power he has in heaven. If Christ was so able in his kingdom of patience to conquer our greatest enemies, what will he do in his kingdom of power? As Paul reasons, “If by his death we are saved, much more now that he triumphs in heaven, and appears for us, is he able to convey greater things to us” (Rom 5:10).

If Christ in the days of his flesh conquered, how glorious will his conquest be at the day of judgment! Note this: Christ has conquered all in his own person, as our head; then he will conquer for us in his mystical body. What is now done in his person will be done in his members. In the meantime, faith is our victory, his conquest over death is our victory; his victory over all our spiritual enemies is our victory. Everyone who believes is a conqueror of death, though he dies, because he sees it conquered in Christ his head; and as it is truly conquered in him, so Christ will conquer it in all his members. For as Christ in his natural body has gone to heaven, there to appear on our behalf, so mystical Christ will be wholly in glory. He will not leave a finger behind. We will all triumph over all our spiritual enemies. As Christ’s natural body is glorious in heaven as our head, so his mystical body will be also.

You+ see, then, how death is swallowed up by Christ as our surety, as the second Adam on the cross; and truly swallowed up in him. And by faith this victory is ours, and the time will come when in our own persons it will be swallowed up in victory.

This might be enlarged on, but I hasten to make use of it.

Notice, I beg you+, how death is swallowed up by Christ in his own person for our good. He yielded a great deal to death, for death seized him on the cross. Death separates soul from body. Death had him in his own private chamber, his grave, for three days. Indeed, this great king and tyrant, death, won a great conquest over Christ himself. But here was the glory of this victory! When death, this great conqueror of the world, had Christ on the cross, and in his own dominion, in the grave, where he rules and reigns, consuming and swallowing up all, death was forced to give up all. And Satan thought he would have a great morsel when he devoured Christ, but there was a hook in Christ’s divine power that caught him, so that when he thought he was swallowing up Christ, he was swallowed up himself. His head was then broken. He never had such a blow as he had from Christ on the cross, when he was overcome. Christ, being visibly a scorn to the world, yet invisibly, in God’s acceptance of that sacrifice and in a spirit of faith, triumphs over Satan. Death was subdued even in his own kingdom, and that makes the victory great.

Because death seized on Christ without right, Christ has freed us from the evil of death when it had a right to us. Death has lost all its right by fastening on Christ, and so has become like a drone without a sting. So the great swallower of all is swallowed up itself at last by Christ.

Use 1. Now for a comforting use of it. First, let us consider that God often yields a great deal to his greatest enemies. God uses a stratagem of retreating: he seems to retreat and give his enemies liberty, but it is to triumph over them and trample on them with greater shame. He will tread them to dust afterward. Christ gave death a great deal of liberty. He was crucified and tormented, then taken to the grave, and there he lay. And this was to raise a greater triumph over this great prevailer, over the world and death itself.

It continues so in the church. Does he not give way to the enemies of the church? They may come to say, Aha, aha, that is how we would have it. Now the poor children of God are where we want them; but then comes sudden destruction. God, to make his victory more glorious, and to expose their cruelty all the more, comes on them when they are at the top of their pleasure, and the church at the bottom of abasement. Then God swallows up all in victory, as Christ did death when it seemed to have been victorious itself.

This is a very comforting consideration, for if death was overcome when it seemed to overcome Christ, why should we fear any other enemy? Christ has broken the net, as an eagle or great bird does, and the rest escape through him.

You+ may enlarge on this in your+ own meditations. He will swallow up death in victory. This is said of the time to come: he will swallow up death. But Paul says it is also past, and swallowed up already. Faith says it is done; and so it is in our head. Would it not be comforting now to all true-hearted Christians to hear that the church is faring better, and that the enemies were swallowed up, for they are only the instruments of this lesser death? Let us get the spirit of faith, and see them all conquered, for certainly they will have the worst of it at last. He who has swallowed up death in victory will swallow up all that are the cause of death. And therefore the Scripture speaks of these things as past, “Babylon has fallen, like a millstone cast into the bottom of the sea” (Rev 18:21).

Get a spirit of faith, and we will never be much troubled by Babylon; for all the enemies of Christ, and the adherents of that man of sin, must go down, and partake of the judgments threatened in the Revelation. Heaven has decided it, and all the scheming of Rome and hell cannot annul it. They are already swallowed up, to faith, and Christ will rule until he has put them all under his feet, Ps 110:1; which will be done, not only to destroy them, but to raise himself higher in giving them up to their downfall.

Use 2. Again, if death is swallowed up in victory, labor to be one with Christ crucified, for union with him. Begin with union with Christ crucified. The first union is with Christ abased, and then with Christ glorified. And therefore labor to see sin, which brought in death, subdued in some measure by the power of Christ’s death, and then we will have comfort in his death glorified. For in my “holy mountain” death is swallowed up, that is, in the true church of Christ. Labor to be members of Christ; otherwise death will come to assail you+ as a tyrant indeed, armed with a terrible sting, in its full force. It is the most terrible thing to see death come armed with the wrath and anger of God, and attended with hell and damnation. Labor, therefore, to be one with Christ crucified, to get our sins crucified, and ourselves partakers of his death; and then there is no damnation, no fear of death, to those who are in Christ. They may die, but they are freed from eternal death, and they will rise again, even as Christ’s body rose, to glory.

Get into Christ, therefore, and desire the power of his death subduing sin. The more we grow in that, the more we grow in boldness and joy, and in whatever privileges follow Christ.

Use 3. Again, when we are in Christ, true members of him, let us be thankful to God for this victory, thankful to Jesus Christ who has given us victory. When we think of death, of sin, of judgment, of hell, of damnation, let us be in the frame a Christian should be in. Now let the man for whom the most terrible and fearful things in the world are conquered enemies say, Oh, blessed be God for Christ, and blessed be Christ for dying for us, and by death disarming death of its sting! Now we can think of it quietly in our minds; now we can think of all these as conquered enemies: this is the fruit of Christ’s death. They are not only enemies, but friends in Christ. As for sin (its guilt, which binds us over to damnation, is taken away), the remainders of it serve to humble us, to make us feel the power of pardon, and to desire another world, where we will be wholly spiritual. So death is a part of the estate settled on us as Christ’s bride. “All things are yours+ … life or death” (1 Cor 3:22). Death does us many excellent services. It is a door and passage to life. Death is the death of itself; it destroys itself. We never truly live until we die, and when we die, we are past the fear of death. So sin dies, misery dies, death dies. Though it takes us from comforts, and employments, and friends here, yet it is a change to a better place, and better company, and better employments, and a better condition, to be in a glorious condition for eternity. And therefore we have cause to bless God in Christ, who took our nature, and in our nature disarmed our greatest enemy, sin, and so disarmed death, and freed us from the wrath of God, and hell, and damnation. Oh, we can never be thankful enough for this!

Use 4. Again, if death is swallowed up in victory, let us be ashamed of the fear of death, because Christ says he will swallow him up, as he has already in his own person. Should we be afraid of an enemy that is swallowed up in our head, and will be swallowed up in every one of us? If we cherish fear, we show we do not look for a share in this promise; for it is a promise that “on this holy mountain death will be swallowed up in victory,” and why should we fear a conquered enemy? No one will fear an enemy that is conquered.

Objection. But how did Christ come to fear death, and we not to fear it?

Answer. Christ had to deal with death armed with a terrible sting, with sin, and the wrath of God for sin. And therefore, when he was to die, he said, “Father, let this cup pass from Me” (Matt 26:39). But death is disarmed for us. He had to encounter sin and the wrath of God, and death in all its strength. But we do not. The death we deal with is like the bronze serpent, which has the shape of death but no sting at all. It has become a drone ever since it lost its sting in Christ. Life took death, so that death might take life, as one said. The meaning is that Christ’s life itself took death, so that we, who were so subject to death that we were death itself, might take life. Oh, blessed consideration! Nothing is comparable to the consideration of the death of Christ! It is the death of deaths.

And then again, we are sure of victory. It is conquered in our head, and will be in us. But you+ say we are to struggle with the pangs of death, and many troubles meet in death. It is true, but to faith it is conquered, and in Christ our head. We must fight. Christ trains us to overcome death ourselves by faith, and then we are sure of victory. Join these two together. It is conquered in Christ our head, and will be conquered by us. Death keeps our dust, and must give it all back again.

Objection. But in the meantime we die.

Answer. It is so, but we are sure of victory. He who has conquered for us will protect us in our combat. We fight against death and the terror of it in the strength and faith of his victory. Join these three together.

He who has been our Savior in life will be so to death, and not up to death only, to leave us then, but to death, and in death, forever; indeed, he is most ready to help us in our last conflict. Indeed, to wicked men death is terrible, for he sends the devil to fetch them out of the world; but for those who are his, he sends his angels to fetch them, and he helps them in their combat. We must not, therefore, fear overmuch. There is a natural fear of death. Death worked on Christ himself, God-man; not only death, but such a death. He was to be forsaken by his Father, and to lie under the sense of the wrath of God; the separation of that soul from the body he took on himself was terrible; and therefore he says, “If it is possible, let this cup pass from Me”; that was nature, and without it he would not have been true man. But what I say is that grace may be above nature. Death is a time of darkness. It strips us of earthly comforts, friends, callings, employments; but then comes the eye of faith to lay hold on the victory of Christ in time to come, when death will be wholly swallowed up in victory; and then on the glorious state to come, to which death brings us. So here faith must be above sense, and grace above nature; and therefore, I beg you+, let us labor for it.

There are two sorts of men to whom I would speak a little.

First. Those who in a kind of bravado seem to slight death; men of base spirits, as we call them; fools, vainglorious spirits, empty spirits. Is there any creature, unless he is in Christ, able on good grounds to slight so great an enemy as death, armed with a sting of sin, and attended with hell and damnation? The Romish and devilish spirits are terrible; but if your sins are not pardoned, it is the most terrible thing in the world to die, for there is a gulf afterward. What shall we say, then, of single combatants, who for vainglory throw away their lives, who for a foul word, a little disgrace, will take on this enemy that is armed with sin; and if they die, they die in sin. And this is the miserable condition of the man who dies in sin: his death opens the gate to another death, which is eternal. They say they have repented, but there is no repenting of a sin yet to be committed. Can you repent of a sin before it is committed? That is only a mockery of God. And what does the Scripture say? Is it not the most terrible judgment under heaven to die in our sins? A man who dies in sin dies in hell: he goes from death to hell, and that for eternity.

I wonder, therefore, that the wisdom of flesh and blood should take away men’s wits, and faith, and grace, and all, so far as to slight death, and repentance, as if it were so easy. Now, beloved, death is a terrible thing. It has a sting, and you will know it. If you do not have grace to feel its sting while you live, when you die the sting will revive; then your conscience will awake in hell. Drunkenness and jollity take away the sense of sin; but sin will revive, and conscience will revive. God has not put it into us for nothing. Death is terrible if it is not disarmed beforehand. And if you set about dying without disarming it before, it will not be faced down. It is not an enemy to be scorned and slighted. And therefore be Christians in good earnest, or else give up your+ profession of faith, and perish eternally. For we must all die, and it is a greater matter than we take it to be. But if we are true Christians, it is the sweetest thing in the world, an end of all misery, a beginning of true happiness, an entrance into whatever is comforting. Blessed are those who are in the Lord by faith, and those who die in the Lord. Their death is better than the day of life. Our birthday brings us into misery; and therefore let me speak to true Christians, and bid them be ashamed of fearing death too much, since death has turned from an enemy into a reconciled friend.

Second. This may, in the next place, yield great consolation to those who are in Christ Jesus, that death by Christ is swallowed up in victory; and all the more because the Holy Spirit means more than a bare victory over death. Death is not only subdued, but is made a friend to us, as in Ps 110:1 it is said, “his enemies will be his footstool.” Now a footstool is not only trampled on, but is a help to rise. And so death is not only subdued, but it advances God’s children, and raises them higher. It is not only an enemy, but a reconciled friend; for death does what no friend in the world can do. It ends all our misery, and is the entrance into all happiness for eternity. And whatever it strips us of here, it gives us the advantage of better things in another world. It cuts off our pleasures, and profits, and company, and callings here; but what is that to our blessed change afterward, to our praising of God forever, to the company of blessed souls, and the profits and pleasures at the right hand of God forevermore? And therefore it is not only conquered, but, to show the excellence of his power, he has made it a friend instead of an enemy, and the best friend in the world. It indeed separates soul from body, but it joins the soul to Christ; so the union we have by it is better than the separation, if the union gives us what we desire. “I desire to be dissolved,” says St. Paul, Phil 1:23, but that is not well translated. “I desire to depart, and to be with Christ, which is best of all.” So it is not only not an enemy, but a friend. And therefore the apostle makes it our marriage settlement, part of our portion: all things are yours+. Why? “You+ are Christ’s, and Christ is God’s” (1 Cor 3:23). What are ours? “The present, the future, life, death” (1 Cor 3:22, 23). And well may death be ours, because sin is our enemy—that remainder which is kept in our nature to exercise us, and humble us, and fit us for grace. As Augustine says, I dare be bold to say it is profitable for some to fall, to make them more careful and watchful, and to prize mercy more. So not only death, but sin and the devil himself are ours; for his plots are for our good. God outshoots him with his own bow. “He will give them over to Satan,” says the apostle, “that they may learn not to blaspheme” (1 Tim 1:20). Yet though they have a spirit of blasphemy, by the humbling of their bodies they are taught not to blaspheme; so not only death, but sin, and he who brought sin into the world, the devil, have become our friends.

This being so, it may serve as a special comfort, so that we do not fear the king of fears. The devil has great advantage by this affection of fear when it is set on this object, death. Overcome death, and all troubles are overcome. Who will fear anything once he has given himself up to God? “Skin for skin! A man will give up all he owns in exchange for his life” (Job 2:4). The devil knows that well enough. Therefore “Do not be afraid,” says Christ, “of those who kill the body” (Matt 10:28). Fear causes snares, says Solomon, Prov 29:25, snares of conscience. But if a man has once overcome the fear of death, what more is to be done? What if they take away life? They cannot take away what is better than life, the favor of God. If we die in the Lord, we die in the favor of God, which is better than life; and we will be found in the Lord at the day of judgment, and will be forever with the Lord in heaven. And therefore this is a ground for standing firm in good causes, in spite of any threats whatever, because death itself is swallowed up in victory.

The worst the world can do is to take away this nature of ours. When they have done that, they have done all they can; and when they have done that, they have done us a favor. What frees us from all that is to be feared is not to be feared, says Tertullian. What is to be feared in the world? Every sickness, every disgrace? Why, death frees us from all. We see that every day takes away a piece of one’s life, and when death comes it overthrows itself; for the soul goes at once to the place of happiness. The body sleeps a while, and death has no more power.

“He who believes in Me,” says Christ, “will not see death, but has passed from death to life” (John 5:24). He will not see spiritual death; but as he lives in Christ, he will die in Christ, and rise again in Christ. He who has the life of grace begun will have it brought to completion without interruption. It is a point of wonderful comfort that death is so overcome that we are in heaven already. And it is no hard saying, but agrees with the truth of other points; for are not Christ and we all one? His body is there, and is he not the head of his mystical body? He who carried his natural body there, will he not carry his mystical body there too? Will he be in heaven piecemeal? Therefore we are in heaven already, in the best part of us. We are represented in heaven, for Christ represents us there as the husband does the wife. He has taken possession of heaven for us.

Christ cannot be divided, as Augustine says. “We sit in heavenly places already with Christ” (Eph 2:6). And what a comfort this is, that while we live we are in heaven, and that death cannot hinder us from our resurrection, which is the restoring of all things. And therefore, as the apostle says, “Comfort one another with these things” (1 Thess 4:18). These things indeed have much comfort in them.

Let us labor, then, to be comforted: this is the use the apostle makes of it; and to be fruitful in our places, considering the victory we have by Christ, 1 Cor 15. It is an excellent chapter that proves at length Christ’s victory as the cause of our victory, because he is the firstfruits that sanctifies all the rest. “Finally, my brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the works of the Lord, because you+ know that your+ labor in the Lord is not in vain.” He raises that exhortation to fruitfulness and constancy from this very ground of the victory Christ has gained by death. “Where, O death, is your sting? Where, O grave, is your victory? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ.” “And therefore be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, because you+ know that your+ labor in the Lord will not be in vain” (1 Cor 15:58). Make the use the apostle makes, of fruitfulness to God for Christ, since we can think of death, and sin, the devil, and all his malice, and not be afraid; indeed, we can think of them all with comfort, since we are not only freed from their tyranny, but they are our friends.

Christ has the key of hell and death; a saying taken from the custom of governors who carried the key. He has the government and command of hell and death. Now if Christ has command of death, he will not allow death to hurt his members, or triumph over them forever. He will keep them in the grave. Our bodies are safe in the grave. The dust is fitted for a heavenly body, for another kind of body than we have now; and Christ, who has the key, will let them out again. Therefore trust a while until the times of restoring come, and then we will have a glorious soul and a glorious body, as the apostle says, 1 Cor 15:43. I beg you+, think of these things, and get comfort against the evil day. And to that end, be sure to get into Christ, so that we may be in Christ, living and dying, and be found in Christ. For what does the Scripture say? “Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord” (Rev 14:13). It is a proof of blessedness to die for the Lord, but if it is not in the Lord, it is to no purpose. If this happiness of dying for the Lord is granted, it is well; “but blessed are the dead who die in the Lord.” Why? “They will rest from their labors.” Death takes them away from their labors. All their good works go to heaven with them. So says the Spirit, whatever the flesh says. And there is no resting until that time. Their life is full of troubles and encumbrances; and therefore labor to get assurance that we are in Christ, that we may be in Christ, and die in Christ, and then “there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ.”

How stupefied we are to put off preparation for death until it comes! He who forgets Christ, and getting into Christ, all his lifetime, it is God’s just judgment that he should forget himself in death. We see how a villain who has no care for his own life may have power over another man’s life.

And therefore labor to be grafted into Christ by faith; and labor that we may know it by the Spirit of Christ prevailing in us over our natural corruptions more and more. As the apostle says, “There is no condemnation for those who are in Christ”; for the spirit of life, “the law of the Spirit of life which is in Christ has set me free from the law of sin and death” (Rom 8:2), the condemning law of sin. If the law of the spirit of life which is in Christ the head is in us in any measure, it frees us from the condemning law of sin, so that it does not carry us wherever it would. Then we may say with comfort, “There is no condemnation for those who are in Christ”; for the law of the spirit of life in Christ has freed us from the condemning, tyrannizing law of sin and death.

Sin has no law. It is in us as a subdued rebel, but it does not set up a throne. Some hope to be saved by Christ, and yet they set up a throne for sin in the soul. Sin tells them to defile themselves, and they must obey it. This is a woeful condition! How can they expect to die in the Lord? Only those can who are freed by the law of the spirit of life. New lords, new laws. When kings conquer, they bring in fundamental laws; and when we are taken from Satan’s kingdom into the kingdom of Christ, the fundamental laws are then altered. Christ by his Spirit sets up a law of believing, and praying, and doing good, and abstaining from evil. The law of the spirit of life frees us from the law of sin and death. I beg you+, enlarge on these things in your+ thoughts. They are things we must all have ready beforehand, against the evil day. It should be comforting and useful to us all to hear that our enemy, our greatest enemy, death, is swallowed up in victory. And yet there is more comfort in the text.

The Fifth Sermon

“And all tears will be wiped away from all faces.—Isa 25:8.”

Not only will death be swallowed up in victory, but God “will wipe away all tears from all eyes.” Religion will be religion; good things will be good things. Nothing will pass under false names. All tears will be wiped away. We have many causes of tears now. In the world there is a continual raising of clouds that distill into drops of tears. Even if we had nothing outside us to raise a vapor to be distilled in tears, we are able to raise up mists from our own mists, from our own doubts and conflicts within.

As we should weep for our own sins, so we should weep for the sins of others. We may see this in Jeremiah, where the prophet says, “Oh, that my head were a fountain of tears, that I might weep continually for the sins of my people” (Jer 9:1). And indeed good men are quick to weep, as the heathen man observes. They are quick to lament, not only for their own sins, but for the sins and misery of another.

As for our blessed Savior himself, we never read that he laughed. We have heard that he wept, and for his very enemies: “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem” (Matt 23:37). He shed tears for those who shed his blood. Tears were chief evidences of Christ’s sweetness of disposition, as was his becoming man, and a curse, and dying for us, and his making so much of little children, and calling to him all who were weary and heavy laden, and never refusing anyone who came to him. That he wept especially for the miseries and afflictions showed his gracious and sweet disposition. And in heaven he is so full of sympathy in glory that when Paul persecuted the church, he said, “Why do you persecute Me?” (Acts 9:4). So though he is free from suffering in heaven, he is not free from compassion, from sympathy with his church. And so every child of God is ready to grieve not only for his own sins and the misery that follows them, but for the sins and miseries of others. “My eyes shed streams of tears,” says the prophet David (Ps 119:136), when he saw that men broke the law of God, whom he loved.

A true natural child takes the disgrace of his father to heart. If we are not grieved to see our father disgraced, we are illegitimate children, not sons. Those who make a sport of sin, what are they? Alas! they do not have one spark of the spirit of adoption. They are not children, who rejoice at what they should grieve at.

So St Paul says, “I have often told you+, and now say again even with tears: Many live as enemies of the cross of Christ” (Phil 3:18). When he saw some men preach against the cross of Christ, and others live as its enemies, whose end is damnation, he told them of it weeping.

We have cause, therefore, to mourn for the sins of others and for the miseries of others, whether we consider God, or the church, or ourselves. First, the love of God moves us to weep when we see him dishonored.

Second, if we love the church, we should mourn for any sins that may harm their salvation.

Does it not move any man to pity to see an ox go to the slaughter? to see a man otherwise gifted going to the slaughter by sinning against his conscience? to see a habitual swearer, an unclean person, a profane wretch, covering himself with pride as with a garment, scorning God and the world and all? Can a Christian look on this, see flesh and blood like himself, under the gospel, in a cursed condition that cannot be escaped without serious repentance, and not be affected by it? Can a man see a poor donkey fall under a burden and not help to lift it up, and yet see a man falling to hell and not be affected by it? So we see we have cause enough for tears. And as there is cause, so we should feel it. We ought to take the afflictions of Joseph to heart. He is a dead man who has no feeling of this kind. If we go to the body and state, or anything about a man, there is cause for grief. Does not every member have many diseases? And are not our lives a kind of hospital, some sick of one thing, some of another? But as there is cause, we should feel it. We are flesh and not stones. It is therefore a foolish opinion to be wooden and brutish, as if to outface sorrow and grief were a glory.

Use 1. When our Savior was sent into the world, Christi dolor, dolor maximus [the grief of Christ was the greatest grief]; there would be no patience without feeling. Away, then, with that iron, that flinty philosophy that thinks it a virtue to be unfeeling, and, as the apostle says, “without natural affection” (Rom 1:31). He counts it the greatest judgment of God on the soul, yet they would have it a virtue. Why should I strike them any more? says God; they have no sense, no feeling (Isa 1:5).

The proud philosopher thought it was not philosophical to weep, a proud Stoic disposition; but Christians desire it.

And therefore we ought to labor to be more feeling, so that we may make our peace and reverence the justice of God, and be more aware of him afterward. It is most true that Sapiens miser, plus miser: the wiser any man is, the more he feels misery. And therefore, of all men, the best men have the most grief, because they have the quickest senses. They are not made numb with insensibility and hardness, to bear it bravely, as the world does; but they take in the cause of grief with grief. And as they have a more sanctified judgment than other men, so they have a wiser affection of love and a livelier life of grace. Where there is life, there is feeling; and where there is a clear sight or cause of grief, there is most grief. Therefore the best men have the most grief, because they are the most judicious, the most loving.

Then, of all others, they have the most grace to bear it out. Therefore, considering there is cause of grief continually in ourselves and in others, we ought to labor to feel it; otherwise it would be no favor to have tears wiped away.

So there is cause for tears, and tears are a duty of Christians who feel the cause, both of sin and of misery, in themselves and in others.

Use 2. And as it is an unavoidable grief, so it is good that we should grieve. We must stoop to God’s course, we must bring our hearts to it, and pray that, since our needs and sins call for this dispensation and we must be under correction, he will make us feel his rod; and that he would make good his covenant of grace, “to take away our stony hearts, and give us hearts of flesh” (Ezek 11:19), so that we may feel.

Most graces are founded on affection, and all graces are nothing but affections sanctified. What would become of grace if we had no affections? Therefore, as there is cause for grief, and for tears from grief, we ought to grieve. It is a condition and a duty: a condition following misery, and a duty following our condition.

Beware of what hinders a feeling of troubles and judgments, that is, hardness of heart, forgetfulness, and trying to put away sorrow with sin. For we ought to feel, and ought to labor to feel, to know the meaning of every cross in ourselves and others.

But suppose we have crosses, and we must feel them; then it follows that “God will wipe away all tears from our eyes.” Is there nothing for the present, no ground of comfort? Yes. As we ought to feel grief, so we ought to feel what there is to rejoice in for the present, especially if we consider the time to come. The life of a Christian is a strange kind of life. He ought to grieve, and he ought to rejoice. He has occasion for both, and he ought to receive both; for what we ought to aim at especially is joy, and if we grieve, it is so that afterward we may rejoice. We must feel any affliction so that we may rejoice afterward, and we ought to labor for it. For is not the joy of the Lord our strength? Are we not fit to do service when our spirits are most enlarged? And is it not a credit to religion when we walk in the comfort of the Holy Spirit? Is it not an offense when we droop under the cross? We ought to feel, yet not so much that we forget matter of joy and comfort. And therefore, as we ought to grieve, so we ought, when we have grieved, to keep up the soul with the consideration of joy for the present as much as we can; indeed, to pick out matter of comfort from the very cross. That is the heart of a Christian, not only to rejoice in other matters, but to pick comfort out of grief. God allows me to fall into this or that condition: it is a fruit of his fatherly love. He might allow me to run the broad way, to be given up to a reprobate mind and a hard heart, but he does not do so. Pick out matter of comfort from grief.

Then consider the presence of God in it. Indeed, I have matter of grief, but I find God moderating it. It might be far worse; it is his mercy that I am not consumed. I find God doing me good by it; I find myself better for it; I cannot well be without it. Who would not labor to feel a cross when he looks up to God’s cross, and justice, and mercy? He has more cause to rejoice than to grieve in the very cross itself.

But especially mark what the Holy Spirit says here. We ought not to be cast down too much by any cross, considering that God “will wipe away all tears from our eyes,” that is, all natural tears, and the miseries of this life. There will be no more misery, no more sickness, no more trouble.

And then all tears that arise from the consideration of sin, and of misery following sin. Death is the accomplishment of all mortification. It is a comfort that we will not always lead this conflicting life, but the war between the flesh and the spirit will be ended; the sense of it will be removed. One day we will be out of Satan’s reach and the world’s reach, which is a great comfort to consider. Whatever the cause is, the cause will be removed before long. If the cause is desertion, because God leaves us comfortless, we will be with the Lord forever hereafter. If the cause is separation from friends, why, we will all meet together before long, and be in heaven forever. If the cause is our own sins, we will cease hereafter to offend God, and Christ will be all in all. Now sin is almost all in all; sin and corruption hold great sway in us. If the matter of our grief is the sins of others and the afflictions of others, there is no sin in heaven: “nothing unclean will enter there” (Rev 21:27). The souls of perfect men are there, and all are of one mind. There is no opposition to goodness; there all will go one way. There, however little they can agree here, all will have mutual solace and contentment in one another, they in us and we in them, and that forever. You+ cannot name or imagine a cause of tears that will not be removed there. Indeed, the more tears we have shed here, the more comfort we will have. As our troubles are increased here, our consolation will increase. What we suffer here, if it is for a good cause, will work for us “an eternal weight of glory that is far beyond comparison” (2 Cor 4:17). We say April showers bring forth May flowers. It is a common saying, from the experience of common life. It is true in religion. The more tears we shed in the April of our lives, the more sweet comfort we will have hereafter. If no tears are shed here, no flowers are to be gathered there. And therefore, besides deliverance from trouble, here is comfort: God will take away all cause of grief, and all kinds of grief whatever.

And therefore think of it in this way.

The next thing to be considered is the order. First we must shed tears, and then they must be wiped away. After a storm, a calm; after sowing in tears comes reaping in joy. What is the reason for that order? Reason 1. The reason is our own necessity. Since the fall we are in such a frame and condition that we cannot be put into a good frame of grace without much pain. The truths of God must cross us, and afflictions must join with them. For the sins contracted by pleasure must be dissolved by pain. Repentance must cost us tears. We may thank ourselves if we have brought ourselves into a sinful course. As for the necessity of this order, a diseased person must not be cured until he feels some of the pain of his wounds.

Reason 2. Again, consider that it is for our increase of comfort afterward that God will have us shed tears and then have our tears wiped away, because we feel joy and comfort more after sorrow. We cannot feel the joys of heaven unless we feel the contrary here. And therefore, of all men, heaven will be most heaven to those who have had their portion of crosses and afflictions here. First, therefore, tears are shed, and then they must be wiped away, because joy is then most felt. As it is with the wickedest of all men, they are most miserable who have been happiest, because their soul is enlarged by their happiness to take in sorrow more quickly and keenly; so those who have been most miserable here will have most joy hereafter.

Use 1. Now for use. Here is not only the mercy of God in Christ, but his tender mercy: though our life is full of tears, which we have brought on ourselves, yet God stoops so low as to wipe our eyes, like a father or mother. His mercy is a sweet and tender mercy. And, as the psalmist says, when we are sick “he makes our beds in our sickness” (Ps 41:3). Christ will come and serve those who watch and serve him; indeed, he will wait on them and “sup with them” (Rev 3:20). He is not only mercy and goodness, but there is in him tender mercy. He not only gives matter of joy and comfort, but he will act like a tender-hearted mother, wiping away all tears from our eyes. We cannot grasp the tenderness of God’s love, the pity and mercy of God toward those who are his and are afflicted in the world, especially in a good cause. However many their tears are, if they are penitent tears, he will wipe them all away.

And since we must shed tears here so that we may be comforted hereafter, let us take care that in this life we do not judge by sight, but by faith. “If we live by sight, we are of all men most wretched” (1 Cor 15:19). In the world the children of God are most miserable, and of the children of God, the best saints. Who has more cause for tears than the best saints? It is only seedtime here. While seedtime continues, there are tears. The farmer, while it is seedtime, cannot do his work without trouble. The minister cannot do his work without being forced to take the sins of the times to heart, and to see his work go backward. Governors of families and such carry their seed weeping. Indeed, the best men at times cannot do good without trouble in themselves and a conflict with corruptions. No good is sown here but it is sown in tears; yet take no offense at this: “God will wipe away all tears.”

The Head of the church, our blessed Savior, and all his gracious apostles, what a life they lived! The glorious martyrs who sealed the truth with their blood! And therefore, as the apostle says, “If our happiness is here only, we are of all men most miserable” (1 Cor 15:19). If we judge by sight, we will condemn the generation of the righteous. We live by sight when, seeing anyone cast down with the sight of sin, the sense of temptation, or distress of conscience, we think him lost. Oh, beware of that! For those who shed tears here, God will wipe them all away. “Woe to those who laugh now, for they will mourn hereafter” (Luke 6:25). Though we weep here, yet matter of joy enough will spring up hereafter. “Afflictions will yield a quiet fruit of righteousness to those who are trained by them” (Heb 12:11). We may not see their fruits at once, but afterward. And therefore let us not be discouraged by anything we can suffer here, or for the church, if we see her under pressure. As darkness is sown for the wicked, and the foundation of their eternal torment is laid in their joy, so the ground and foundation of all a godly man’s joy is laid in tears. “Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted” (Matt 5:4). Yet for the present there is more matter of joy than of grief, if we look with both eyes. For we ought to have double eyes, one to feel our grief, as we must, the other to see our comfort, so that we are not overwhelmed by grief. There is a sorrow to death, an excessive sorrow. It is unthankfulness to God to forget our comforts, as it is unfeeling to forget our sorrow. Take us at the worst: do we not have more cause for joy than for sorrow? Notice Rom 5:1: “Since we have been justified through faith, we have peace with God,” and “we rejoice in the hope of glory.” Indeed, afterward he says, “we also rejoice in tribulations.” And why? On what ground? “Knowing that tribulations bring proven character, and proven character hope, and hope does not put us to shame.” Now we rejoice in God reconciled in Christ. So, just as we ought to look with one eye on the grief, so that we may have ground to exercise grace, which we are not capable of without feeling, so we must look to the grounds of joy. Our life is woven of matter of sorrow and joy; and as it is woven of both, our affections should feel both, so that they may better take in the grounds of comfort.

When the day of persecution approaches, this will comfort us, for our life is a valley of tears; and are we not to go through this valley of tears to this mountain where all tears will be wiped away from all eyes? When we are dejected by the loss of any friend, they say, as Christ said to the women, “Do not weep for Me” (Luke 23:28). They are happy, “and all tears are wiped away from their eyes.” And therefore, as it is matter of comfort while we live, so it is ground of comfort when we die. For there is occasion of sorrow in death, in parting with friends and the comforts of this world. Then tears are shed in more abundance, and then we think back on former sins, and there is a renewing of repentance more than at other times; yet then we are near the time of joy, and nearest the fulfillment of the promise that “all tears will be wiped away.”

And so you+ have the whole state of a Christian life: an afflicted condition. Yes, but it is a comforting condition. The more afflictions here, the more comfort here, but especially hereafter. The life of a carnal man is all in misery. If he falls to joy, he is all joy; if to sorrow, he is all sorrow. He has nothing to hold him up. He is like Nabal: he sinks like a piece of lead to the bottom of the sea (1 Sam 25:37, 38); like Ahithophel, down he goes (2 Sam 17:23). When he is in a merry mood, he is nothing but joy. But a Christian’s state and disposition are both mixed. He has ground of sorrow for his own sins, and for the sins and miseries of the times. So he has matter of comfort for the present, in the favor of God, in the pardon of sins, in the presence of God, in God’s delivering him from trouble. He has special ground of joy in the hope of glory in the time to come. Therefore, as we have a mixed state, let us labor for a mixed disposition, and labor to be in a joyful frame: to grieve in such a way that we raise matter of joy out of it. And when we would rejoice, let us grieve first, for joy is sown in grief. The best method of joy is to take away all that disturbs our joy. Search the bottom of the heart! See what sin is unconfessed, unrepented of! Spread it before God; ask God to pardon it and to seal the pardon! When our souls are searched to the bottom, then out of that sorrow springs joy; and out of these sighs and groans that cannot be expressed comes joy unspeakable and full of glory. If a man would be joyful, let him labor to weep first, so that what interrupts his joy may be taken away. Those who would be joyful, and will not search to the bottom, must be brought back to sorrow with shame. When we would rejoice in earnest, let us judge ourselves, so that we may not be judged by the Lord. Let us mourn for our sins, and then lay hold on the promise that “all who mourn for sin will be comforted” (Matt 5:4). And blessed are those who shed tears here, for all tears will be wiped away.

We are apt to wrong ourselves, both good and bad: for the good think, if they are in misery, that they will always be so; the bad, if they are in prosperity, that they will always be so, and they bless themselves in it. Now the joy of the hypocrites is like the “crackling of thorns” (Eccl 7:6), and the grief of the godly lasts only a short while. And therefore let not the wicked fool themselves with groundless hopes, nor the godly vex themselves with needless fears; but put away the fancy that troubles will continue long. Time is short, and before long God “will wipe away all tears from our eyes.” No mists, no clouds, will reach to heaven. The state in heaven will be like the state of the heavens, where there is no cloud, but all is pure, all serene. Therefore in Christianity consider not the beginning of things but their ends. “Mark the end of the upright, for the end of the upright is peace” (Ps 37:37). Ways take their praise from the point at which they end. “If by any means I may attain to the resurrection of the dead,” says Paul (Phil 3:11). Through thick and thin, fair and foul, rough winds, a dry or a bloody death: if by any means I may come to the resurrection of the dead, the first degree of glory, all is well. It is a good way that ends well. Non qua, sed quo: consider not by what way he brings us to heaven, but where he brings us. If he brings us to heaven through a valley of tears, it does not matter; for in heaven “all tears will be wiped from our eyes.” And therefore Christianity is called wisdom. “And this wisdom is justified by her children” (Matt 11:19). What is the chief point of wisdom? To look home to the end, and to direct all means to that end. He is wise who is wise for eternity. The wicked will have their payment here. “But woe to those who laugh, for they will mourn,” says Christ (Luke 6:25). They will not wait for ground of joy hereafter, but will have present payment. But though the ways of Christians are foul and wet with tears, yet blessed are they; for God “will wipe away all tears from their eyes.” “Comfort one another with these words” (1 Thess 4:18).

The Sixth Sermon

“And He will swallow up death in victory, and God will wipe away tears from all faces, so that the rebukes of His people may be taken away from the earth; for the Lord has spoken it.—Isa 25:8.”

You+ have heard before of a feast provided for God’s people, its founder being God himself, who alone can truly comfort the soul and the conscience (which is what most especially needs comfort), since he is above the conscience. The place where the feast is kept is “Mount Zion,” the church of God. The delicacies are described as “fat things, wine refined on the lees,” the best of the best that can be thought of, which is Christ with all his benefits; he is bread indeed and drink indeed, who cherishes and nourishes the soul to everlasting life. And so that there should be nothing to disturb the celebration of the feast, he promises to “destroy the face of the covering,” “to take away the veil spread over all nations,” the veil of ignorance and unbelief, to shine on the soul and fill it full of knowledge and heavenly comfort. And because there can be no comfort where death is feared, death being the greatest enemy in this life, he will therefore “swallow up death in victory,” and all that makes way for death or attends it. And when death is taken away, all its attendants vanish with it: “God will wipe away all tears from all faces.” Because the best things do not have the best welcome in the world, nor the best persons either, God promises that the rebukes of his people will be taken away from off the earth; what they are, they will be known to be. These are very great matters, and therefore there is a great confirmation; they have a seal. And what is that? “The Lord has spoken it.”

Last time I showed that God’s children will shed tears, and that they have cause to do so. I will now enlarge on it a little.

It is the condition of men since the fall. In paradise, before, there was no cause of tears; nothing was out of joint, all was in order. There was no sin, therefore no sorrow, therefore no sense of sorrow. And so in heaven there will be no tears, because there is no cause for them; they will be as far from heaven as their cause. This life is a valley of tears, a life of misery, and therefore we shed tears here. And we lack no cause for them as long as there is sin in the world, and sorrow, and the misery that follows sin: our own sins and the sins of others, our own miseries and the miseries of others. And surely a child of God finds this the greatest cause of mourning in this world, that he has a principle in him always troubling him in the service of God. He cannot serve God with the cheerfulness he would. His unfeelingness, that he cannot feel so deeply that God is dishonored by himself and others, is his burden. He is grieved that he cannot grieve enough. He can find tears for other things, matter enough for them, as the heathen man could say. A man loses his goods and has tears for them, but has to force tears for other things which are the true ground of grief. A child of God has a remainder of corruption, which pushes him on to offend against God and hinders him in his service, in the freedom and cheerfulness of it. And this he complains of with Paul and others: “What a wretched man I am!” not for his affliction, though that was great, but “Who will rescue me from this body of death?” (Rom 7:24).

Case 1. I will add a case here. Some say they cannot weep, but they can grieve; is it necessary, then, to weep or not? Tears are taken for the spring of tears; grief, all grief, will be taken away. Tears are only the messengers of grief; and often the deepest understanding, which takes things deeply, cannot express itself in tears. In some, the passages that carry the thought to the heart are more tender, so that they can weep. Now, the grief of a Christian is a grief of the judgment, a rational grief. Not only must felt tears have a felt grief, but a Christian’s grief is a felt grief and a grief of the judgment. He has a right judgment of the things that cause sorrow, and wills it; and tears are only an expression of it.

But how will I know whether grief is right or not? There are tears God has no bottle for. “You put my tears in Your bottle” (Ps 56:8). He makes much of them. They are vinum angelicum [the wine of angels], as he says. God is an angel to his people, to wipe away their tears. But there are some tears God has no bottle for: hypocritical tears, Delilah’s tears, tears of revenge and anger, Esau’s tears. And therefore the true tears that God will wipe away are, first of all, such as follow our condition here, our misery. God will wipe them away. If we speak of tears from a ground in the judgment,

  1. The spring of true tears is the love of God, and of Christ, and of his church, and the love of the state of Christianity. Tears spring from love, these tears especially.

    Oh! a Christian takes it to heart that God should be so badly used in the world; that Christ, the Savior of the world, should meet with such treatment; that he himself should have anything in him that should offend such a Savior! This unkindness stings him to the heart. He takes it hard that God should be abused. Laetitia habet suas lachrymas: there is not only grief, which is the immediate cause of tears, but another cause before it, that is, love. Joy likewise has its tears, though they are not chiefly meant here.

  2. Again, tears are good and sound when we weep for our own sins as well as for the sins and miseries of others. And I will go further: we must weep for the sins of others as well as for our own. For it is a greater sign of the truth of grace to take the sins of others to heart more than our own. You+ will say this is something of a paradox. But a man may often take his own sins to heart as a matter of terror of conscience: not as sins, as contrary to God, set against him, opposed to the state of the soul, not as sin is properly sin; he is grieved and troubled over sin because it brings him trouble and terror of conscience. When a man can take the sins of another to heart, and truly, as an offense against his good God and a crucifying again of his sweet Savior, these are true tears indeed. It is a greater sign of grace than weeping for a man’s own sins.

    Some are so taken up with terrors of conscience that they let their children, family, and friends alone. Their heart is eaten up with self-love, and they are nearly eaten up with their own terrors of conscience. But here is true grief and a hatred of sin in the right respect, when it exercises itself on others as well as on ourselves.

  3. Again, tears arise from the right spring, from true grief, when we can weep in secret. Oh! says Jeremiah, if you+ do so and so, “My soul will weep in secret because of your+ pride” (Jer 13:17). Here was a good soul indeed. Many will have tears of comfort in public. Yes, but when they can weep in secret for their own sins and the sins of others, it is evidence that their grief comes from the right spring.

  4. Again, when tears lead to the reform of what they grieve for; otherwise they are steriles lachrymae, barren tears. Do they lead us to reform what we weep for? Do they lead to action? Affections are good when they carry us to action; grief, love, joy are all for action. When we weep and grieve, and reform as well, it is a good sign. I will name no more. You+ see, then, that grief is sound when it springs from the love of God, and is for the sins of others as well as our own, and for our own as well as others’; when it stirs us up to reform; when it is in secret. And therefore let us examine our grief by these and similar evidences. It will be a good mark of a gracious soul. Then God will act as a sweet nurse, or a loving mother to her child who sheds tears. God will “wipe away all these tears.” Oh, the surpassing love of God! His love is a tender love: the love of a mother, the love of a nurse! It is not love only, but the very tenderness of love, tender mercy and compassion. How low he stoops to wipe away the tears of his children! “God will wipe away all tears.”

I will raise one more question, and then go on. But we are told to rejoice always. Why then are we required to weep and mourn? Can two opposites stand together?

Case 2. I answer: very well. For we may grieve, as we have matter of grief and are in a condition of grief; and we may rejoice, and ought to rejoice, as we look to the promise that God “will wipe away all tears.” When we think of the present cause, we cannot help but grieve; but when we look beyond all troubles, we cannot help but rejoice; it has an influence of joy on our heart. Indeed, for the present we may at times rejoice and grieve without looking to eternity. If we consider that we have offended God, done what grieves his Spirit, that is matter of grief. But when we consider that we have Christ at God’s right hand, who speaks peace for us and makes our peace by virtue of his mediation, that gives comfort. So we have cause of joy and cause of grief about the same things at the same time.

We are never in such a state of grief here that there is no cause of joy, if we look about us, look forward, look upward. A Christian, that is, a good Christian, is a person who has many things to look after, so that he may manage his state of Christianity wisely. He is to look to himself and his sins, to the mercies of God in Christ and their constancy, which answers to their fruit in peace and joy here and happiness hereafter, which are constant too. God’s grace, like God himself, is constant, its fruits constant. Therefore “rejoice always.” And the apostle says, “I know what I say, I say it advisedly: rejoice always” (Phil 4:4). So the life of a Christian is a mixed life; indeed, the ground of our joy is our sorrow and grief, and joy is sown in grief. If we would rejoice indeed, let us mourn indeed. True joy arises and springs out of sorrow.

I go on to the next point. “And the rebukes of His people will be taken away from off the face of the earth.” Another benefit that makes the feast sweet and comforting is this: “He will take away the rebukes of His people.” And here the same method is to be used: that God’s children, his church and people, are under rebukes and under reproach. We need not stop to prove the truth of it. It is true, first, that the head of the church, and the church itself, and every particular member go under rebukes. As for the head of the church, we would spend the time to no purpose proving it. What was Christ’s life? It was under a veil. He did not appear to be what he was. You+ know he was thought the chief of devils, an enemy to his prince, to Caesar. I will not spend time on clear truths.

As for the church itself, you+ see in the book of Esther 3:8, “There is a strange people that acknowledge no law; they are against the laws of the prince.” They pass under the charge of being rebels. The poor church, which had thoughts of peace, the meek church of God, they counted enemies of the state, as Christ, the head, was. And so the church in Babylon, under what rebukes was it? They reproached them: “By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept, when they said, ‘Sing us one of the songs of Zion’” (Ps 137:1). The church sits by the waters of Babylon all this life. The world is a kind of Babylon to God’s people, and then: Sing us one of your+ songs. Where is your+ God now? say the hearts of wretched people, when they saw the people of God in disgrace. Cicero could say of the nation of the Jews, “It shows how God regards it; it has been conquered so often.” So the heathen man could scorn the state of God’s people. You+ see how the psalmist complains in the name of particular Christians: “Where is his God? He trusted in Him; let Him save him” (Ps 22:8). Oh, this was daggers to David’s heart. “It pierced my heart when they said, ‘Where is your God?’” (Ps 42:10). To touch a Christian in his God, as if God had no care for him, is more than his own grief and affliction. So it is when a child of God is rebuked and affronted, when religion must suffer by it. So the head of the church and the members of the church are under rebukes, as could be proved if I took you+ through all the histories.

Today you+ see what the church of the Jews has come to, and under what reproach the nation of the Jews lies. And surely this prophecy aims partly at the conversion of the Jews. It will be fulfilled at the resurrection, when all tears will be perfectly wiped away. But it also bears on the conversion of the Jews. In what state are they now? Are they not a byword of reproach? Moses’s words come true in them: “They will be a hissing to all nations” (Deut 28:37). And is it not a proverb, “Hated like a Jew”?

Reason. But what is the reason for it? Not to dwell long on the point, you+ know there are two seeds in the world, the seed of the serpent and the seed of the woman; and the enmity between them is the true ground of it, and the antipathy in the hearts of carnal men to goodness. There is a light that shines in the life of those who are good, and those who are evil hate the light, as it reveals them to themselves and to the world not to be what they seem to be. There is a saltiness in the truth. It is savory, but it is tart, whether in the word preached or however else truth lays open what is contrary to corruption. And from this, pride and self-love in carnal men schemes how to overcast, as far as they can, the names of those who are better than themselves with a cloud of disgrace. It is the property of vile men to make all others vile, so that they may be alike. Men cannot bear distinctions of one from another. The Scripture distinguishes the “righteous man, more excellent than his neighbor” (Prov 12:26), but they will not have that. The hatred of distinction is the reason they make everyone as bad as they can. And this is why good things were never clothed in the right dress, nor evil things either; both pass under a veil. Take away the true garment of grace and holiness and goodness and put a false veil on it, and it does not pass in this world for what it is, because wicked men will not allow it, but will raise up the credit of other things, of empty learning, or empty things, or vain courses, and cry up the credit of worldly things, so that those who are carried after those things may seem to be wise and not fools. The best things never had the happiness of passing under their own names, but had other coverings. Truth always goes with a torn and scratched face; it is a stranger in the world and meets with a stranger’s welcome.

Use 1. If this is so, we ought to beware of bringing offense or reproach on religion. Salvian complains that in his time wickedness had grown so strong that those who were good and honorable, mali esse volunt, ne a malis abhorreantur: those who were good strove to be vile, so that they might not be vilified by others. “Oh,” he says, “how much Christ owes the world, that those who acknowledge him, and acknowledge goodness, and acknowledge his cause, should be counted base for that very reason, because they are his friends!” Beware of taking offense.

Use 2. We need to be wise, so that we are not caught in this snare of Satan, mistaking error for truth and good for evil. Satan and his agents make things pass for their opposites. Superstition passes for religion, and religion for superstition, schism, and heresy. It has always been so. Therefore seek wisdom to discern rightly. The devil has two properties: he is a liar and a murderer. The one makes way for the other, for he could not murder unless he lied. The devil himself will not be an open murderer if he can help it. Fraudulent persecution is worse than violent persecution. If he can bring men to hell by fraud and lying, he will never do it by violence. He is a liar so that he may be a murderer; for when he can raise a charge against the church and children of God, that they are rebels, enemies of the state, then he may be a murderer cum privilegio [with license]. When he has tainted God’s people in the opinion of the world, they are treated not as they deserve, but as they are thought to deserve, once other men’s opinion of them is poisoned. “Oh, people everywhere are speaking against this sect,” they say to Paul (Acts 28:22). Therefore we need to be wise; for if the instruments of Satan, led by his spirit, had not hoped that slanders would take, they would never have become so skillful in that trade. But they know they will find some shallow fools who will believe them without looking into them deeply, and will judge persons and things by prejudice. It is enough for these fools that this is said of them. Following their lusts, they have neither the sense nor the judgment, nor even the patience, to examine them; and that is what makes them as mad as they are. Calumniare audacter, aliquid haerebit, slander boldly, something will stick; they are sure of it. What has raised and ruined many a man is Haman’s way of casting suspicion on those who are better than themselves. That was Haman’s trick, and it will be the practice of the wicked to the end of the world, as it has been from the beginning. “You are no friend of Caesar,” they say, and that is enough for Pilate (John 19:12). So it has been, and so it will be to the end of the world. Therefore we need to be wise, so that we are not misled. Men will never stop speaking evil till they have learned to speak better, till the Spirit of God has taught them.

Now, it is said that Christ will take away the rebukes of his people. That is the promise. They will be known to be what they are. He will set all in joint again. Harmony is a sweet thing, and order is a sweet thing. The time will come when things that now appear out of order will all be set in their due order again. Those who are basest will be lowest, and those who are excellent will be highest. This is being worked out and shaped now. In this confusion we must look to the catastrophe, the conclusion of all. He will “take away the rebukes of all.” God is the father of truth, and truth is the daughter of time. Time will bring forth truth at last. And those who are truly honorable will be held in honor. It is as true as God is just; for goodness and holiness are beams of God, and will he allow them always to pass under a false veil? There is no attribute of God that will not shine forth gloriously, all his excellence and dignity. Nothing will be above him and his excellence. No, though for a while he seems not to rule in the world, or to have power, but allows his enemies to get away with it, he is working something else by allowing them: he is working the glory of his children and the downfall of his enemies. There is nothing in God that will not shine gloriously, and nothing in his children, no beams of God, that will not shine gloriously, to the shame of the world. Those who are good will be known to be good; God will bring their righteousness to light. The witnesses who vexed the world and were basely treated were slain and disgraced, but they rose again and were carried up to heaven (Rev 11:12), like Elijah. So there will be a resurrection of name, a resurrection of reputation. What is good will be good, and what is bad will be bad. It will be known for what it is. This is for comfort.

Use 1. You+ hear, therefore, what course to take under disgrace. What shall we do when the church is in disgrace, as it is now? To the railing papists a Protestant is worse than a Turk or a Jew. Among ourselves we see how the best things are regarded. It is too well known to need speaking of. And the offense taken from this hardens men extremely. It keeps men from religion; it draws many away from religion who have entered into it, because they have not learned enough self-denial to risk disgrace. And surely where there is no self-denial there is no religion. Christ knew what doctrine he was teaching when he taught self-denial in this respect.

What then shall we do? 1. First of all, labor for innocence, so that if men reproach us, they reproach us without cause.

  1. Then labor for a spirit of patience to serve Christ with. “Great is your+ reward when men speak evil of you+” (Matt 5:12), for a good cause. It is the portion of a Christian in this life to do well and suffer ill. Of all men, certainly those are best who, out of love for goodness, are carried to goodness without looking to rewards or disgrace, and who follow it with a single eye. Labor, therefore, for patience, and not only that, but,
  2. For courage. For the moon goes its course and lets the dog bark. We have a course to run; let us keep our course constantly, and pass through good reports and bad reports; let us be settled, whatever the world thinks. We seek applause in another theater than the world.
  3. Again, then, labor for sincerity under rebukes, so that we have a good aim, such an aim as Paul had: “If I am mad and out of my wits” (2 Cor 5:13, 14). Because he was earnest for his Master, Christ, they counted him out of his wits. If I am out of my wits, it is for Christ. “If I am sober, it is for you+; the love of Christ compels me to be so” (2 Cor 5:14). Get the love of Christ, and that will make a man care for nothing. If I go beyond myself, it is to God. As David said, when he was mocked by Michal for dancing before the ark, “It is to the Lord” (2 Sam 6:20, 21). Bonus ludus, a good dance, where Michal scoffs and David dances. Where gracious men magnify God and have Michals to scoff at them, it is bonus ludus. God will look on them, for it is to the Lord. Let us labor that our aims are good, and then it does not matter what the world thinks of them.
  4. And when none of this will do, let us commend our good name to God in prayer. As we commend our souls and conditions, so let us commend our reputations to him, that he would take care of them, that he would bring our righteousness to light, that it may shine out like the noonday. So David does: he complains to God and commits all to him, and asks him to take his side against his enemies and to right his cause. And when we have done that, we have done our duty. Yet let us also hope for better things, and be content to pass through the world as unknown men, and to be inwardly worthy, and to pass as unknown men. Rich men, if they are truly rich, will applaud themselves in their own hearts; though the world disgraces them, they can say, Yet at home I am well provided for. And so it is with a Christian who knows his worth: that he is a child of God, an heir of heaven, that he is attended by angels, that he is a jewel to God in his esteem, absolutely the best thing in the world. He knows the worth of a Christian, and his own worth as a Christian. He applauds and comforts himself because he knows he has a hidden life, a state of glory hidden in Christ. Now it is covered with disgrace and disrespect in the world, scorned and reproached; but what is that to him? It is a hidden life, and for the present he knows his own excellence, and therefore he can pass through good report and bad report. “I do not care for man’s day,” says Paul; “there is another day before which I must stand” (1 Cor 4:3).

And if we do this, as Peter says, “A spirit of glory will rest upon us” (1 Pet 4:14). The ground of comfort we have under rebuke and disgrace is that there is a spirit of glory. What is that? A large spirit, enlarging our hearts with inward comfort, inward joy, inward love of God. “A spirit of glory will rest upon you+,” and will continue with you+ as long as disgrace continues. He sets this against all the disgrace he meets with in the world.

God at times puts such a glory and excellence on his children under disgrace and ill treatment in the world that he daunts the world by it, as Stephen’s face shone like the face of an angel; this came from a spirit of glory that rested on him, and showed him to be the servant of God. He who takes away from our good name, if we are good, adds to our reward. Our Savior Christ says as much: “Blessed are you+ when you+ are spoken ill of, for great is your+ reward” (Matt 5:11, 12).

The Seventh Sermon

“And the rebukes of His people He will take away from all the earth; for the mouth of the Lord has spoken it.—Isa 25:8.”

Use 3. This is a great promise, and I pray you+, be comforted by it. For of all the grief that God’s people suffer in the world, none is greater than reproach, disgrace, and insult. Movemur contumeliis plus quam injuriis, we are more moved by reproaches than by injuries. Injuries come from various causes, but disgrace from an abundance of slighting. There is no man who does not think himself worthy of respect from someone or other. Now, slanders come from an abundance of malice, or else an abundance of contempt; and therefore nothing sticks so much as reproaches, especially because of opinion and imagination, which raise them too high.

Our Savior, Christ, “endured the cross, despising its shame” (Heb 12:2). The shame that vain people cast on religion and the best things, they despise and bear with patience. They knew the cross would not be shaken off; persecution and troubles must be endured; and therefore they “endured the cross, despising its shame.” Now, to bear crosses, take the counsel of the holy apostles: look up to him, consider Christ. And whatever disgrace we may endure in words or treatment, we are sure, though we will never know it till we feel it by experience, that “the spirit of glory will rest on us,” and rebuke will be taken away.

Before long there will be no glory in heaven and earth but the glory of Christ and of his spouse, for all the rest will be in their own place, as it was said of Judas that “he went to his place” (Acts 1:25). Their proper place is not to domineer, but to be in hell, and before long they will be there. Heaven is the proper element of the saints. That is the place of Christ, the head, and where should the body be but with the head? Where should the spouse be but with the husband? I say this will come to pass: all the wicked will be in their place, and all the godly in theirs with Christ, and then the rebukes of God’s people will be taken away. It is a great matter, and therefore it is sealed with a great confirmation: “The Lord Jehovah has spoken it.” Therefore it must and will be so. “The mouth of the Lord has spoken it.” This is not added in vain, for the Lord knows well enough that we need it if we are to believe such great things: that there is such a feast provided; that there is such a victory over death, our last enemy; that there will be such glory that all the glory will be Christ’s and his spouse’s; that the wicked who are now so insolent will be cast into their proper place with the devil, by whose spirit they are led. These are great matters, and there is a great disproportion between the present condition and that condition in heaven; and since unbelief is in the soul, it is hard to fasten such things on the soul, that such great things should be done. But they are no greater than God has said, and he is able to make good his word. “The Lord has said it,” and when God has said it, heaven and earth cannot unsay it. When heaven has decreed it, earth and hell cannot annul it. “The mouth of the Lord has spoken it”; that is, truth itself, which cannot lie, has spoken it. A man may lie and still be a man, and an honorable man too. He may sometimes speak an untruth; it does not take away his nature. But God, who is pure truth, unchangeable truth, truth itself, cannot lie.

When we hear of great matters (and the matters of Christianity are great matters; they are as large as the capacity of the soul, and larger too, and yet the soul is large in understanding and in affection too), when we hear of such large matters, we need a great faith to believe them. Great faith needs great grounds, and therefore it is good to have all the help we can. When we hear of great things promised, great deliverances, great glory, let us remember, to strengthen our faith, that God has spoken them. He knows our weakness, our infirmity, and therefore helps us with this prop: “The mouth of the Lord has spoken it.” Let us therefore remember that these great things are promised in the word of God, in the word of Jehovah, who can make them all good, who gives being to all his promises. He is being itself, and gives being to whatever he says. He is able to do it. Set God and his power against all opposition of any kind from created things, and against all the doubts that may arise from our own unbelieving hearts: “The mouth of the Lord has spoken it.”

Question. But you+ will say, the prophet Isaiah says it; the words were his.

Answer. I answer, Isaiah was the penman, God the mouth. The head dictates, the hand writes. Christ the head dictates, and his servant writes. So holy men wrote as they were inspired by the Holy Spirit, a better spirit than their own. “Why do you+ look at me?” says Isaiah. Do not think it is I who say it; I am only a man like yourselves+; but “the mouth of the Lord has spoken it.”

We should not regard men, nor the ministry of men, but consider who speaks by men, who sends them, with what commission they come. Ambassadors are not regarded for themselves, but for those who send them. And therefore Cornelius said well, “We are here in the presence of God to hear what you will speak in the name of God” (Acts 10:33). And so people should come with that reverent expression: We have come into the presence of God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, into the presence of the blessed angels, to hear what you will say in the name of God, by the Spirit of God. We are not dealing with men, but with God. And therefore he says, “The mouth of the Lord has spoken it.”

Question 2. Hence this question may easily be answered: From where does the Scripture have its authority?

Answer. Why, from itself. It is the word; it carries its own credentials with it. Should God borrow authority from men? No; the authority the word has is from itself. It has a supreme authority from itself. And so we may answer the question about the judge of all controversies: What is the supreme Judge? The word, the Spirit of God in the Scriptures. And who is above God? It is a shameless, ridiculous impudence in men to set themselves up as judges of Scripture, as if a man would climb onto the throne and sit there as judge. Before long the Scriptures must judge all, even that great antichrist. Now an ignorant man, a simple man, who perhaps never read the Scriptures, must judge all controversies; indeed, he must judge what is the judge of all and of himself, the word, which comes from the very mouth of God.

Question 3. You+ will ask me, How can I know it is the word of God if the church does not tell us?

Answer. A carrier tells us that these are letters from a certain man, but when we open the letter and see the handwriting and seal, we know they are his. The church knows the word and explains it; and when we see and feel the efficacy of the word in itself, then we believe it to be the word, for there is something in the word that shows it to be the word:

  1. The majesty that is in it.
  2. The matter, which is mysterious: forgiveness of sins through a mystery, forgiveness of sin, victory over death, life everlasting in the world to come, great matters, “what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, nor has entered into the heart of man” (1 Cor 2:9). If it had not been revealed, it could not have entered into the heart of angels, it contains such glorious, transcendent mysteries. And then again,
  3. The word, to all those who belong to God, has the Spirit of God, through whom it passes, rightly accompanying it, witnessing to the soul of man that it is so.
  4. By a divine efficacy it is mighty in operation. What does it do in the heart? It warms the heart in the hearing, and speaking, and discussing of it, as when the disciples went to Emmaus, Luke 24:32. It has a heat of the Spirit going with it to affect the heart with heavenly joy and delight; it has power going with it by the Spirit to raise joy inexpressible and glorious; it has a power to calm the soul amid all troubles. When nothing will quiet the soul, the Spirit of God in the word will do it by its divine power. Indeed, it will change a man from a beastly or devilish disposition to a higher and happier state, as you+ have it, Isa 11:6–9. It makes lions lambs, and leopards kids. And what is the ground of it all? In that very place, “the earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord.” The knowledge of God reconciled is such a powerful knowledge that it has a transforming power to alter men’s dispositions. What was Paul before conversion? And Zacchaeus? Therefore it is the word, because it has a divine operation to heat the soul, and raise the soul, and change the soul, and cast the soul down, as low in a manner as hell, in the sense of its own misery. It will make a Felix tremble, a man on whom it does not work effectually. Its truths are so moving that it will make a carnal man quake. When Paul spoke of judgment to come, of giving account of all that is done in the flesh, when a possibility of it was perceived, it made Felix quake. It levels mountains, and it fills up the valleys. The word can raise up the soul; when a man is as low as hell, and looks for nothing but damnation, the Spirit with the word will fetch him from there. So with the jailer, Acts 16:31: there was little between him and hell. “What must I do to be saved?” “Why, believe in the Lord Jesus.” And with these words there went out an efficacy. He believed, and afterward he was full of joy.

The first gospel of pardon ever preached was preached by God himself. Never was any creature so near damnation as our first father Adam, cast down from the greatest happiness. Miserrimum est fuisse felicem [the most miserable thing is to have been happy]. For he had enjoyed communion with God and his angels before; and having sinned, and having a consciousness of his sin, considering his great gifts and his understanding of the state he had been in, this could not but affect him deeply. And being in this condition, the promise of the “seed of the woman to break the serpent’s head” revived him.

There is a remarkable efficacy in the gospel. The Roman empire was the greatest enemy the church ever had. You+ see what the ten persecutions were; and yet in spite of them the word gained ground on them and never rested, the gospel spreading, and the Spirit with it, till the cross got above the crown, as it did in the time of Constantine, and so it continues.

  1. And must not this be a divine word which has this efficacy: to revive, comfort, change, cast down, raise up again, search secrets, search the heart to the bottom? A poor unlearned man who comes to hear the word of God, when he hears the secrets of his heart laid open by the word, concludes for certain, “God is in you+, and you+ are God’s ministers” (1 Cor 14:25). The word “divides between the marrow and the bone” (Heb 4:12); it arraigns the heart before God’s judgment seat. In those who are saved it has these effects that I have named. And if you+ ask how they know whether the word is the word, a man may answer, I have found it to be so, raising me up, comforting me, and strengthening me. I would have perished in my affliction if the word had not raised me. Principles are proved, you+ know, from experience, for they have nothing above them. There is no other principle to prove the word but experience from its working. How do you+ know the light to be the light, but by itself, and that fire is hot, but by itself? Principles prove themselves only by experience; and this principle is so proved by itself that there is no child of God who cannot say by experience that the word is the word.
  2. If a man might go to reason, one might bring for the satisfaction of an atheist what could not easily be answered. Let him only grant there is a God, and he will grant one thing in religion or another. But let him grant there is a God and a reasonable creature, then there must be a service, a religion; and this service must be according to some prescribed rules, for the superior will not be served as the inferior pleases. The superior must reveal what good he intends, and what duties he expects. This must be revealed in some word. God and the reasonable creature, and religion, make a word necessary, and that must be the word we have, or another; and what word in the world is likely to be the word but this?

Objection. You+ will say it may be corrupt.

Answer. The Jews watched over the Old Testament so that it would not be corrupted; for they knew every syllable in it, and preserved every letter. It is one part of their superstition, and God blesses that superstition to take away all such quibbles. As for the New Testament, the Jews did not care for it; but the heretics, for their part, watch over it so that there is no corruption; they watch one another that closely. But what are these reasons compared with those which the soul of a gracious Christian knows by the operation of the word on the heart?

Use 1. And therefore let us regard it as the word of God, hear it as the word of God, read it as the word of God. You+ will find a crowd of profane wretches, the scum and lowest of the people, who will talk, and to dress up their talk they must have Scripture phrases; but whose word is it? It is the word of the great God. Eglon was a heathen king, and yet when a message came from God, he rose up and paid reverence, Judg 3:20. We should never read the word without reverence, considering whose book it is, and that we must be judged by it one day.

Use 2. If it is the word, I beg you+ to consider what we say, and know that God will make every part of it good. Not a jot of it will fail; nothing of it will miscarry. God speaks all these words. And therefore if you+ are blasphemers, you+ will not get away guiltless. God has said it. If you+ do not continue to obey, you+ are under God’s curse. Unless you+ repent, you+ will perish. Every threat God will make good. You+ must repent and get into Christ, or else perish eternally. God has said it, and we may confirm it in the unfolding and reading of it. The time is coming for its execution, and then God will wait no longer. Now God waits for our leisure, and pleads with us; but if we will not repent, we will have that arrow in our sides that will never be gotten out till we die in hell. When Scripture condemns a man’s sins, God condemns them; and when we shut heaven to men by opening the Scriptures, God will shut heaven to them. The opening of the Scriptures is the opening of heaven. If the Scripture says that a man who lives in such a sin will not be saved, heaven will be shut to him; he is in a state of death, he is stricken, and he remains in danger till he repents. How many live in sins against conscience, and are under the guilt and danger of their sins! They are wounded, they are struck by the word. There is a threat against their sins, although it is not yet carried out; and they are as much in danger of eternal death as a condemned traitor, except that God allows them to live so that they may make their peace. They have blessed times of visitation. Oh, make use of it! It is the word of God; and know that God will make every part of his word good, in threats as well as in promises.

Use 3. Take occasion from this, too, to shame ourselves for our unbelief in the promises. When we are in any disconsolate state, we are in Job’s case. In his trouble, the consolation of the Almighty seemed light to him, Job 15:11. These are the comforts of God. When we come to comfort some people, though the sweet promises of the gospel are opened to them, they do not consider them as the word, the consolations of the Almighty, and so they seem light to them. But it should not be so. Consider that they are the comforts of the word; so we should hear them with faith, labor to love them, and shame ourselves. Is this God’s word that gives this direction, that gives this comfort, and shall I not heed it? Is it the consolation of the Almighty, and shall I not embrace it? Therefore we should be ashamed that we are not more moved than we are by the heavenly, sweet things God has promised.

How ashamed of himself a man should be who refuses heavenly comforts to embrace comforts below! Has God promised such things, God who cannot lie? And shall I lose my hope of all these glorious things to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season? I profess myself to be a Christian; where is my faith? Where is my hope? A man must acknowledge: either I have no faith, for if I had faith, believing God speaking these excellent things, I would not risk losing them, the good things promised in another world, to enjoy poor temporary things here. Labor, therefore, to bring men’s hearts to believe the word, and ask God to seal it to our souls that it is so.

Means. I will give one direction. Labor for the Spirit of God, who wrote the word, who inspired the word. Beg God to seal it to our souls that it is the word, and to sanctify our hearts to suit the word; and never rest till we can find God seasoning our hearts by his Spirit, so that the relish of our souls may suit the relish of divine truths, so that when we hear them we may relish the truth in them, and may so feel the work of God’s Spirit that we may be able to say, he is our God; and so that when we hear any threat, we may tremble at it, and when any sin is exposed, we may hate it. For unless the Spirit of God has worked something in us suited to the word, we will never believe the word to be the word. And therefore pray the Lord by his Spirit to frame our hearts to suit divine truths, and so to frame them in our affections that we may find the word in our joy, in our love, in our patience, that all may be seasoned with the word of God. When there is a relish in the word, and a relish in the soul suited to it, then a man is truly a Christian in earnest. Till then men will fall away, turn papist, turn atheist, or anything, because there is a distance between the soul and the word. The word is not engrafted into the soul. They do not know the word to be the word by arguments drawn from the word, and therefore they fall from the power of the word. But if we would not fall from divine truths, let us get truth written in the heart, and our hearts so seasoned by it, and made so harmonious and suited to it, that we may embrace it until death, that we may live and die in it.

To go on:

““In that day it will be said, ‘Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him.’””

Here is a gracious promise that closes up all that was spoken before. He spoke of great things before. And now here is a promise of a day in which he will make good to the soul of every believing Christian all the things promised.

““In that day it will be said, ‘This is our God; we have waited for Him; He will save us.’””

It is an excellent portion of Scripture to show the gracious disposition that the Spirit of God will work in all those who embrace the gracious promises of God. The time will come when they will say, “Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him, and now we enjoy Him.”

The points to be considered are these:

  1. First of all, it supposes that there are glorious, excellent things promised to the people of God: rich and precious promises of feasting, of taking away the veil, of conquest over death by victory, of wiping away tears and removing rebukes. Great things, if we go no farther than my text.

  2. Secondly, these have a day when they will be performed, which is not at once; for the end of a promise is to support the soul till the performance. God not only reserves great things for us in another world, but to comfort us on the way, he reaches out promises to us to comfort us till we come there. There is a time when he will perform them; and not only a time, but there are also promises of performance. At that time the promises of these great things will be performed.

  3. The next thing is that God will stir up in his children a suitable disposition, that is, the grace of waiting. As great things were promised before, so the soul has a grace fit for them. “We have waited for You.”

  4. And as they wait for them while they are being performed, so they will enjoy them. “We have waited for You, and we will be glad in Your salvation.” We will so enjoy them that we will rejoice in them. Good things, when they are enjoyed, are rejoiced in.

  5. Again, “we will rejoice in our salvation, we will glory in our God.” After they have been exercised a while in waiting, then comes performance; then they are enjoyed, and they are enjoyed with joy, in glorying in God. For that is the outcome for a Christian: when he has what he longs to enjoy, when he enjoys it with joy, when the fruit of it is that God has his glory, and therefore the heart can rejoice in his salvation.

    Then there is a day: as there is a day for the exercising of his people here by waiting, so there is a day of performing promises. “In that day.” That is, a day of all days. When that day comes, then all prophecies and promises will be fulfilled to the uttermost.

    But before that great day there is an intermediate performance of promises, assisted by waiting, to drop comfort to us by degrees. He does not reserve everything for that day. There are lesser days before that great day. As at the first coming of Christ, so at the overthrow of antichrist and the conversion of the Jews, there will be much joy. But that is not that day. These days make way for that day. Whenever prophecies end in performances, then there will be a day of rejoicing and glorying in the God of our salvation forever. And therefore in Revelation, where this Scripture is cited, Rev 21:4, the conversion of the Jews is meant, and the glorious state they will enjoy before the end of the world. “We have waited for our God,” and now we enjoy him. Yes, but what does the church say there? “Come, Lord Jesus, come quickly.” There is yet another “Come, Lord,” till we are in heaven. So though intermediate promises are performed here, yet there is another great day of the Lord to come, which is especially meant here.

  6. The last thing to be considered in the words is the manner of expression. They are expressed full of life, and with repetition, to make them sure and more certain: “In that day it will be said, ‘This is our God; we have waited for Him; He will save us.’” He brings them in speaking these words of affection.

Indeed, when we come to enjoy the performance of God’s gracious promises, if we live to see the fullness of the Gentiles come in and the Jews called, we will speak of it again and again. Affections are large, and a few words will not serve large affections. It will be no tautology to say, “This is our God; we have waited for Him.”

Beloved, times are yet to come which may greatly affect the hearts of the children of God. Though we may not live to see the performance of these things, yet we will all live to see that day of judgment, and then we will say, “This is our God; we have waited for Him.” We now see God in the promises, and then we will see him “face to face,” him whom we have waited for in the promises, and we will see him in heaven forever.

“Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him.” While we live here we are in a state of waiting. We are under promises, and a condition under promises is a waiting condition; a condition of performance is an enjoying condition. We are in a waiting condition till our bodies are raised out of the grave; for when we die we wait for the resurrection of our bodies. We may say as Jacob did when he was dying, “I have waited for Your salvation.” We are in a waiting condition till body and soul are joined together at the day of judgment forever.

And so we should labor to have the graces that are suited to this condition. The things we wait for are of such transcendent excellence, such as glory to come, that they cannot be waited for unless the Spirit, by the things waited for, fits us to wait for them. A man cannot wait for glory of soul and body unless the Spirit, who raises up faith to believe and hope to wait, purges and fits and prepares him for that glorious condition. “Everyone who has this hope purifies himself, just as Christ is pure” (1 John 3:3). Oh, it is a life-giving waiting, and a purging waiting. It is efficacious by the Spirit to fit and purify a man’s soul, suited to that glorious condition he waits for. Where that is not, it is only a fancy. Even a very slender grasp of the glory to come will make men better. If a man has hope of heaven and happiness in glory, it will make him fit for the place he looks for.

The Eighth Sermon

“He will swallow up death in victory, and the Lord God will wipe away tears from all faces, and the rebukes of His people He will take away from all the earth; for the Lord has spoken it. And it will be said in that day, “Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him, and He will save us. This is the Lord we have waited for Him; we will be glad and rejoice in His salvation.”—Isa 25:8, 9.”

To come closer to the particulars. “It will be said in that day, ‘Behold, this is our God.’” The mouth of the Lord has spoken gracious things before; it has promised a feast, and an excellent feast. God’s way is first of all to give promises to his church. Why? His goodness comes from his goodness: his goodness of grace comes from his goodness of nature. “He is good and does good.” Now the same goodness of disposition that we call bounty, which reserves heaven and happiness for us in another world, that same goodness will not allow us to be without any comfort in this world, because the knowledge and revelation of the glory to come has much comfort in it. Therefore in mercy he not only intends the performance of glory, but out of the same fountain of goodness he intends to reveal whatever is good for his church on the way to glory. So promises of good come from the same goodness of God by which he intends heaven. For what moved God to come out of that hidden light, which no man can come into, and reveal himself in his Son? And what moved him to give the word in his promises, to reveal his mind to mankind, and to make known what he wants us to do, and what he will do for us? Only his goodness is the cause of all. And therefore the end of promises, in God’s intention, is to comfort us on the way to heaven, so that we may have something to support us. They are promissa, quasi praemissa. They are promises and premises, sent before the thing itself.

Now this is why the glory to come is termed the joy of heaven and the glorious state to come. “You+ need patience, so that you+ may obtain the promises.” Heaven and happiness are called the promises, because we have them assured in promises. The blessings of the New Testament are called promises: so we read of the children of the promise, indeed, the heirs of glory. Because all is conveyed by a promise, therefore all happiness is conveyed by a promise.

Now the promises are of good things. For their spring, they are free, from God’s free goodness; for their measure, full; for their truth, constant, even as God himself who promises. And therefore we may well build on them.

Use. Before I go any further, I beg you+, let us count the promises of the good we have to be our best treasure, our best portion, our best riches, for they are called precious promises (2 Pet 1:4): not only because they are precious in themselves, but because they come from the precious love of God in Christ to us. They are also for precious things. They are laid hold of by precious faith, as the Scripture calls them, and therefore they are precious promises. Let us not count only the riches that we have; for what are the things we have, compared with what we speak of, with what we have in promise? A Christian is rich in an inheritance yet to come to him, rich in bills and bonds. Christ has bound himself to him, and he can sue him for it when he pleases. In every kind of necessity he can sue God for good. He can go to God and say, “Remember Your promise to Your servant, Lord, in which You have caused me to trust” (Ps 119:49); and he can bind God with his own word.

But I mention this only in passing, as the foundation of what I have to say.

From the mouth of God you+ see the great promises delivered; and now we have waited for them. What answers promises is expectation and waiting.

The second thing, therefore, between the promises, in which God is a debtor, and the performance, is that there is a long time, a long day. Often God takes a long day for performing his promise: Abraham’s posterity were to be four hundred years in Egypt. And it was four thousand years from the beginning of the world till the coming of Christ, which was the promise of promises, the promise of the seed: a great long day. And therefore Christ is said to come in “the latter end of the world.” Abraham had the promise of a son, but it was not performed till he was an old man. Simeon had a promise that he would see Christ in the flesh, but he was an old man, ready to give up the ghost, before it was performed. God takes a long day for his promises; long to us, not to him, “for to him a thousand years are like a day.”

Reason 1. The promises of God take long to be performed: to exercise our faith and our dependence to the full;

Reason 2. To draw us off from created things; and

Reason 3. To make the things promised dearer to us, so that we set a higher value on them when we have them. Many other reasons might be given, if I intended to enlarge on that point. A Christian has a title to heaven. As soon as he is a Christian, he is an heir to heaven. Perhaps he may live here twenty or forty years more before God takes him up to glory. Why does God defer it so long?

Reason 4. The reason is that God will fit us for heaven little by little, and will perfume us as Esther was perfumed before she could go in to Ahasuerus (Esth 2:12). There were many weeks and months of perfuming. So God will sweeten and fit us for heaven and happiness. It is a holy place; God is a holy God. Christ is that Holy One; and for us to have everlasting communion with God and Christ in so holy a place requires great preparation. And God, by deferring it so long, will mortify our affections little by little, and will have us die in our affections to all base things here before we actually die. David had a title to the kingdom as soon as ever he was anointed; but David was made fit to be a truly excellent king by God’s deferring the performance of the promise till later. So between our right and title and our possession of heaven there is a long time.

Our Savior Christ lived thirty-four years before he was taken up to heaven, because he was to work our salvation. And he was willing to suspend his glory for such a time, that he might do it: to suspend the glory due to him from the first moment of his conception. For by virtue of the union, glory was due to him from the first; but because he had taken on himself to be a Mediator, out of love he would suspend the glory due to him, that he might suffer. And so God, by way of conformity, will suspend the glory due to us, that we may be conformed to Christ. Though we have a right to heaven as soon as we are born, yet God will suspend its full performance, because by correction and by length of time he will subdue, little by little, what keeps us from being conformed to our head.

And can we complain of any delay of heaven, when we are only being conformed to our glorious head, who was content to be without heaven so long?

But to go on. As there are gracious and rich promises, and they take a long time to be performed to us, and “hope deferred makes the heart sick” (Prov 13:12), so God grants a spirit to suit that expectation of his, a spirit of hope and waiting. And this waiting has something perfect in it, and something imperfect. It is a mixed condition. There is good, because there is a promise; for a promise is the declaration of God’s will concerning good. But because it is a promise of a thing not yet performed, there is an imperfection. So there is a mixture in the promise, and a mixture in the grace. Hope and expectation and waiting is an imperfect grace. That there are glorious things is the perfection of good; that we do not have them in possession is the imperfection. So hope is something, but it is not possession; a promise is something, but it is not the performance; a seed is something, but it is not the plant.

Thus God mixes perfection and imperfection in our condition here. He keeps us in a state of imperfection so that we may not think ourselves at home in our country when we are only on our way. Therefore he keeps us in a state of imperfection, so that we may long for home; yet he makes it a state of good, so that we may not sink on the way.

And not only promises; for on the way to heaven God does not keep everything for heaven. He often lets in drops of comfort in the midst of misery. At some times he reveals himself more glorious and sweet than at others. There is nothing reserved for us in another world of which we do not have a beginning, a taste, a down payment here, to support us till we come to the full possession of what remains. We will have full communion of saints there; we have it here, in the taste of it. We know what it is to be acquainted with those who are gracious spirits. We will have praising of God forever there. We know the sweetness of it here in the house of God, which made David desire this one thing, “to dwell in the house of the Lord … to gaze on the beauty of the Lord” (Ps 27:4). There we will have perfect peace; here we have inward peace, unspeakable and glorious, “a peace that passes understanding” (Phil 4:7), in its beginning. There we will have joy without any mixture of its contrary; here we have joy, and “an inexpressible and glorious joy” (1 Pet 1:8). There is nothing in heaven that is perfect, that is sweet, and good, and comforting, of which we do not have a taste and down payment here. The Spirit will be all in all there; there is something of it in us now: more light in our understandings, more obedience in our wills, more and more love in our affections, and it is growing more and more.

And therefore not everything is kept for the time to come; we have something begun here besides promises. There are some small degrees of performance. So the state between us and heaven is a state mixed of good and imperfection.

Now God has fitted graces suitable to that condition, and that is expectation or waiting: a fit grace and a fit disposition of soul for an imperfect condition, which is afterwards to be perfected. For full enjoyment is the condition of perfect happiness, not of waiting; for waiting implies imperfection.

This waiting carries almost all graces with it. Waiting for better times in the glory to come has them to support it. It is a disposition of the soul that is upheld by many graces. For, first, we wait for what we believe. We have a spirit of faith to lead to it. And then we hope before we wait, and hope is the anchor of the soul, which holds the soul steady in all the waves and miseries of the world. It is the helmet that keeps off all the blows. This hope springs from faith; for what we believe, we hope to see accomplished.

So all graces make way for waiting, or accompany it. The graces that accompany waiting for good things to come are patience, to endure all griefs between us and the full possession of heaven; then long-suffering, which is nothing but patience lengthened, because troubles are lengthened and the time is lengthened. So there is patience, and patience lengthened, which we call long-suffering; and then, together with patience and long-suffering, there is contentment, without murmuring at the dispensation of God: a sense in the soul that he would have it so. A man whose heart rises up because he does not have what he wants does not wait with the grace of waiting that flows from a right spring.

God reserves joy for the time to come, for our home. We should be content to have communion with God and the souls of perfect men, and not murmur, though God exercises us with many crosses here. And therefore the Scripture calls it a silence: “In silence and in hope will be your+ strength” (Isa 30:15). The soul keeps silence before God in this waiting condition, and this silence quells all risings in the soul at once; as David, “My soul kept silence before the Lord” (Ps 39:2). It will still all risings of the heart, issuing from a resignation of the soul to God, to do as he will have us do. So it implies patience and long-suffering, contentment, holy silence, without murmuring and repining.

And then it implies watchfulness over ourselves until the promises are fully fulfilled, so that we do not behave unworthily in the meantime. We should not spend the time of our waiting in wickedness, fetching sorrow from the devil and the world to comfort us, or being beholden to Satan. This is no waiting, but murmuring and rebellion, when in crosses and discomforts we cannot be content, but must be beholden to the devil. So there must be watchfulness. And not only that, but fruitfulness in waiting. For he who waits in doing good, who waits in service, is the one who waits. He who is doing his duty all the time in a fruitful course of service and obedience is the one who waits for his master’s coming; otherwise it is no waiting. Waiting is not merely a stretch of time, but filling that time with all gracious conduct: with obedience and silence, with long-suffering and contentment, with watchfulness that we take no evil course, with service, and with fruitfulness, so that we may fill the times of waiting until the promise is performed with all the graces, and may have communion with God.

It is another kind of grace than the world thinks. What is the reason for all the wickedness of the world, and barrenness, and voluptuousness, but that they have not learned to wait? They hear of good things, and precious things promised; but they would have present payment, they will have something in hand. As with Dives: “Child, … during your lifetime you received your good things” (Luke 16:25); they will have their good things here. And what is the reason for wickedness, but that they will have the present pleasures of sin? We must prefer the afflictions of Christ to the pleasures of sin (Heb 11:25). Now that impatience of spirit, to have the reward here, is the cause of all sin. They have no hope, no obedience, no expectation to endure the long continuance of time. Where then is patience, and hope, and contentment?

The mark of a Christian is that he is in a waiting condition and has the grace of waiting. Others must have the pleasures of sin, their profits and satisfactions, or else they will crack their consciences, and sell Christ, God, heaven, and all.

Just as a Christian has excellent things above the world, so he has the grace of expectation, and all the graces that store up and maintain that expectation until the promise is performed.

And therefore it is a hard thing to be a good Christian, quite another thing than the world takes it to be. For notice, I beg you+, what lies between us and heaven, what we must go through if we are ever to get there. Between us and heaven, the thing promised, there are many crosses to meet, and they must be borne, and borne as a Christian should bear them. “Through many hardships we must enter the kingdom of God” (Acts 14:22). Besides crosses, there are offenses that make men stumble, enough, without grace, to drive us from the profession of religion. Sometimes good men, by their failings and fallings out, fall into sin, and fall out; and that is a stumbling block to wicked men. Oh, they say, who would belong to this religion, when they cannot agree among themselves? This is a great hindrance and obstacle. It is an offense and a stumbling block in the way, not so much in themselves. We are full of offense ourselves, and seize on anything that lets us object to the best ways. There is a root of offense in the hearts of all, because men will not go to hell without a reason.

Now because we are apt to take offense, rather than be damned without a reason, it is not easy to hold out. Besides this, Satan presses hard with his temptations from affliction and from offense; he magnifies these things in the imagination. Who would be a Christian? You see what their profession of faith is. And so he makes the way all the more difficult.

And then again, look at our own disposition to suffer, to hold out, to stand fixed. There is an unsettledness, which is an inborn infirmity in our natures since the fall. We love variety, we are inconstant, and cannot fix ourselves on the best things, and we cannot bear to suffer anything. We are not only disinclined to do good, but still more disinclined to suffer any ill. The Spirit must help us over all this, and his help must continue all our life long. Until we are in heaven, something or other will be in our way. Now the Spirit of God must help us over all these afflictions. We will never come to heaven without overcoming afflictions, and offenses, and the temptation with which Satan plies us here. And then overcoming the tediousness of time needs a great deal of strength. Now this grace of expectation does it all. And that is why Scripture so often insists on it. In Isaiah, and in the Psalms, how often it is repeated (Ps 37:7)! “Wait on the Lord if He tarries, wait.” The Lord will wait for those who wait for him; and in Scripture it is the mark of a Christian. Moses speaks of those who waited for the consolation of Israel (Gen 49:18) before Christ came in the flesh; such a man is one who is “waiting for the consolation of Israel” (Luke 2:25). To have a gracious disposition and a grace of waiting was the mark of good people. Now, since the coming of Christ, the mark of the New Testament is to wait for Christ’s appearing. “There is a crown of glory for me, and not only for me, but for all who love His appearing” (2 Tim 4:8). That is an ingredient in waiting, when we love the thing we wait for. And so Titus 2:12: “The grace of God that teaches us to deny ungodliness and worldly lusts, and to live holily, and justly, and soberly in this present evil world, looking for and waiting for this glorious appearing of Jesus Christ.”

So looking with the eye of the soul partly to the first coming of Christ, which was to redeem our souls, and partly to the second, which is to redeem our bodies from corruption and to make both soul and body happy, makes a man a good Christian. For the grace of God, in the first, teaches us to deny ungodliness; and looking for Christ’s appearing makes us zealous for good works. You+ have scarcely any epistle but you+ have the time described for looking for the coming of Christ, as Jude: “Keep yourselves+ in the love of God, and wait for the coming of Christ.” So, as there are gracious promises, and a long day for them, God grants grace to wait for their accomplishment.

Now as God gives grace to wait, so he will perform what we wait for; as they say here, “We have waited.” That is the speech of enjoying. God will at length make good what he has promised; and what his truth has promised, his power will perform. Goodness inclines him to make a promise, truth speaks it, and power performs it, as you+ will see here: “We have waited.”

In God there is a mouth of truth, a heart of pity, and a hand of power. These three meeting together make good whatever is promised. “He fulfills the desires of those who fear Him” (Ps 145:19). The desires that God has put into his children are kindled from heaven; and he will satisfy them all out of his tender pity and compassion. He will not allow the creature to be always on the rack of desire, on the rack of expectation, but he will fulfill the desire of those who fear him. And therefore learn this for the time to come.

Though we must wait, God will perform whatever we wait for. And therefore, “Behold, we have waited for Him.” As there is a time of promising, so there is a time of performing; as there is a seedtime, so there is a time of harvest. There is a succession in nature, and a succession in grace: as the day follows the night, and the Sabbath the week, and the jubilee its set number of years; as the triumph follows the war; and as the consummation of marriage follows the betrothal; so it is a happy and glorious condition, above all conditions here on earth. Therefore in this text you+ have not only the seedtime of the Christian (we may sow in tears, and in expectation, as in sowing), but also the harvest of a Christian. As there is a time of sowing, so there is a time of reaping; as there is a time of waiting, so there is a time of enjoying. We have waited, and now, behold, we have what we waited for.

But why does the Holy Spirit not set down a fixed time, but leaves it indefinite, “In that day”? God keeps times and seasons in his own power; the time in general he leaves open. There is a day; but the point and moment of time he keeps in his own power. It is enough to know there is a day, and a day that will come in the best season. God’s time is the best time. When judgments were threatened against the wicked, they say, “Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die” (1 Cor 15:32). So Saul: “Tomorrow you will die” (1 Sam 28:19); and was he the better for it? So where there is a fixed time of God’s coming in judgment, godly men would be none the worse, and wicked men never the better. Therefore God keeps it indefinite, “In that day.”

There is a day, and it is a glorious day, a day of all days, a day that will never have night, a day that we should think of every day: “that day,” by way of excellence. And before that day there are particular days in this world in which God shows himself and fulfills the expectation of his children, to cherish the grand expectation of life everlasting. So in times of trouble they look to God and wait for deliverance in God’s time, and they must be able to say, “Behold, we have waited.” For such a deliverance is a beginning and pledge of the great performance that will be consummated at that great day, and of the removal of all miseries then. So there is a day when the Jews will be converted, and the fullness of the Gentiles brought in, and the man of sin revealed and consumed by the breath of Christ. And when the church of God sees these things, they may say, “Behold, we have waited for the Lord,” and behold, he has come; what we looked for is now fulfilled. So God does not reserve the fulfilling of all the promises to the great day of all days, but even in this life he will have a “that day.”

And it would be very good for Christians, in the events of their lives, to see how God answers their prayers and delivers them. Let them do as the saints in the Old Testament, who gave names to places where they saw God: as Peniel (Gen 32:30), that is, he will see God; and Abraham, “God will be seen on the mount” (Gen 22:14). So Samson and others gave names to places where they had deliverance, that they might be moved to be thankful. A Christian takes in all the comforts of this life to believe the things of the last great day. “Behold, we have waited for Him.”

That will be a time of sight and full enjoyment, of full power and full joy, which is reserved for heaven; then we will say, “Behold! See, this is the Lord.” The more we see God here, the more we will see him hereafter. There are many ways of seeing in which we can say, “Behold, this is the Lord!” We may say, from the poorest creature, “Behold, this is the Lord!” Here are beams of his majesty in the works of his justice and mercy: “Behold, here is the Lord!” The Lord has brought mighty things to pass, the Lord is marvelously loving to his children. “Behold and see the salvation of the Lord!” We may say, “Behold, here,” and see something of God in every creature. There is no creature that does not have something of God. Things that merely exist have something of God; but things that have life have more of God. And so in some there is more of God, in some less.

But in the church of God especially we may see his going in the sanctuary. Behold, this is what God has done for his church. And in the sacraments we may say, I have seen the Lord, and felt the Lord in his ordinance by his Holy Spirit. We do all this before we come to see him in heaven. But that is not what is chiefly meant here.

We will say, “Behold, this is the Lord!” when we see him in heaven. All sight here leads to that sight. Faith has a sight here, but it is in the word and sacrament, and so imperfect; but the sight in heaven is direct and perfect, and therefore set against faith. We live by faith, and not by sight. In heaven we will live by sight; not that we do not live by sight here in some degree, for the lesser sight leads to the greater. But compared with the sight in heaven, there is no sight. Scripture speaks of seeing God comparatively. Moses “saw God,” that is, more than any other did; and Jacob “saw God,” that is, comparatively more than before, but not fully and wholly. We can apprehend him, but not comprehend him, as they say. We may see something of him, but not the whole.

But in heaven we will have another sight of God, and then we will say, “Behold, this is the God we have waited for!” We will see Christ face to face.

Beloved, that is the sight indeed. And if you+ ask me whether we will see God then or not, consider what I said before. This is the God we have waited for in obedience, and fruitfully.

If we are to be enraptured with the sight of God, then surely, if we see him here, we may see him there. We see him with the eye of faith, we see him in the ordinance; we have some sight of God that the world does not have. God reveals himself to his children more than to the world; and therefore they say, “You reveal Yourself to us, and not to the world” (John 14:22). A Christian wonders that God should reveal his love, and mercy, and goodness to him more than to others. And therefore, if we belong to God and will see him hereafter, we must see him now. As we may see him, we must have some knowledge of him. And if we see God in any way, everything in the world will count for nothing compared with communion with God in Christ: as in “We have seen the Lord, and what have we to do with idols?” (Hos 14:8). The soul who has seen Christ grows in hatred of sin, and loathes all things in comparison.

And then again, if we are ever to see God in glory, in this glorious and triumphing manner, “This is the Lord,” this sight is a changing sight. There is no sight of God that does not change us and alter us to the likeness of God, when he calls us to look up to him, and he looks on us in favor and mercy. The best fruit of his favor is grace, peace, and joy, for these are beams that issue from him, grace, as beams from the sun. But wherever God looks with any favor, there is a conformity to Christ: a gracious, humble, compassionate, merciful, obedient disposition, which is a down payment of the Spirit of Christ. And there is a pursuit of purity, of a disposition refined from the pollutions of the world. “The pure in heart … will see God” (Matt 5:8). Those who hope to see God forever in heaven will pursue that purity that may dispose and fit them for heaven. And there is such a gracious influence in it that, for those who hope for heaven, the very hope cannot but help to purify them.

As there is grace suited to waiting, so there is an influence from the things hoped for that gives vigor to all grace. As all the graces of a Christian fit and enable him for heaven, so the hope of heaven gives life to all grace. Each works on the other. God grants a revealing of these glorious things to help us wait, and be patient, and fruitful, and abounding in the work of the Lord. And the more we wait fruitfully, and patiently, and silently, the more we see of heaven. As in nature the seed brings forth the tree, and the tree the seed, so in the things of God one thing breeds another, and that breeds the first again. So waiting and grace fit us for heaven, and the thought of heaven puts life and vigor into all the graces that fit us for heaven. What is our faith aimed at, but those glorious things we will see hereafter? What would patience be, if not for the thought of that? What would hope be, if not for the excellence of the object of hope? And what would the enduring of troubles be, if there were not something in heaven to make amends for all? They help us to come to glory, and lively, hopeful thoughts of those things animate and enliven all the graces that fit us for heaven. If we are ever to possess heaven hereafter and say, “Behold, this is he we have waited for,” we must see him here in such a way that we think little of all things, and see him with a sight that changes us; for the object of glory cannot be revealed without stirring up a disposition suited to glory. If this is not so, never hope for a sight of him in heaven.

And therefore let me entreat and beg you+, with the apostle Paul, to “look to the end”: look to the main thing that can come in this world, and that will come hereafter. It is wisdom to look to the end. A man who builds a house will think of the end he sets before himself, that is, dwelling and habitation. We are meant for everlasting communion with God; we are to be perfect, in glory as in grace. Heaven is our element; we do not rest till then—we are in motion till then—that being our station. Then think often of this: never rest in any intermediate condition, because we are waiting till we come to that condition. Let us so carry ourselves that we may say, This we waited for; it is the glory we expected. It is our wisdom often to have the end of our lives in our eyes, that we may be helped to wait patiently, cheerfully, and comfortably till the consummation comes, when all promises will end in performance, when all that is ill and imperfectly good will be removed: a consumption of ill, and a consummation of all good.

Oh, let us have that day in our eyes, that day of all days, and the very thoughts of it will fit us for the day. The thoughts of our end will fit us and stir us up to use all the means that lead to that end. Medicine is good if it leads to health. The very thought of that prescribes order and means. We read, “Seek first the kingdom of God … and all these things will be added unto you+” (Matt 6:33). The thought of the end prescribes order to all means, and it prescribes measure: “how to use the world as though I did not use it” (1 Cor 7:31); for the thoughts of my end stir me up to take every course suited to that end. And therefore the best wisdom in Christians is often to set the end before them, and to be content with no grace or comfort as it stands in its imperfection, but to look on every grace, every comfort, every good, as it leads toward perfection. When David desired to dwell in the house of God forever, it was not that he would let his desire stop at the house of God here; he aims at heaven. And so when the saints of God set the bounds of their desires and contentment, it is with an eye to the last day, the rest of a Christian, beyond which they cannot go, even communion with God himself.

The Ninth Sermon

“And it will be said in that day, “Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him, and He will save us. This is the Lord we have waited for Him; we will be glad and rejoice in His salvation.”—Isa 25:9.”

In the worst age of the church, so that the church may not be swallowed up with fear in the worst times, God prepares promises for his people. Our blessed Savior himself did the same for his poor disciples, so that they might not be overwhelmed with sorrow. Therefore he adds sacraments to the Passover, and the New Testament to the Old, and all to confirm faith, knowing that our hearts are very easily daunted.

The Lord promises here a feast of fat things, and all things belonging to a feast, the best of the best, and the removal of all that may hinder joy, such as the taking away of the veil that keeps them from seeing it. And then death is swallowed up in victory, as it already is in our Head, who is gloriously triumphing in heaven; and then all tears will be wiped from all faces. There is a turn and change of things. They are now in a valley of tears, but it will not always be so. The time will come when all tears will be wiped away, and the cause of all tears, which is sorrow. The rebukes of his people will be taken away; the offense that lies on the best things will be taken away. The worst things now go under a better appearance, and the best things under a veil; but one day things will be seen as they are. The God of truth will have truth made clear enough. And all this is sealed with the highest authority, which admits no contradiction: “The Lord of Hosts has spoken it.”

We came last time to these words, “Behold, this is our God.” In them we may consider, first of all, that God has left to his church rich and precious promises, such as were spoken of before: a feast, and the removal of all hindrances whatever. He not only grants heaven when we die, and eternal happiness; but in this world, on our way, he grants precious promises to support our faith, so that we may begin heaven upon earth. We showed last time what these promises are.

The second observation was that God’s people are here in a state of expectation, since it will be said, “Behold, we have waited for Him.” We are in a condition of waiting while we live in this world, because we are not at home. Our state requires waiting; heaven requires settledness and rest. There all appetites, all desires will be satisfied to the full. Our state here is a passage to a better state, and waiting is the disposition fit for such a condition.

And in this there is both good and imperfection. Good, that we have something to wait for; imperfection, that we have to wait for it, that we do not possess it; and until we are in heaven we are in a state of waiting. In Revelation, “Come, Lord Jesus, come quickly” (Rev 22:20), a glorious state of a church is set forth; but until all is done, it does not have what it wants. We cannot be in such a state in the world; but there is room for a desire, namely, for direct and eternal communion with Christ in heaven. And therefore “it will be said in that day, ‘Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him.’”

I will add a little on this state of waiting before I go further. God does not want our condition to be perfect at once; he wants us to continue in a state of waiting.

Reason 1. First of all, it is his pleasure that we should live by faith, and not by sight. We have sense and feeling of many things; he does not reserve everything for heaven. How many sweet refreshments we have on the way! But the course of our life is by faith, and not by sight. God wants us in such a condition.

Reason 2. Again, we are not fitted here for the sight of the glory to come. Our vessels cannot hold that glory. A few drops of that happiness so overcame Peter in the transfiguration that he did not know himself.

Reason 3. God is so good to us that he would have us enjoy the best at the last. Heaven is the sweeter, the harder our way there is. Heaven is heaven, and happiness is happiness, after a long time of waiting. For waiting enlarges the capacity and desires of the soul to receive more; it sets off the happiness afterward. And therefore God keeps the best for the last, because he will never interrupt the happiness of his children. When they are in heaven, every cause of sorrow is banished. He will have a distinction between the church militant and the church triumphant. He will train up his children here before he brings them to heaven. He will perfume his spouse, and make her fit for an everlasting communion with him in heaven. The third thing is that, as there are promises, and these promises are not fulfilled at once, which puts us in a state of waiting, so God gives grace to uphold us in waiting. Waiting is not an empty time, to wait so long and have no grace in the meantime; but waiting is a time of fitting us for what we are to receive afterward.

We see in nature that winter is a dull time compared with the spring and harvest, and the weather is very cold; yet it ripens and mellows the soil, and fits it for the spring. A great deal is done toward the harvest in winter. It is not just a stretch of time. So between the promise and heaven itself, it is not merely a time of waiting, and that is all; but it is a time taken up by the Spirit of God in preparing the heart, in subduing all base lusts, and in drawing us away from ourselves and from whatever is contrary to heaven. The time is filled up with a great deal of what fits us for glory in heaven. The gracious God who fits us for heaven, and heaven for us, furnishes us with all the graces necessary for that condition: faith to believe; patience to wait for, and to depend on, what he does not see, to be above sense; a grace of hope to wait for what he believes, to be an anchor to his soul in every condition. And then a grace of patience to wait meekly all the while. And then long-suffering, patience lengthened out. As the wearisome time between us and heaven is long, so there are lengthening graces. We want everything at once: “How long, Lord, how long?” (Rev 6:10). We are so impatient, even David and others; and therefore God gives grace to hold out and lengthen our spiritual faith, and hope, and perseverance, and constant courage to meet all difficulties on the way. When the spirit of a man beholds heaven, and happiness, and God, it makes him constant, in some measure like the things he beholds, for the spirit transforms him into the likeness of the object. Now, he beholds a constant covenant; and as faith looks to a constant God, constant happiness, and constant promises, it shapes the soul to suit the excellence of the object it lays hold on.

And then the Spirit of God, on the way to heaven, subdues all evil murmurings and objections, not allowing us to put out our hands to any iniquity. Though we do not have what we would have, he keeps us in a good and fruitful way; for to wait is not only to endure, but to endure in a good course, fitting us for happiness, until grace ends in glory.

In the fourth place, God will perform all his promises in time. As the church says here, “This is the Lord we have waited for Him.” Now he has made good whatever he has said.

To enlarge on this point a little. As there is a time of waiting, so there will be a time when God’s people will say, “Behold, this is the Lord we have waited for Him.” Why?

Reason 1. God is Jehovah. A full and pregnant word! A word of comfort and support for the soul, this word Jehovah! He is a God who gives being to all things, and being to his word, and therefore what he says he will make good. He is Lord of his word. Every man’s word is as good as his nature, and power, and ability: the word of a man, or the word of an honest man; but since this is the word of a God, he will make all good.

Reason 2. And then he will make all good, because he is faithful. God says it, and he will do it.

Reason 3. You+ need no more reason than his pity for his people, his deepest compassion. The hearts of people would fail if he delayed too long. And therefore, out of his tender mercy, in his time, which is the best time, he will make good all his promises, not only because he is faithful, but because he is loving and full of pity. And then he will do it.

Reason 4. For what is grace, but a down payment of the fullness we will have in heaven? What is peace here but a down payment of that peace in heaven? And what is joy here but a down payment of fullness of joy forevermore? And will God lose his down payment? Therefore we will enjoy what God has promised and what we expect, because we have the down payment. It is not only a pledge, for a pledge may be taken back, but a down payment, which is never taken back, but is made up into the full bargain. Grace is completed in glory, as beginnings are completed in perfection. Where God lays a foundation, he will perfect it. Where God gives the firstfruits, he will give the harvest.

But it will be a long time first, because he will exercise every grace to the utmost. You+ see how Abraham was brought to the very last moment. On the mountain God provided a sacrifice, when the knife was ready to seize on Isaac’s throat, Gen 22:12-14.

We should answer God’s dealing with our faith; that is, if God delays, let us wait, indeed, wait to the uttermost, wait to death. He is our God to death, and in death, and forever. If God performs his promise at the worst, then, until we are at the lowest, we must wait.

And, therefore, one mark that sets a child of God apart from others is this. “Give me the present,” says the carnal, brutish man, the world; but God’s people are content to wait. The child of God knows that what he has in promise is better than what he has in possession. The gleanings of God’s people are better than the harvest of the others. The others cannot wait, but must have present payment. God’s child can wait, for he lives by faith. And therefore we should learn patiently to wait for the performance of all God’s promises.

And to give a little direction in this, remember some rules, which every man may gather for himself, such as,

  1. God’s time is the best time. Deus est optimus arbiter opportunitatis, the best discerner of opportunities. And “on the mountain God will be seen.” Though he delays long, he will come, and not delay too long; and then all the strength of the enemy is with God. Robur hostium apud Deum. The strength of the enemy is in his hand; he can suspend it when he pleases.
  2. Then, though God seems to work by ways contrary to what he promises, which makes waiting so difficult, yet he will bring things about at last. He promises happiness, and there is nothing but misery. He promises forgiveness, and opens the conscience to cry out against sin. Yes, but Luther’s rule is very good in this case. Summa ars, the greatest art of a Christian is credere credibilia, and sperare dilata, to hope for things a long time, and to believe God when he seems contrary to himself in his promise.

But though God delays, in that day he does perform. It is set down without a fixed date, for it is not fit that we should know the particular time. And therefore he says, “in that day.” He does not set down a particular time, but “in that day,” in which he means to be glorious in performing his promise. There is a time, and a set time, and a short time too, as God counts time, and a fit time. If the time were shorter than God has appointed, it would be too short; if longer, too long. “My times,” says David, “are in Your hands” (Ps 31:15). If they were in the enemy’s hands, we would never be out of trouble; if in our own, we would never enter it; if in our friends’ hands, their goodwill would be more than their ability. “But my times”—he does not say, “my time,” but—“my times are in Your hands,” that is, my times of trouble and times of waiting. And it is well that they are in God’s hands, for he has a day, and a fixed day, and a fit day to answer the waiting of all his people.

And when that day has come, you+ see how their hearts are enlarged; they will say, “This is the Lord we have waited for Him.”

When God means to perform his promise, either in this world or in the world to come, especially in the world to come, when there will be the completion of all promises, God will enlarge the hearts of his people. “This is the Lord we have waited for Him.” “This is the Lord.” He repeats it again and again.

Our soul has a great capacity, being a spiritual substance; and then God will fill the soul, and make it take in misery, or take in happiness, when every corner of the soul will be filled; and then, since men have bodies too, it is fit that the bodies should have a part; so the whole man will show forth the justice or the mercy of God.

From the nature of the thing, it cannot be otherwise. Every member of the body will be fit to glorify God. What the psalmist says of his tongue, “Awake, my glory,” he may say of every member: Do your office in glorifying the Lord and rejoicing in the Lord. Pectus facit disertos. The heart makes a man eloquent and full. So the performance of any promise fills the heart so full of affections, the affections are so enlarged; and therefore we must not be fond of a courtly kind of expression, as they were in old times, and the like court eloquence, when men were not free to speak fully. But when joy possesses the heart to the full, there are full expressions: “This is the Lord, this is the Lord let us rejoice in Him.” And therefore there seem to be so many tautologies in the Psalms, though they are no tautologies, but sheer overflowings of a sanctified affection.

Oh! beloved, what a blessed time that will be, when this large heart of ours will have what will fill it; when the best parts of us, our understanding, will, and affections, will be carried to what is better and larger than themselves, and will be, as it were, swallowed up in the fullness of God. And that is the reason for the repetition of the word, “This is the Lord, this is the Lord.”

And it follows, “We will rejoice and be glad in His salvation.” When a gracious heart is full of joy, how does it express that joy? A wicked heart, when it is full of joy, is like a dirty river that runs over its banks and carries a great deal of filth with it: dirty expressions. But when a gracious heart expresses itself, being full of joy, it expresses itself in thanks and praises, in stirring up others. “Behold, this is our God; we will rejoice and be glad in His salvation.” “Is anyone cheerful?” says the apostle St. James, “He should sing praises” (Jas 5:13). God has affections for any condition. “Is a man in misery? Let him pray.” This is a time of mourning. Does God perform any promise, and so give cause for joy? Let him sing. There is an action for every affection, an affection for every condition. And this may stir us up to begin the employment of heaven here on earth. We will say so in heaven, “Behold, this is the Lord we have waited for Him.”

For every performance of promises, give abundant thanks. “Our way of life is in heaven,” says the apostle, Phil 3:20. And what is the greatest part of a Christian’s way of life, but to give thanks in all things? Here the holy church speaks this way because their cause for praise was too big for their soul, and so they broke out in this manner. And so it often is with a child of God. His heart is so full that it is too big for his body when he tries to express his praise. But it is his comfort that in heaven he will have a large heart, equal to the large occasion of praise. I will not enlarge on the common topic of thanksgiving.

In this condition we can never be miserable; for it springs from joy, and joy disposes a man to thankfulness, and peace follows thankfulness; and can we be miserable in peace of conscience? Therefore, says the apostle, “In all things give thanks, and let your+ requests be made known to God” (Phil 4:6). And what will follow from that, when I have made known my requests and paid my tribute of thanks? “Then the peace of God, which passes understanding, will guide your+ mind” (Phil 4:7). When we have paid God the tribute we can pay him, then the soul is at peace, like one who has paid off a debt. I have prayed to God, I have laid my petition in his bosom, I am not in arrears for former favors; “therefore the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your+ hearts and minds.” Hannah had prayed once; she did not go away, but prayed again, 1 Sam 2:1. The happiness of heaven follows the actions of heaven. Since praising is the main work of heaven, the happiness and comfort of heaven follow it.

And although these promises are fulfilled in heaven, yet they have a gradual performance on earth. For he speaks certainly of the state of the Jews yet to come, in which there will be the fulfilling of all these promises.

“We have waited for Him; He will save us.” Experience of God’s performance always stirs them up to wait for him, and to rejoice in his salvation. Experience stirs up hope. The beginning and the middle of a Christian’s life is to hope for the end; and surely our beginning should help the latter end! All a Christian’s life should help the end. All that went before should come in and help his latter end.

Beloved, we are too backward in this, in treasuring up the benefit of experience. There are few people of any years who could not tell stories of God’s gracious dealings with them, if all were kept: the comforts past, and those for the time to come, and all little enough. It was David’s course: “You are my God from my mother’s womb, and on You I have leaned ever since I was born; do not fail me when I am old” (Ps 22:10). Go along with God’s favors, and use them as pleas for future blessings. As former victories help to win the second victory, every former favor helps to strengthen our faith.

Next, God is a fountain never drained, and when we have to deal with an infinite God, the more we take of him the more we offer him. It is no good plea to say, You have done kindnesses, therefore keep doing them. But we cannot honor God more than by looking, from former experience, for great things from the great God.

“We have waited for Him; He will save us; we have waited for Him, and we will rejoice in His salvation.” What a child of God gives thanks for, and rejoices in, and labors for, is more and more experience of his salvation. “We will rejoice in His salvation.” There is not a stronger word in all the Scripture, nor in nature. He does not speak of rejoicing in this or that benefit, but in his salvation, that is, in deliverance from all evil. We will rejoice in his preserving us; when he has delivered us, we will rejoice in his advancing us; and we will rejoice in his salvation. And therefore, when the wisdom of heaven wanted to include everything in one word, he used the word Jesus: all happiness in that word, that pregnant, full word, a Savior.

So God’s dealing with his children is salvation. He is the God of salvation, or a saving God. And God sent his name from heaven, and the angels brought it: the name of Jesus. Therefore consider its full sense. We have a Savior who will live up to his name; as he is Jesus, so he will save his people from their sins, Matt 1:21. And therefore we will rejoice in his salvation. God dealt with us like a God when he delivered us from all misery, from all sins, and advanced us to all the happiness that nature is capable of. As he said before, he will wipe away all tears from all faces, and take away the rebukes of all people. He will punish the wicked with eternal destruction. And if he advances a people, he will be salvation to them, and he can say no more than that.

And this shows that the children of God rejoice, more than in anything else, in salvation, because it is the salvation of God, and because God is salvation itself. Heaven would not be heaven if Jesus, and God in our nature, were not there. And therefore the apostle says, “I desire to depart,” not to be dissolved, “and be with Christ, which is far better.” The sight of God, especially in our nature, God the second person taking our nature so that we might be happy, will make us happy forever. Loving God, and joying in God, and enjoying God make full happiness; but that is not the cause of joy in heaven: the cause of all is God’s influence flowing into us. Here in the world our happiness comes through means: in God’s revealing himself to us by his Holy Spirit, in the use of means, in his dealings and deliverances, letting us see him by his grace, so that we see him, and joy and delight in him forever. It is no good love that rests in any of God’s blessings for their own sake. It is a harlot’s affection to love the gift more than the giver. So the saints of God all desire to see him as they may, and to joy in God, and enjoy God himself, and to see God in our nature, and to be with him forever. Before, he spoke of a feast; and if the feast-maker is not there, what is it all? At a funeral feast there is plenty of food, but the feast-maker is gone. In heaven there is joy; but where is God, where is Christ, he who has done so much and suffered so much for us, who has taken possession of heaven and keeps a place for us there? What is heaven without him? Salvation, severed from him, is nothing.

We will say when we are there, Behold, here is David, Abraham, St. John; here are the martyrs! Yes, but here is Christ, here is God, here is our Savior, the cause of all; and the seeing of him in them, that he will be glorious in his saints, is what makes us rejoice. We will see all our friends in heaven. There we will see the excellence of the happiness of Christ, his love, his grace, his mercy.

The words are spoken with a kind of glorying: “Behold, this is our God.” So the joy of a Christian ends in glory, and in the highest degree of glory, as you+ have it in Rom 5:3, “We also glory in our sufferings; we glory in the hope of glory”; indeed, we glory in God as ours, reconciled. And if we glory in him now as a God reconciled, what will we do in heaven? Can a worldling glory in his riches, his greatness, his favor with such a man, as Haman did? And will not a Christian glory in his God, and make his boast in his God? And therefore in this world we should learn to glory, before we come to that glory in heaven, especially when we are assailed by anything that is apt to discourage us. Glory then in our Head. Perhaps a Christian has no wealth, no great income to glory in; yes, but he has a God to glory in; let him glory in him. The world may take everything else from him, but not his God. So it is with the church in the Song of Solomon 5. The virgins ask the church to describe her beloved: “What is your beloved more than another beloved? My beloved is dazzling and ruddy, outstanding among ten thousand.” Then she goes on in particulars, “my beloved is thus and thus”; and if you+ would know what my beloved is, “this is my beloved.” So a Christian who has a spirit of faith should glory in God here, for heaven is begun here; and he should glory in Christ his Savior, and should set Christ against all discouragements and oppositions. If you+ want to know what my beloved is, “this is my beloved, outstanding among ten thousand.” “Our God is in heaven; He does as He pleases, in heaven and on earth and in the depths” (Ps 115:3). Indeed, we make our boast of God, says the psalmist, when there is occasion. “This is the Lord, this is our God; we have waited for Him,” especially in times of affliction; and what is the reason? This will hold out to eternity. “This is our God.” As in Revelation, it is a plea and a glory forever; for God is our happiness. As the schoolmen say, he is our objective happiness and our formal happiness; he is our happiness, as he is ours, and he is ours in life and death, and forever. So there is always ground for glorying; only God reveals himself to be ours little by little, as we are able to bear him. He is ours in our worst times. “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” Yet my God still, Matt 27:46.

He is our God to death, and he is ours in heaven. “This is our God; we will rejoice in Him.” And therefore we may well boast of God, because in God is everlasting salvation. If we boasted in anything else, our boasting would end with the thing itself; but if we rejoice in God, we rejoice in what lasts as long as our souls, and goes along with the soul to all eternity.

And therefore we should learn to rejoice in God, and then we will never be ashamed. It is spoken here with a kind of exulting, a kind of triumphing over all opposition: “Behold, this is our God.” Beloved, this, that God is our God and Christ is ours, is the ground of rejoicing and of all happiness. All joy, all comfort is founded on this, our share in God; and therefore,

  1. We must make sure of this while we live here, that God is our God; and so that we may do so, observe this. Christ is called Emmanuel, God with us. God, in the second person, is God-man, and so God with us; and the Father in Emmanuel is God with us too. So we are God the Father’s, because we are Christ’s. “All things are yours+,” says the apostle, “whether Paul or Apollos … the present or the future.” Why? “Because you+ are Christ’s” (1 Cor 3:22, 23). Yes, but what if I am Christ’s? Christ is God’s. So we must be Christ’s, and then we will be God’s. If Christ is ours, God is ours, for God is Emmanuel; in Christ, Emmanuel, God is with us in Christ, who is with us. God is reconciled to us in him who is God and man, in our nature. And therefore get into Christ by faith, and get union, and get communion; by prayer open our souls to him, welcome his words to us by his word and Spirit and blessed promptings, and open our spirits to him, and so maintain a blessed fellowship.
  2. Make sure that God is our God by daily acquaintance. These words, spoken at the end, are founded on acquaintance before. “This is our God.” Grace and glory are knit together indissolubly. If God is our God here, he will be ours also in glory; if not here, not in glory. There is a communion with God here before communion with him in glory; and therefore make sure first that God is our God here, by union with him. And then maintain daily acquaintance with him, by seeing him with the eye of faith, by speaking to him, and hearing him speak to us by his Spirit, joining in his ordinances; and then he will acknowledge us and be acquainted with us. In heaven we will say, “Behold, this is our God.” We have had sweet acquaintance with one another: he by his Spirit with me, and I by my prayers with him. Our Savior Christ will not be without us in heaven. We are part of his mystical body, and heaven would not be heaven to Christ without us. Let it be said with reverence: we are the fullness of Christ, as he is the fullness of his church. And if he lacked us, in a sense he would be miserable, having fixed on us as the objects of his eternal love. What would become of him if he lost that object? And therefore, as we glory in him, he glories in us. “Who is this coming up from the wilderness?” Who? “His beloved” (Song 3:6). And, “Woman, is this your faith?” (Matt 15:28). He wonders at the graces of the church, as the church wonders at him. “This is the Lord.” The church cannot be without him, nor he without the church. These words are spoken with a kind of wonder: “Behold, this is the Lord we will rejoice in Him.” So I say, as there is thanks and joy, so there is wonder: “Behold! See!” This is a God worth beholding; and so he wonders at the graces of his children. Beloved, there is nothing in the world worth wondering at. Sapientis non est admirari. It was a saying of the proud philosopher: a wise man will not wonder at anything, for he knows the cause. But in heaven our faculties are lifted up so high that there is nothing but matter for wonder, things “no eye has seen, no ear has heard, nor has entered into the heart of man to conceive” (1 Cor 2:9). They are things beyond expression, and nothing fits them but wonder at the great things granted to the church.

And as with wonder, so with invitation. That is the nature of true thankfulness. There is no envy in spiritual things. No man envies another the light of the Scriptures; but, behold! See, with wonder and with invitation to all others, “This is the Lord.”

Let us therefore rejoice beforehand at the glorious times to come, both for ourselves and for others; let us stir up and urge one another to glory, and rejoice in God our salvation.

  1. And therefore let us all be stirred up by this to learn not to be offended with Christ or with religion. Do not be offended, says Augustine, with the littleness of religion. Everything in religion is little in the eyes of the world. A Christian is a despised person, and the church the lowliest part of the world in outward glory. But,
  2. Consider, along with the littleness, and lowliness, and despisedness of the church, the glory to come. The time will come when we will rejoice, and not only see, but boast with wonder, stirring up others, “Behold, this is the Lord.” And therefore say with our Savior Christ, “Blessed is he who is not offended with Me” (Matt 11:6), nor with religion. There is a time coming that will make amends for all. Who in the world can say at the hour of death and the day of judgment, Behold, this is my wealth, these are my honors! Alas! the greatest persons must stand naked to give account; all must stand on even ground to hold up their hands at the great bar. We may say to the carnal, presumptuous man, Behold, this is the man who put his confidence in his riches. And only reconciled Christians can say, “Behold, this is our God.” Therefore beware of being offended with anything in religion.
  3. Again, if the time to come is so surpassingly glorious, let us not be afraid to die, let us not be too much cast down, for it will end in glory. And let us always be in expectation of good times, wait for this blessed time to come, and never be so content with any condition as to make our rest here. We may write on everything, Hic non est requies vestra [Here is not your rest]. Our rest is still to come; these things are passing. And therefore rest content with nothing here. Heaven is our center, our element, our happiness; and everything is contentedly happy, and thrives, in its element. The birds in the air, the fish in the sea, the beasts on the earth rest there as in their center. And our place forever is heaven; it is God. The direct enjoying of God in heaven is our rest, our element, and we will never rest until we are there. And therefore he who makes his rest here is called a fool for it in the gospel. Whoever says, I have enough, and will now be content with them, is a fool. “There remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God” (Heb 4:9), but it is not here.
  4. Nor rest in any measure of grace or comfort. What is faith compared with sight? We have hope, an anchor and a helmet, which keeps up many a soul, as the cork keeps it from sinking. What is this hope compared with the enjoyment of what we hope for? Here we have love, many love tokens from God. Yes, but what is love compared with union? Ours is only a love of desire. We are only in motion here; we lie in motion only, and our desires are not fulfilled. What is this love compared with the fulfilling of the union with the thing beloved forever? Here we have the communion of saints. But what is this communion of saints compared with communion with God forever? We have infirmities here, as others do, which breed distrust and suspicion. Yes, but we will have communion in heaven, and there will be nothing in us to displease others, but everlasting friendship. Indeed, our communion will be with perfect souls. Our communion of saints here is our heaven upon earth, but it is communion with imperfect souls. Peace we have; yes, but it is mixed peace, peace in the midst of enemies. There we will have peace without enemies. Christ now rules in the midst of enemies. In heaven he will rule in the midst of his friends. So we can imagine no condition here, however good, that is not imperfect. And therefore rest in nothing in the world, no, not in any measure of grace, any measure of comfort, until we are in heaven; but wait for the time to come, “and rejoice in hope, by which we are saved” (Rom 12:12). Wait continually; and though we do not have contentment here, yet this is not our home; this is a good refreshment on the way. As when the children of Israel came from Babylon, they had wells on the way: as in the Valley of Baca, Ps 84:6, they dug wells. So from Babylon to Jerusalem we have many sweet refreshments; but they are refreshments far from home, on the way. God digs many wells; we have breasts of consolation to comfort us; yes, but they are only for the way. And therefore let us answer all temptations, and not seek contentment in anything here. It is good, but it is not our home. Cui dulcis peregrinatio, non amat patriam [He to whom the pilgrimage is sweet does not love his homeland]. If we have eternity, if we love heaven, we cannot be too much taken with anything on the way.
  5. And so for the church, let us not be too much dejected at the desolation of the church, but pray for a spirit of faith, which makes things real to the soul, and presents them to the soul as present, sees Babylon fallen, presents things in the Scripture’s way of speaking, in the words, “Babylon is fallen,” since all the enemies of the church fall. Mighty is the Lord who has spoken, and he will perform it; and, as the angel says, “It is done” (Rev 16:17).

So the time will come before long when it will be said, “It is done.” The church will be gathered, and then, “Behold, this is our God.”

It was the comfort of the believing Jews that the Gentiles would come in. And why should it not be the comfort of the Gentiles that there are blessed times for the ancient people of God, when they will all cry out and say, “Behold, this is our God; we have waited for Him long, and He will save us”? Therefore do not be too much discouraged by whatever present desolation the church lies under. If it were not for this, “we would be of all men most miserable,” as Paul says, 1 Cor 15:19. But there are times to come when we will rejoice, and rejoice forever, and make our boast of the Lord. If it were not so, “we would be of all men most miserable.” Although our happiness is still to come, yet of all persons he is most happy who has Christ and heaven. The very foretaste of happiness is worth all the world. The inward peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Spirit, the beginnings of the image of God and of happiness here, are worth all the enjoyments of the world. Ask any Christian whether he would change places with the greatest worldling and be in his condition; he would not exchange his place in grace for all the worldling’s glory. And therefore, even setting heaven aside, the very firstfruits are better than all the harvest of the world. Let us therefore get the soul raised by faith to see its happiness. We need it all, for until the soul is raised up to see its happiness here, and especially in the world to come, it is not in a frame fit for any service; once raised, it will not stoop to any base sin. Where the affections are so possessed, they look on all base courses as unworthy of their hope. What! I, who hope to rejoice forever with God in heaven, who am an heir of heaven, who have the image of God on me, who am in covenant with God, to take any brutish course, to place my happiness in things lower than myself, I who have God to delight in, a God in covenant, who has taken me into covenant with himself! So I say, in all enticements to sin, let us get ourselves into a frame that may stand firm and immovable.

In all troubles let us know that we have a God in covenant, so that we may find joy in him here, and rejoice with him in heaven forever hereafter.
