To the Reader

Christian reader,

Perhaps you are in any measure awake in these days in which we live, and have taken notice of the many, great, and various temptations that beset all sorts of persons who know the Lord and profess his name, and to which they are continually exposed. You have seen too what success those temptations have had, to the unspeakable scandal of the gospel, and to the wounding and ruin of countless souls. If so, I suppose you will not look any further for other reasons for publishing the warnings and directions that follow, since they are suited to the times that pass over us and to your own concern in them. To those who think fit to keep asking, I will say only this. My first commitment to setting these meditations before the public did arise from the desires of some people. By their personal holiness, and by their constant adherence to everything that is made precious by its relation to Christ, they have openly stood for his cause in the world, and that has given them power over me to require services of greater importance at any time. Yet I dare not lay my doing it on that account in such a way as to suggest in the least that I did not myself consider it timely and necessary, in view of the general state of things I have mentioned. I have myself been tried in this world by a variety of outward providences and dealings of God, and with the inward trials that came with them. Added to this is what I have had the opportunity to observe of the ways and walk of others: their beginnings, progress, and endings, their risings and falls, in profession of religion and conduct, in darkness and light. All this has left on my mind and spirit such a constant sense and impression of the power and danger of temptations that, without any other pleas or pretexts, I must acknowledge it to have been needful in this season, in my own judgment, to give men a serious call to beware, and to uncover some of the most notable ways and means by which present temptations prevail.

But now, reader, perhaps you are among those who take no notice of these things, or do not care about them. You have no sense of the efficacy and dangers of temptations in your own walk and profession of religion, and you have not observed their power over others. You do not discern the many advantages they have gained in these days, when all things are shaken, and you have not been troubled or moved by the sad successes they have had among professing Christians. You suppose that all things are well, in private and in public, and that they would be better still if you could get fuller satisfaction for some of your lusts in the pleasures or profits of the world. If so, I want you to know that I am not writing for you, and I do not consider you a fit reader or judge of what is written here. Consider the times. All the outcomes of God’s providential dealings with regard to the public affairs of these nations are perplexed and entangled; the footsteps of God lie in the deep, where his paths are not known. In particular, unparalleled distresses and strange prosperities are measured out to men, yes, to professing Christians. A spirit of error, giddiness, and delusion goes out with such strength and efficacy that it seems to have received a commission to go and prosper. Such divisions, strifes, and rivalries are found among brethren, and with them such evil suspicions, wrath, and revenge. The desperate outcomes and products of men’s temptations are seen daily in partial and total apostasy, in the decay of love and the overthrow of faith; our days are filled with fearful examples of backsliding such as former ages never knew. There is a visible decline from reformation taking hold of the professing party of these nations, both in personal holiness and in zeal for the cause of Christ. While all this is so, the man who does not understand that an “hour of temptation” has come upon the world, “to test those who dwell on the earth,” is without doubt one of two things. Either he is himself at present held captive under the power of some woeful lust, corruption, or temptation, or he is indeed stark blind and does not know at all what it is to serve God in temptations. With such men, then, I have nothing to do at present. The warning that follows is intended for those who have a general sense of these things, and who are also able in some measure to consider that the plague has begun. It is meant to awaken them further to look about them, in case the infection has come nearer to them, by some secret and imperceptible ways, than they realized, or in case they are caught unawares later by some of those temptations that in these days either lay waste at noon or else walk in darkness. And the directions that follow are put forward for consideration for the sake of those who mourn in secret for all the abominations found among and upon those who profess the gospel. They are under the leadership of the Captain of their salvation, fighting and resisting the power of temptations, from whatever spring in themselves those temptations rise.

That our faithful and merciful High Priest, who both suffered and was tempted, and on that account is touched with the feeling of our weaknesses, would accompany this small discourse with timely supplies of his Spirit and fitting mercy to those who consider it, so that it may be useful to his servants for the ends for which it is designed, is the prayer of one who received this handful of seed from his storehouse and treasure.

John Owen.

Chapter I

The words of the text that are the foundation of the discourse that follows—The occasion of the words, and their connection—The things especially aimed at in them—Things to be considered in the words for the general purpose in hand—The general nature of temptation, and what it consists in—The special nature of temptation—Temptation taken actively and passively—How God tempts anyone—His ends in doing so—The way in which he does it—Temptation in its special nature; its actions—The true nature of temptation stated.

Matt 26:41

Watch and pray so that you+ will not enter into temptation.

These words of our Savior are repeated with very little change in three of the evangelists. Only, where Matthew and Mark have recorded them as written above, Luke reports them like this: “Get up and pray so that you+ will not enter into temptation.” So the whole of his warning seems to have been, “Get up, watch and pray, so that you+ will not enter into temptation.”

Solomon tells us of some who are like one “lying on the top of a mast” in “the middle of the sea” (Prov 23:34): men overcome by false security in the very mouth of destruction. If ever poor souls lay down on the top of a mast in the middle of the sea, these disciples with our Savior in the garden did so. Their Master, a little distance from them, was offering up “prayers and petitions with loud cries and tears” (Heb 5:7). He was then taking into his hand, and beginning to1 taste, the cup that was filled with the curse and wrath due for their sins. On the other side, the Jews, armed for his destruction and theirs, were only a little further away. Our Savior had told them a little before that on that night he would be betrayed and handed over to be killed. They saw that he was “sorrowful and deeply distressed” (Matt 26:37). Indeed, he told them plainly that his “soul is consumed with sorrow to the point of death” (verse 38), and so he begged them to stay and watch with him, now that he was dying, and dying for them. In this condition, when he had left them only a little while, they fell fast asleep, like men who had lost all love for him and all care for themselves! Even the best of saints, left to themselves, will quickly prove to be less than men; they will prove to be nothing. All our own strength is weakness, and all our wisdom folly. Peter was one of them, and only a little before he had declared with great self-confidence that though all men forsook Christ, he never would. So our Savior reasons with him in particular about it: “ ‘Were you+ not able to keep watch with Me for one hour?’ He asked Peter” (verse 40). It is as if he had said, “Are you the one, Peter, who just now boasted of your resolve never to forsake me? Is it likely that you will hold out in that, when you cannot watch with me one hour? Is this your dying for me, to be dead in false security when I am dying for you?” And indeed it would be an amazing thing to consider that Peter should make so high a promise and be so careless and slack in carrying it out immediately afterward, if we did not find the root of the same treachery remaining and working in our own hearts. We see its fruit brought forth every day: the noblest commitments to obedience quickly end in pitiful negligence (Rom 7:18).

In this state our Savior warns them of their condition, their weakness, and their danger, and stirs them up to prevent the ruin that lay at the door. He says, “Get up, watch and pray.”

I will not dwell on the particular thing our Savior aimed at in this warning to those who were present with him then. No doubt he had in view the great temptation that was coming on them from the offense of the cross. But I will consider the words as containing a general direction to all the disciples of Christ as they follow him through all generations.

There are three things in the words:

I. The evil warned against: temptation.

II. The means by which it prevails: our entering into it.

III. The way to prevent it: watch and pray.

I. What Temptation Is

I do not intend to treat the general subject of temptations, but only the danger of them in general, with the means of preventing that danger. Yet, so that we may know what we are asserting and what we are speaking of, some things concerning the general nature of temptation may be laid down first.

1. First, as for the general nature of tempting and temptation, it belongs among things neither good nor bad in themselves. To try, to test, to prove, to pierce a cask so that what is in it may be known: that is all the word means. That is why God is said at times to tempt. And we are commanded as our duty to tempt, or try, or search ourselves, to know what is in us, and to pray that God would do so too. So temptation is like a knife, which may cut either a man’s food or his throat; it may be his food or his poison, his exercise or his destruction.

2. Secondly, temptation in its special nature, as it denotes some evil, is considered either actively, as it leads to evil, or passively, as it has evil and suffering in it. So temptation is taken for affliction (Jas 1:2), for in that sense we are to “consider it pure joy” when we fall into temptation; in the other sense, we are to pray that we “will not enter into temptation.”

Again, temptation actively considered denotes one of two things. It may denote, in the tempter, a design to bring about the special end of temptation, namely leading into evil. In this sense it is said that God does not “tempt anyone” (Jas 1:13), with a design toward sin as such. Or it may denote the general nature and end of temptation, which is trial. In this sense “God tempted Abraham” (Gen 22:1). And he tests or tempts by false prophets (Deut 13:3).

Now, as to God’s tempting anyone, two things are to be considered: 1. the end for which he does it; 2. the way in which he does it.

1. As for the first, his general ends are two:

(1.) He does it to show a man what is in him, that is, the man himself, whether as to his grace or as to his corruption. (I do not speak now of temptation as it may have a place and play a part in God’s judicial hardening of men.) Grace and corruption lie deep in the heart. Men often deceive themselves in searching for one or the other. When we open the soul to test what grace is there, corruption comes out; and when we search for corruption, grace appears. So the soul is kept in uncertainty; our trials fail. God comes with a gauge that goes to the bottom. He sends his instruments of trial into the depths and inmost parts of the soul, and lets a man see what is in him, what metal he is made of. In this way he tempted Abraham to show him his faith. Abraham did not know what faith he had (I mean, what power and vigor there was in his faith) until God drew it out by that great trial and temptation.2 When God says he knew it, he means he made Abraham know it. So he tested Hezekiah to uncover his pride. God left him so that he might see what was in his heart (2 Chr 32:31). Hezekiah did not know that he had so proud a heart, so ready to be lifted up, as he proved to have, until God tested him, and so let out his filth and poured it out before his face. I will not treat here of what such disclosures yield to the saints in thankfulness, humbling, and storing up experiences.

(2.) God does it to show himself to man, and that:

[1.] In the way of grace that goes before us. A man will see that it is God alone who keeps him from all sin. Until we are tempted, we think we live on our own strength. Though all men do this or that, we will not. When the trial comes, we quickly see where our preservation comes from, by standing or falling. So it was in the case of Abimelech: “I have kept you from sinning” (Gen 20:6).

[2.] In the way of renewing grace. He would have the temptation remain with Paul, so that he might reveal himself to him in the sufficiency of his renewing grace (2 Cor 12:9). We do not know the power and strength that God puts forth on our behalf, nor the sufficiency of his grace, until it appears to us when we compare the temptation with our own weakness. The efficacy of an antidote is found when poison has been taken, and the preciousness of medicines is made known by diseases. We will never know what strength there is in grace if we do not know what strength there is in temptation. We must be tried, so that we may be made aware of being preserved. And he has many other good and gracious ends that he accomplishes toward his saints by his trials and temptations, which are not to be dwelt on now.

2. As for the ways by which God carries out this search, trial, or temptation of his, these are some of them:

(1.) He puts men to great duties, duties they cannot imagine they have any strength for, and indeed they do not have it. So he tempted Abraham by calling him to the duty of sacrificing his son: a thing absurd to reason, bitter to nature, and grievous to him on every account whatever. Many men do not know what is in them, or rather what is ready for them, until they are put to what seems utterly beyond their strength; indeed, to what really is beyond their strength. The duties that God ordinarily requires of us are not proportioned to the strength we have in ourselves, but to the help and relief laid up for us in Christ. We are to set ourselves to the greatest tasks with a settled persuasion that we do not have the ability for the least. This is the law of grace. Yet when some extraordinary duty is required, a secret not often uncovered in the yoke of Christ, it is a trial, a temptation.

(2.) By putting them to great sufferings. How many have unexpectedly found strength to die at the stake and to endure tortures for Christ! Yet their call to it was a trial. Peter tells us this is one way in which we are brought into trying temptations (1 Pet 1:6, 7). Our temptations arise from the “fiery trial,” and yet its end is only the trial of our faith.

(3.) By so ordering things in his providence that occasions for sin will be presented to men. This is the case mentioned in Deut 13:3, and countless other instances could be added.

Now, it is not the temptations that properly come from God, with his ends in them, that are meant here, and so I will set these aside from our present consideration. What I intend, then, is temptation in its special nature, as it denotes an active force toward sinning (as it is carried on by evil and toward evil).

In this sense temptation may come from Satan, or the world, or other men in the world, or ourselves, each singly; or from all or some of them jointly, in their various combinations:

(1.) Satan at times tempts singly, by himself, without taking advantage of the world, its things or persons, or ourselves. This is how he deals when he injects evil and blasphemous thoughts of God into the hearts of the saints. That is his own work alone, without any help from the world or our own hearts. For nature will contribute nothing to it, nor will anything in the world, nor any man of the world; for no one can conceive of a God and conceive evil of him. In this Satan is alone in the sin, and he will be alone in the punishment. These fiery darts are made in the forge of his own malice, and they will be turned, with all their venom and poison, into his own heart forever.

(2.) At times he makes use of the world and joins forces with it against us, without any help from within. So he tempted our Savior, when he “showed Him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory.”3 And the variety of help he finds in the world, in persons and things, is beyond expressing, along with the countless instruments and weapons of all sorts that he takes from it at all seasons; but I must not dwell on them.

(3.) At times he takes in help from ourselves as well. It is not with us as it was with Christ when Satan came to tempt him. Christ declares that Satan “has nothing in Me” (John 14:30). It is otherwise with us. For carrying out most of his ends, Satan has a sure party within our own breasts (Jas 1:14, 15). So he tempted Judas. He was at work himself: he put it into Judas’s heart to betray Christ; “Satan entered” him for that purpose (Luke 22:3). He set the world to work: its things, providing “thirty pieces of silver” for him (verse 5, “they … agreed to give him money”), and its men, namely the priests and Pharisees. And he called in the help of Judas’s own corruption: he was covetous, “a thief,” and “keeper of the money bag.”

I might also show how the world and our own corruptions act singly by themselves, and jointly together with Satan and with one another, in this business of temptation. But the truth is that the principles, ways, and means of temptations, and their kinds, degrees, efficacy, and causes, are beyond words in their breadth and variety. Their circumstances, arising from providence, from men’s natures and conditions, spiritual and natural, together with the particular cases that come from these, are so countless that they cannot be brought within any limit or order. To attempt to give an account of them would be to undertake an endless task. I will be content to describe the general nature of what we are to watch against, which will make way for what I am aiming at.

Temptation, then, in general, is anything, state, way, or condition that, on any account whatever, has a force or efficacy to seduce, to draw the mind and heart of a man away from the obedience God requires of him into any sin, in any degree of it whatever.

In particular, that is a temptation to any man which causes or occasions him to sin, or in anything to depart from his duty. It does so either by bringing evil into his heart, or by drawing out the evil that is in his heart, or by some other way turning him aside from communion with God, and from that constant, even obedience in all things, in matter and manner, that is required of him.

To clear this description I will only observe this. Temptation seems to have a more active meaning, and so to denote only the power of seduction to sin itself. Yet in the Scripture it is commonly taken in a neutral sense, and denotes the matter of the temptation, the thing by which we are tempted. And this is one ground of the description I have given of it. Whatever it may be, anything within us or outside us that has an advantage to hinder us in duty, or to provoke us to sin, or in any way to occasion sin, is a temptation and is to be regarded as one. It may be business, employment, way of life, company, affections, nature, or a corrupt design; relations, delights, name, reputation, esteem, abilities, gifts or excellences of body or mind, place, dignity, or skill. As far as these further or occasion the ends mentioned above, they are all of them no less truly temptations than the most violent solicitations of Satan or allurements of the world; and the soul that does not discern this lies at the brink of ruin. This will be uncovered further as we proceed.

Chapter II

II. What It Is to Enter into Temptation

Having shown what temptation is, I come next to make plain what it is to enter into temptation.

1. This is not merely to be tempted. It is impossible for us to be so free from temptation as not to be tempted at all. As long as Satan continues in his power and malice, and as long as the world and lust exist, we will be tempted. “Christ,” says one writer, “was made like us so that he might be tempted; and we are tempted so that we may be made like Christ.” Temptation in general takes in our whole warfare, just as our Savior calls the time of his ministry the time of his “temptations” (Luke 22:28). We have no promise that we will not be tempted at all. Nor are we to pray for absolute freedom from temptations, because we have no promise of being heard in that. The direction we have for our prayers is, “lead us not into temptation” (Matt 6:13). It is “entering into temptation” that we are to pray against. We may be tempted and yet not enter into temptation. So:

2. Something more is meant by this expression than the ordinary work of Satan and of our own lusts, which are sure to tempt us every day. There is something notable in this entering into temptation, something that is not the saints’ everyday work. It is something that befalls them in a special way with regard to being seduced to sin, on one account or another, by way of allurement or of fright.

3. It is not to be conquered by a temptation: to fall down under it, to commit the sin or evil we are tempted to, or to omit the duties it opposes. A man may “enter into temptation” and yet not fall under temptation. God can make a way for a man to escape. When he is in, God can break the snare, tread Satan down, and make the soul more than a conqueror, though it has entered into temptation. Christ entered into it, but was not in the least defeated by it. But:

4. It is, as the apostle expresses it, ἐμπίπτειν, to “fall into temptation” (1 Tim 6:9), as a man falls into a pit or a deep place where there are traps and snares that entangle him. The man is not immediately killed and destroyed, but he is entangled and held fast; he does not know how to get free or be at liberty. It is expressed again to the same effect in 1 Cor 10:13: “No temptation has seized you+”; that is, to be seized by a temptation and entangled with it, held in its cords, finding no way to escape for the present. So Peter says, “the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from temptations” (2 Pet 2:9). They are entangled with them; God knows how to rescue them out of them. When we allow a temptation to enter into us, then we “enter into temptation.” While it knocks at the door we are at liberty. But when any temptation comes in and bargains with the heart, reasons with the mind, and entices and allures the affections, we “enter into temptation.” That is so whether it lasts a long time or a short one, and whether it does this without our sensing or perceiving it or the soul takes notice of it.

So, then, for our entering into temptation it is required:

(1.) That by some advantage, or on some occasion, Satan is more earnest than usual in his solicitations to sin, by frights or allurements, by persecutions or seductions, by himself or by others. Or else that some lust or corruption, through his urging and through the advantages of outward objects, stirs up a greater tumult within us than usual, provoking us, as in prosperity, or terrifying us, as in trouble. A special activity of the author and the principles of temptation is required for it.

(2.) That the heart is so far entangled with it as to be driven to dispute and argue in its own defense, and yet is not able wholly to throw out or get rid of the poison and leaven that has been injected. Rather, if it is ever so little off its watch, it is caught by surprise in an entanglement not easily avoided. So the soul may cry, and pray, and cry again, and yet not be delivered; as Paul “pleaded with the Lord” three times for his temptation to depart, and did not prevail. The entanglement continues. And this usually happens in one of these two seasons:

[1.] When Satan, by God’s permission, for ends best known to God himself, has gained some special advantage against the soul; as in the case of Peter. Satan sought to sift him, and prevailed.

[2.] When a man’s lusts and corruptions meet with specially provoking objects and occasions through the condition of life he is in and its circumstances; as it was with David. Of both of these I will speak later.

In this state of things a man has entered into temptation. And this is called the “hour of temptation” (Rev 3:10), the season in which it comes to a head. Uncovering what this is will throw further light on our present inquiry into what it is to “enter into temptation”; for when the hour of temptation has come upon us, we have entered into it. Every great and pressing temptation has its hour, a season in which it comes to a head, in which it is most vigorous, active, working, and prevailing. It may be a long time rising, and it may be long in pressing, more or less. But it has a season when, through the coming together of other circumstances, outward or inward, such as those mentioned, it has a dangerous hour; and then, for the most part, men enter into it. That is why the very temptation that at one time has little or no power over a man, so that he can despise it, scorn its promptings, and easily resist it, at another time carries him away completely before it. It has gained new strength and efficacy from other circumstances and events, or else the man is drained of strength and weakened. The hour has come, he has entered into it, and it prevails. David probably had temptations to adultery or murder before, in his younger days, as he had in the case of Nabal. But the hour of temptation had not come; the temptation had not gathered its advantages around it, and so he escaped until later. Let those who are exposed to temptations look out for it, and who is not exposed? They will have a season in which temptations will press harder, their reasonings will be more plausible, their pretexts more glorious, the hopes of recovery more in view, the opportunities wider and more open, and the doors of evil made more beautiful than ever before. Blessed is the man who is prepared for such a season; without that preparation there is no escape. This, as I said, is the first thing required for entering into temptation. If we stop here, we are safe.

Now that I have come to this, before I go down to other particulars I will show in general: 1st, how, or by what means, any temptation commonly reaches its hour; 2dly, how we may know when any temptation has come to its high noon and is in its hour.

1st. It does the first in several ways:

(1st.) By long solicitations. When it makes the mind often occupy itself with the evil it is urged to, this produces thoughts that make light of it. If temptation makes this progress, it is coming toward its hour. Perhaps when it first began to press on the soul, the soul was appalled at the ugly look of what it aimed at, and cried, “Am I a dog?” If this indignation is not heightened daily, and the soul, by dwelling with the evil, begins to grow familiar with it, as it were, and is not startled as it used to be, but is rather inclined to cry, “Is it not a little one?” then the temptation is coming toward its high noon. Lust has then enticed and entangled, and is ready to “conceive” (Jas 1:15). Of this I will speak more fully later, when we inquire how we may know whether we have entered into temptation or not. Our present inquiry is into the hour and power of temptation itself.

(2dly.) When it has prevailed over others, and the soul is not filled with dislike and abhorrence of them and their ways, nor with pity and prayer for their deliverance. This proves an advantage to the temptation and raises it toward its height. When a temptation that has at the same time taken hold of and prevailed over many sets upon someone, it gains so great and so many advantages by this that it is surely growing toward its hour. Its prevailing over others is a means of giving it its hour against us. The falling away of Hymenaeus and Philetus is said to “undermine the faith of some” (2 Tim 2:17, 18).

(3dly.) By entwining itself with many considerations that are perhaps not absolutely evil. So it was with the Galatians’ temptation to fall from the purity of the gospel: freedom from persecution, and union and agreement with the Jews. Things good in themselves were pleaded in it and gave life to the temptation itself. But I will not now dwell on the various advantages any temptation has to heighten and enlarge itself, to make itself prevailing and effectual, with the help it receives to this end from various circumstances, opportunities, plausible pleas and pretexts, necessities of doing what cannot be done without yielding to the temptation, and the like. For I must speak of some of them later.

2dly. As for the second, it may be known:

(1st.) By its restless urgency and arguing. When a temptation is in its hour it is restless. It is the time of battle, and it gives the soul no rest. Satan sees his advantage and considers how his forces have come together. He knows that he must prevail now, or be without hope forever. Here are opportunities, here are advantages, here are plausible pleas and pretexts. Some ground has already been gained by earlier arguing. Here are excuses that make light of the evil, and hopes of pardon through later efforts, all ready at hand. If he can do nothing now, he must sit down defeated in his undertakings. So when he had got everything ready against Christ, he made it the “hour” of “darkness.” When a temptation uncovers “mille nocendi artes” [a thousand arts of doing harm], pressing within the house by imaginations and reasonings, and outside by solicitations, advantages, and opportunities, let the soul know that the temptation’s hour has come. The glory of God, along with the soul’s own welfare, depends on how it behaves in this trial, as we will see in the particular cases that follow.

(2dly.) When it combines frights and allurements. These two take in the whole forces of temptation. When both are brought together, temptation is in its hour. Both were present in David’s case in the murder of Uriah. There was the fear of Uriah’s revenge on his wife, and perhaps on David himself, and at least the fear of his sin being made public. And there was the allurement of his present enjoyment of the woman he lusted after. Men are at times carried into sin by love of it, and kept in it by fear of what will follow from it. But in any case where these two meet, where something allures us and something frightens us, and the reasonings that run between them are ready to entangle us, then it is the hour of temptation.

This, then, is what it is to “enter into temptation”; this is its “hour.” Of this I will say more as the discourse proceeds.

III. The Means of Prevention: Watch and Pray

III. Next there is the means of prevention prescribed by our Savior. There are two: 1. “Watch”; 2. “Pray.”

1. The first is a general expression, by no means to be limited to its native meaning of staying awake. To watch is the same as to be on our guard, to take heed, to consider all the ways and means by which an enemy may approach us. So the apostle uses it (1 Cor 16:13). This is what it is to “watch” in this business: to “stand firm in the faith,” as good soldiers, and to “be men of courage.” It is the same as προσέχειν, to “take heed” or look to ourselves, as our Savior often expresses the same thing; so too Rev 3:2. What this word presses on us is a care and diligence in all things, exercising itself in and by all the ways and means God prescribes, over our hearts and ways, over the baits and methods of Satan, and over the occasions and advantages of sin in the world, so that we are not entangled.

2. As for the second direction, prayer, I need not speak of it. The duty and what concerns it are known to all. I will only add that these two take in the whole endeavor of faith for the soul’s preservation from temptation.

The Doctrine and Its Grounds

Having opened the words in the previous chapters as far as is necessary to uncover the foundation of the truth to be dwelt on and applied, I will lay it down in the following observation:

It is the great duty of all believers to use all diligence, in the ways Christ has appointed, so that they do not fall into temptation.

I know that God is “able to rescue the godly from temptations.” I know that he is “faithful,” that he “will not let us be tempted beyond what we can bear,” but “will also provide an escape.” Yet I dare say that I will convince all who pay attention to what is set down and written here that it is our great duty and concern to use all diligence, watchfulness, and care so that we do not enter into temptation. I will show it by the following considerations:

1. In the brief summary our Savior gave us of what we ought to pray for, this matter of not entering into temptation is expressly one heading. Our Savior knew how much it concerned us not to “enter into temptation” when he gave us this as one special subject of our daily dealings with God (Matt 6:13). And the order of the words shows us how important it is: “lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil.” If we are led into temptation, evil will befall us, more or less. How God may be said to tempt us, or to “lead us into temptation,” I showed before. In this direction what is meant is not so much his not giving us up to it as his powerfully keeping us from it. The last words are, as it were, an explanation or exposition of the former: “Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil”; that is, “Deal with us in such a way that we may be powerfully delivered from the evil that comes with our entering into temptation.” Our blessed Savior knows our state and condition full well. He knows the power of temptations, having had experience of it (Heb 2:18). He knows our vain confidence, and the reserves of ability we think we have for dealing with temptations, as he found in Peter. But he knows our weakness and folly, and how soon we are thrown to the ground. That is why he made this provision of instruction at the very beginning of his ministry, to make us careful, if possible, in something of such great concern to us. If, then, we will place any confidence in the wisdom, love, and care of Jesus Christ toward us, we must grant the truth I am pleading for.

2. Christ promises this freedom and deliverance as a great reward of most acceptable obedience (Rev 3:10). This is the great promise made to the church of Philadelphia, in which Christ found nothing he would blame: “I will also keep you from the hour of temptation.” Not “You will be preserved in it”; he goes higher: “You will be kept from it.” Our Savior says: “There is an hour of temptation coming, a season that will make havoc in the world. Multitudes will then fall from the faith, and deny and blaspheme me. Oh, how few will be able to stand and hold out! Some will be utterly destroyed and perish forever. Some will receive wounds in their souls that will never be well healed while they live in this world; they will have their bones broken and go limping all their days. But,” he says, “ ‘because you have kept the word of My patience,’ I will be tender toward you, and ‘keep you from the hour of temptation.’ ” Surely what Christ promises in this way to his beloved church, as a reward of her service, love, and obedience, is no light thing. Whatever Christ promises to his bride is a fruit of unspeakable love; and that is so in a special way with what is promised as a reward of special obedience.

3. To this end let us consider the general outcomes of men’s entering into temptation, both of bad men and of good: of professing Christians without a foundation, and of the choicest saints.

(1.) For the first, I will offer only one or two texts of Scripture. “The seeds on rocky ground are those who hear the word and receive it with joy, but they have no root. They believe for a season” (Luke 8:13). Well! How long do they believe? They are moved by the preaching of the word, and believe because of it; they make a profession of religion and bring forth some fruits. But until when do they last? He says, “in the time of temptation, they fall away.” Once they enter into temptation they are gone forever. Temptation withers their whole profession of religion and kills their souls. We see this fulfilled every day. Men have sat under the preaching of the gospel, been moved and delighted by it, and perhaps been regarded as believers, and have continued like this for some years. Yet no sooner does a temptation befall them that has vigor and staying power in it than they are turned out of the way and gone forever. They come to hate the word they delighted in, they despise those who profess it, and they are hardened by sin. So in Matt 7:26: “everyone who hears these words of Mine and does not act on them is like a foolish man who built his house on sand.” But what does this house of profession do for him? It shelters him, keeps him warm, and stands for a while. But, he says in verse 27, “The rain fell,” that is, temptation came, “and it fell—and great was its collapse!” Judas follows our Savior for three years, and all goes well with him. No sooner does he enter into temptation, and Satan gets hold of him and sifts him, than he is gone. Demas will preach the gospel until the love of the world befalls him, and then he is utterly turned aside. There would be no end of giving instances of this. With this sort of men, entering into temptation is entering into apostasy, more or less, in part or in whole; it never fails.

(2.) As for the saints of God themselves, let us see by some instances what outcome their entering into temptation has had. I will name a few.

Adam was “the son of God” (Luke 3:38), created in the image of God, full of that integrity, righteousness, and holiness which could be, and was, an outstanding likeness of the holiness of God. He had a far greater store of ability in himself than we have, and he had nothing in him to entice or seduce him. Yet no sooner does this Adam enter into temptation than he is gone, lost, and ruined, he and all his descendants with him. What can we expect in the same condition, we who have in our temptations not only a cunning devil to deal with, as he had, but a cursed world and a corrupt heart as well?

Abraham was the father of the faithful, whose faith is set forth as a pattern to all who will believe. Yet he entered twice into the same temptation, namely fear concerning his wife, and was twice overpowered by it, to the dishonor of God and, no doubt, to the disquiet of his own soul (Gen 12:12, 13; 20:2).

David is called “a man after My own heart” by God himself. Yet what a dreadful thing is the story of his entering into temptation! No sooner is he entangled than he is plunged into adultery. Then, seeking deliverance by his own devising, like a poor creature in a net, he is entangled more and more, until he lies like a dead man under the power of sin and folly.

I might mention Noah, Lot, Hezekiah, Peter, and the rest, whose temptations, and their falls in them, are on record for our instruction. Surely the man who has any heart in these things must say, as the people of Samaria said at Jehu’s letter, “ ‘If two kings could not stand against him, how can we?’ O Lord, if such mighty pillars have been thrown to the ground, and such cedars blown down, how shall I stand before temptations? Oh, keep me from entering in!” “Vestigia terrent” [The footprints frighten me]. Look at the footprints of those who have gone in. Whom do you see coming back without a wound, or at least a blemish? On this account the apostle would have us show tenderness toward those who have fallen into sin: “But consider yourself, or you also may be tempted” (Gal 6:1). He does not say, “or you also may sin, or fall, or be caught in a fault,” but, “or you also may be tempted.” That is, “You see the power of temptation in others, and you do not know how soon you may be tempted, nor what the state and condition of your soul will be then.” Certainly a man who has seen so many better and stronger men than himself fail and be thrown down in the trial will think it his duty to remember the battle and, if possible, to come there no more. Is it not madness for a man who is so weak he can hardly crawl about (and that is the case with most of us) not to avoid what he has seen giants defeated in attempting? You are still whole and sound. Beware of temptation, lest it go with you as it went with Abraham, David, Lot, Peter, Hezekiah, and the Galatians, who fell in the time of trial.

In nothing does the folly of men’s hearts show itself more openly in the days in which we live than in this cursed boldness of theirs in running into temptations and putting themselves in their way, after so many warnings from God and so many sad experiences every day before their eyes. They are ready for any society, any company, any conditions of outward advantage, without once weighing what their strength is, or what the concern of their poor souls is. Though they walk over the dead and the slain who fell before them just now in those very ways and paths, yet they will go on without heeding it or trembling. Through this door hundreds, thousands of professing Christians have gone out within a few years. But:

4. Let us consider ourselves, what our weakness is; and what temptation is, its power and efficacy, and what it leads to.

(1.) As for ourselves, we are weakness itself. We have no strength, no power to withstand. Confidence in any strength in us is one great part of our weakness; it was so in Peter. The man who says he can do anything can do nothing as he should. And what is worse, it is the worst kind of weakness that is in us, a weakness from treachery, a weakness arising from the party that every temptation has within us. However strong and well fortified a castle or fort may be, if there is a treacherous party within, ready to betray it at every opportunity, there is no keeping it from the enemy. There are traitors in our hearts, ready to take the part of every temptation, to join with it and side with it, and to give up everything to it; yes, to solicit and bribe temptations to do the work, as traitors incite an enemy. Do not flatter yourselves+ that you+ will hold out. There are secret lusts lurking in your+ hearts, which perhaps do not stir now, but which, as soon as any temptation befalls you+, will rise, make a tumult, cry out, disquiet, seduce, and never give up until they are either killed or satisfied. The man who promises himself that the frame of his heart will be the same under a temptation as it is before it will be woefully mistaken. “Am I a dog, that I should do this thing?” says Hazael. Yes, you will be just such a dog if ever you become king of Syria; temptation arising from your self-interest will rob you of your humanity. The man who now abhors the very thought of this or that thing, if he once enters into temptation, will find his heart inflamed toward it, and all reasonings against it overpowered and silenced. He will laugh at his former fears, throw out his scruples, and despise the consideration he lived by. Little did Peter think that he would deny his Master and swear he did not know him as soon as he was ever asked whether he knew him or not. It went no better when the hour of temptation came. All his resolutions were forgotten, all his love for Christ buried; the present temptation, joining with his fleshly fear, carried everything before it.

To treat this a little more distinctly, I will consider what means of safety from the power of temptation, if we enter into it, may be expected from ourselves. I will consider them in general, as to their spring and source, and in particular, as to the ways of putting forth the strength we have, or seem to have.

[1.] In general, all we can look for is from our hearts. As a man’s heart is, so is he. But what is the heart of a man in such a season?

1st. Suppose a man is not a believer, but only professes the gospel. What can the heart of such a man do? “The heart of the wicked has little worth” (Prov 10:20); and surely what is of little worth in anything is not worth much in this. A wicked man may be of great use in outward things; but come to his heart, and it is false and a thing of nothing. Now, withstanding temptation is heart-work. When temptation comes like a flood, can such a rotten trifle as a wicked man’s heart stand before it? But I have spoken of these before. With them, entering into temptation and apostasy are one and the same.

2dly. Let it be whose heart it may, “He who trusts in his own heart is a fool” (Prov 28:26). The man who does so, be he who he may, is foolish in that. Peter did so in his temptation; he trusted in his own heart: “Even if all fall away on account of You, I never will.” It was his folly. But why was it his folly? Because he will not be delivered by it; his heart will not preserve him in snares or deliver him in temptations. A man’s heart will make him very fair promises before a temptation comes. “Am I a dog, that I should do this thing?” says Hazael. “Even if all should deny You,” [says Peter,] “I will not. Shall I do this evil? It cannot be.” All the arguments fitted to check the heart in such a condition are mustered. Do you not think Peter did so? “What! Deny my Master, the Son of God, my Redeemer, who loves me? Can such ingratitude, unbelief, and rebellion befall me? I will not do it.” Shall a man, then, rest on his heart’s being steadfast? Let the wise man answer: “He who trusts in his own heart is a fool.” “The heart is deceitful” (Jer 17:9). We would not willingly trust anything in which there is any deceit or guile; and here is something that is “deceitful above all things.” It has a thousand tricks and treacheries to deal in. When it comes to the trial, every temptation will steal it away (Hos 4:11). Generally men’s hearts deceive them no more often than they trust in them; and then they never fail to do so.

[2.] Consider the particular ways and means that such a heart has, or can use, to safeguard itself in the hour of temptation, and their insufficiency for that purpose will quickly appear. I will give only a few instances.

1st. Love of honor in the world. Reputation and esteem in the church, gained by earlier profession of religion and walk, is one of the heart’s own weapons for defending itself in the hour of temptation. “Shall a man like me flee? I who have had such a reputation in the church of God, shall I lose it now by giving way to this lust, to this temptation, or by joining with this or that public evil?” This consideration has such an influence on the spirits of some that they think it will be a shield and buckler against any assaults that may befall them. They would die a thousand times before they would forfeit the reputation they have in the church of God! But, alas! this is only a green willow branch, or a new rope, to bind a giant of a temptation with. What do you think of “a third of the stars” (Rev 12:4)? Had they not shone in the sky of the church? Were they not aware, more than enough, of their own honor, height, usefulness, and reputation? But when the dragon comes with his temptations, he throws them down to the earth. Indeed, great temptations will make men who have no better defense fortify themselves, without their noticing it, against the dishonor and disrepute that come with their ways. “Populus sibilet, at mihi plaudo” [Let the people hiss; I applaud myself]. Do we not know instances still living of some who, after the glory of a long and useful profession of religion, ventured on going along with wicked men? Within a while they found themselves cast down from their reputation with the saints because of it, and they hardened themselves against it and ended in apostasy (as in John 15:6). This did not keep Judas; it did not keep Hymenaeus or Philetus; it did not keep the stars of heaven; nor will it keep you.

2dly. There is, on the other side, the consideration of shame, reproach, loss, and the like. Men may also put their trust in this as a defense against temptations, and not doubt that they will be safeguarded and preserved by it. They would not for the world bring on themselves the shame and reproach that come with this or that failure! Now, this consideration extends only to open sins, such as the world takes notice of and abhors. So it is of no use at all in cases where pretexts and disguises may be invented and used; nor in public temptations to loose and careless walking, like those of our days; nor in cases that may be disputable in themselves, though plainly sinful to the consciences of those under temptation; nor in sins of the heart. In all these cases, and most other cases of temptation, countless reliefs are ready to be offered to the heart against this consideration. But besides all this, I say, we see by experience how easily this rope is broken once the heart begins to be entangled. Every corner of the land is full of examples of this.

3dly. They still have something that outweighs these lesser considerations: namely, that they will not wound their own consciences, disturb their peace, and bring themselves into danger of hellfire. Surely this, if anything, will preserve men in the hour of temptation. They will not squander their peace, or venture their souls by rushing headlong at God and the thick bosses of his shield! What could have more efficacy and power? I confess this is of great importance; and oh that it were weighed more than it is! Oh that we laid more weight on preserving our peace with God than we do! Yet I say that even this consideration will not preserve a man who is off his watch elsewhere and does not make it his work to follow the other rules dwelt on here. For:

(1st.) The peace of such a man may be false peace or complacency, made up of presumption and false hopes; yes, even if he is a believer, it may be so. Such was David’s peace after his sin, before Nathan came to him. Such was the peace of Laodicea when it was ready to perish, and the peace of Sardis when it was dying. What should assure a soul that its peace is otherwise, since it is supposed not to labor in every respect to keep the word of Christ’s patience and to be watchful in all things? Do you think the peace of many in these days will be found to be true peace at last? Nothing of the kind. They go down alive to hell, and death will rule over them in the morning. Now, if a man’s peace is like this, do you think that something which cannot preserve itself can preserve him? It will give way at the first vigorous assault of a temptation in its height and hour. Like a broken reed, it will pierce the hand of the man who leans on it. But:

(2dly.) Suppose the peace that is cared for, and set up to safeguard the soul, is true and good. Yet when everything is staked on this one venture, so many reliefs will be offered against this consideration when the hour of temptation comes that it will be made useless. “This evil is small; it is questionable; it does not fall openly and directly on the conscience. I only fear the consequences; perhaps I can keep my peace in spite of it. Others of the people of God have fallen, and yet kept their peace or recovered it. If it is lost for a season, it may be gained again. I will not trouble its standing any more; or even if peace is lost, safety may remain.” There are a thousand pleas like these, all set up as batteries against this fort, so that it cannot hold out long.

(3dly.) Fixing on this one particular only is guarding one passage or entrance while the enemy assaults us all around. It is true that a little armor would be enough to defend a man if he could choose where his enemy should strike him. But we are commanded to take “the full armor of God” if we mean to resist and stand (Eph 6). What we are speaking of is only one piece. When our eye is only on that, temptation may enter and prevail in twenty other ways. For instance, a man may be tempted to worldliness, unjust gain, revenge, vainglory, or the like. Suppose he fortifies himself with this consideration alone, that he will not do this thing, wound his conscience, and lose his peace. He fixes his eye on this particular, and counts himself safe as long as he is not overcome on that side. Meanwhile, perhaps, neglect of private communion with God, sensuality, and the like creep in, and he is not one bit better off than if he had fallen under the power of the part of the temptation that pressed on him most visibly. Experience shows that this consideration also does and will fail. There is no saint of God who does not set a value on the peace he has; yet how many of them fail in the day of temptation!

4thly. But they have yet another consideration as well, and that is the vileness of sinning against God. How shall they do this thing and sin against God, the God of their mercies, of their salvation? How shall they wound Jesus Christ, who died for them? Surely this must preserve them. I answer:

1. First, we see this consideration failing too, every day. There is no child of God overcome by temptation who has not first overcome this consideration. So it is not a sure and unfailing defense.

2. Secondly, this consideration is of two kinds. Either it expresses the thoughts of the soul with particular reference to the temptation it is struggling with, and then it will not preserve the soul. Or it expresses the universal, habitual frame of heart that is in us on every account, and then it coincides with what I will offer later in this discourse as the universal medicine and remedy in this case. Of that I will speak later.

(2.) Consider the power of temptation, partly from what was shown before, from its effects and fruits in the saints of old, and partly from other effects that we find ascribed to it in general. For instance:

[1.] It will darken the mind, so that a man will not be able to make a right judgment of things as he did before he entered into it. In the men of the world, the god of this world blinds their minds so that they do not see the glory of Christ in the gospel (2 Cor 4:4), and “promiscuity, wine, and new wine take away the heart” (Hos 4:11). So it is in the nature of every temptation, more or less, to take away the heart, or to darken the understanding of the person tempted.

And it does this in various ways:

1st. By fixing the imagination and the thoughts on the object it tends toward, so that the mind is turned aside from considering the things that would relieve and help it in the state it is in. A man is tempted to think that he is forsaken by God, that he is an object of God’s hatred, that he has no share in Christ. By the craft of Satan his mind will be so fixed on considering this state and condition, with its distress, that he will not be able to make use of any of the reliefs suggested and offered to him against it. Following the fullness of his own thoughts, he will walk on in darkness and have no light. I say, a temptation will so possess the mind and fill it with thoughts of itself and its matter that it will draw the mind away from the clear consideration of things it otherwise could and would have. And the things the mind used to have a vigorous sense of, to keep it from sin, will by this means come to have no force or efficacy with it. Indeed, temptation will commonly bring men to such a state and condition that when others who know their state speak to them of the things that concern their deliverance and peace, their minds will be so possessed with the matter of their temptation that they will not understand at all, and will hardly hear one word that is said to them.

2dly. By woefully entangling the affections. When the affections are engaged, everyone knows what influence they have in blinding the mind and darkening the understanding. If anyone does not know it, let him only open his eyes in these days, and he will quickly learn it. I will not now declare by what ways and means engaged affections cloud and darken the mind. I only say this: give me a man whose hope, love, or fear is engaged in any particulars where it ought not to be, and I will quickly show you where he is darkened and blinded. This, then, is where you will fail if you enter into temptation. The judgment you now have of things will not be utterly altered, but it will be darkened and made too weak to sway the will and master the affections. And the affections, set at liberty by temptation, will run on in madness. At once detestation of sin, abhorrence of it, the terrors of the Lord, the sense of love, and the presence of Christ crucified all depart, and leave the heart a prey to its enemy.

3dly. Temptation will give oil and fuel to our lusts. It will incite and provoke them, and make them rage in a tumult beyond measure. It offers to a lust, a corruption, a suitable object, advantage, or occasion, and so it heightens and inflames it, and makes it wholly dominant for a season. So it dealt with fleshly fear in Peter, with pride in Hezekiah, with covetousness in Achan, with uncleanness in David, with worldliness in Demas, with ambition in Diotrephes. It will throw the reins on the neck of a lust and put spurs to its sides, so that it may rush forward like a horse into battle. A man does not know the pride, fury, and madness of a corruption until it meets with a suitable temptation. And now what will a poor soul think to do? His mind is darkened, his affections entangled, his lusts inflamed and provoked, his relief defeated. What will be the outcome of such a condition?

(3.) Consider that temptations are either public or private; and let us look a little at the efficacy and power of each separately.

[1.] There are public temptations, such as the one mentioned in Rev 3:10, which was to come upon the world “to test those who dwell on the earth”; or a combination of persecution and seduction for the trial of a careless generation of professing Christians. Now, concerning such a temptation, consider:

1st. It has efficacy with respect to God, who sends it to avenge the neglect and contempt of the gospel on the one hand, and the treachery of those who falsely profess it on the other. So it will certainly accomplish what it receives a commission from him to do. When Satan offered his service to go out and seduce Ahab so that he might fall, God said to him, “You will surely entice him and prevail. Go and do it” (1 Kgs 22:22). He is permitted as to his wickedness, and commissioned as to the outcome and the punishment intended. When the Christian world was to be given up to folly and false worship for its neglect of the truth, and for its naked, barren, fruitless, Christ-dishonoring profession of religion, it is said of the temptation that fell on them that “God will send them a powerful delusion so that they believe the lie” (2 Thess 2:11). What comes from God in this way, in a judicial manner, has power with it and will prevail. In these days a selfish, spiritually slothful, careless, and worldly frame of spirit has infected almost the whole body of professing Christians. If it has a commission from God to kill hypocrites, and to wound negligent saints, break their bones, and make them a scandal so that they may be ashamed, will it not have power and efficacy to do so? What havoc the spirit of error has made among us! Does it not come from this: that as some men did not delight to keep God in their hearts, so “He gave them up to a depraved mind” (Rom 1:28)? A man would think it strange, indeed it is a matter of amazement, to see persons of a sober spirit, who laid claim to great things in the ways of God, overcome, taken captive, ensnared, and destroyed by weak means, by stupid opinions and foolish imaginations, such as a man would think could never take hold of sensible or rational men, much less of those who profess the gospel. But let us not think weak what God will have to be strong. No strength but the strength of God can stand in the way of the weakest things in the world that are commissioned by God for any end or purpose whatever.

2dly. In such temptations there is the secret influence of examples in those who are counted godly and profess the gospel: “Because of the abounding of wickedness, the love of most will grow cold” (Matt 24:12). The abounding of wickedness in some will imperceptibly throw water on the zeal and love of others, so that little by little it grows cold. Some begin to grow negligent, careless, worldly, and wanton. They break the ice toward pleasing the flesh. At first others blame them, judge them, perhaps rebuke them. In a short time their love too grows cold; and once the first shock is over, they too conform to them and are cast into the same mold with them. “A little leaven works through the whole batch of dough.” Paul repeats this saying twice (1 Cor 5:6; Gal 5:9). He wants us to take notice of it, and what he is speaking of is the danger that the whole body will be infected by the bad examples of some. We know how imperceptibly leaven goes on to give its flavor to the whole. So it is called a “root of bitterness” that “springs up to cause trouble and defile many” (Heb 12:15). If one little piece of leaven, if one bitter root, may endanger the whole, how much more when there are many roots of that kind, and much leaven is scattered abroad! It is easy to follow a multitude to do evil, and to say “A conspiracy” to all to whom the people say “A conspiracy.” Would anyone have thought it possible that this or that professing Christian in our days would fall into ways of self, of the flesh, of the world? That they would play at cards and dice, revel and dance? That they would neglect family and private duties? That they would be proud, haughty, ambitious, worldly, covetous, oppressive? Or that they would be turned aside after foolish, empty, ridiculous opinions, deserting the gospel of Christ? In these two things lies the great temptation that has come upon us, the inhabitants of this world, to test us. But does not every man see that this has come about? And may we not see how it came about? Some loose, empty professing Christians, who never had more than a form of godliness, began the way to these things when that form had served their turn. Then others began to go along with them a little, and to please the flesh in doing so. Little by little this has reached even the top boughs and branches of the professing church, until almost all flesh has corrupted its way. And the man who departs from these iniquities makes his name a prey, if not his person.

3dly. Public temptations are usually accompanied by strong reasons and pretexts that are too hard for men, or at least imperceptibly win them over to think less of the evil the temptation leads to. These give strength to the compound temptation that in these days has thrown the people of God down from their excellence, has cut their locks, and made them like other men. How full the world is of plausible pretexts and pleadings! There is the liberty and freedom of Christians, delivered from a frame of bondage. In my own observation I have seen a number of people going out through this door into sensuality and apostasy. They begin with light conduct, go on to neglect of the Sabbath and of public and private duties, and end in dissoluteness and profaneness. Then there is leaving public things to Providence and being content with things as they are. These things are good in themselves, but they have been argued into wretched, fleshly compromises, and into the utter ruin of all zeal for God and for the cause of Christ or his people in the world. These and similar considerations, joined with the ease and plenty, the greatness and promotion of professing Christians, have brought things to this point: whereas by Providence we have changed places with the men of the world, by sin we have changed spirits with them too. We are like a colony of men carried into a foreign country. In a short time they fall away from the customs of the people they came from and take up those of the country they have been brought into, as if there were something in the soil and the air that transformed them. Allow me to follow my comparison a little further. Look at the prevailing party of these nations, many of those in rule, power, and favor, with all their followers. Remember that they were a colony of Puritans, whose dwelling was “in a low place,” as the prophet speaks of the city of God, and that they have been moved by a high hand to the mountains they now possess. Whoever sees this cannot help wondering how soon they have forgotten the customs, manners, and ways of their own old people, and have been cast into the mold of those who went before them in the places they have been moved to. I speak of us all, especially of us who are among the lowest of the people, where perhaps this iniquity abounds most. What were those before us that we are not? What did they do that we do not? Prosperity has slain the foolish and wounded the wise.

[2.] Suppose the temptation is private. This has been spoken of before; I will add two things:

1st. Its union and incorporation with lust, by which it gets within the soul and lies at the bottom of all the soul does. John tells us (1 John 2:16) that the things “in the world” are “the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life.” Now, it is evident that all these things are chiefly in the subject, not in the object; in the heart, not in the world. But they are said to be “in the world” because the world gets into them, mixes itself with them, and unites and incorporates with them. As faith and the promises are said to be “mixed” (Heb 4:2), so lust and temptation are mixed. They twine together. They help each other on, and each of them grows higher and higher by the strength they give one another. Now, by this means temptation gets so deep into the heart that no reasonings against it can reach it. Nothing but what can kill the lust can conquer the temptation. It is like leprosy that has mingled itself with the wall: the wall itself must be pulled down, or the leprosy will not be cured. It is like gangrene, which mixes poison with the blood and spirits and cannot be separated from the place where it is; both must be cut off together. For instance, in David’s temptation to uncleanness, ten thousand considerations might have been brought in to stop the mouth of the temptation. But it had united itself with his lust, and nothing but killing that lust could destroy the temptation or win him the victory. This deceives many people. They have some pressing temptation that has gained some advantages and is urgent on them. They pray against it and oppose it with all kinds of powerful considerations, each of which seems enough to conquer and destroy it, or at least to overpower it so that it will never be troublesome again. Yet no good is done and no ground is gained; indeed, it grows on them more and more. What is the reason? It has incorporated and united itself with the lust, and is safe from all the opposition they make. If they would really do the work, they must set upon the whole of the lust itself: their ambition, pride, worldliness, sensuality, or whatever it is that the temptation is united with. All other dealings with it are like tampering with a spreading gangrene. The part or the whole may be kept for a little while, in great torment; but cutting off or death must come at last. With such a course the soul may torture itself for a season, but it must come to this: its lust must die, or the soul must die.

2dly. In whatever part of the soul the lust is seated that the temptation is united with, it draws the whole soul after it by one means or another, and so forestalls or heads off any opposition. Suppose it is a lust of the mind, for there are lusts of the mind and uncleanness of the spirit, such as ambition, vainglory, and the like. What a world of ways the understanding then has to restrain the affections from clinging to God so tenaciously, since there is so much in what it aims at to give them contentment and satisfaction! It will not only forestall all the reasonings of the mind, which it necessarily does, being like a bloodshot infirmity in the eyes that presents all things to the senses and perception in its own hue and color. It will also draw the whole soul into the same frame on other accounts and side considerations. It promises the whole soul a share in the spoil it aims at, as the money Judas first desired out of covetousness was to be shared among all his lusts. Or suppose it is in the more fleshly part and first takes possession of the affections. What prejudices they will bring on the understanding, how they will bribe it into consenting, what arguments and hopes they will supply it with, cannot easily be expressed, as was shown before. In short, every particular temptation, when it is in its hour, has such a supply of help from things good, evil, and neither good nor evil, is fed by so many considerations that seem utterly alien and foreign to it, and in some cases has such plausible pleas and pretexts, that its strength will easily be acknowledged.

(4.) Consider the end of any temptation. It is Satan’s end and sin’s end: that is, the dishonor of God and the ruin of our souls.

(5.) Consider what the outcome has been of any former temptations you have had. Have they not defiled your conscience, disquieted your peace, weakened you in your obedience, and clouded the face of God? Though you were not won over to the outward evil or the full outcome of your temptation, yet have you not been defeated? Has not your soul been soiled and grievously troubled by it? Indeed, did you ever in your life come off fairly, without some loss you could feel, from almost any temptation you have had to deal with? And would you willingly be entangled again? If you are at liberty, beware. Enter no more, if possible, lest something worse happen to you.

These, I say, are some of the many considerations that might be dwelt on to show the importance of the truth set forth, and how deeply it concerns us to take care that we “will not enter into temptation.”

Objections Answered

Against what has been said, some objections secretly creep into men’s souls and have the power to make them negligent and careless in this matter, which is of such importance to them, a duty so absolutely necessary for those who intend to walk with God in any peace or with any faithfulness. These objections are to be considered and removed. They are the following:

Obj. 1. “Why should we so fear temptation and labor to avoid it? We are commanded to ‘count it all joy … when you+ fall into temptations of many kinds’ (Jas 1:2). Now, surely I need not anxiously avoid falling into something which, when I have fallen into it, I am to count as all joy.” To this I answer:

1. You will not hold to this rule in all things, namely, that a man need not seek to avoid something that it is his duty to rejoice in when he cannot help falling into it. The same apostle tells the rich man to “rejoice in his low position” (1:10). And without doubt, to the man who knows the goodness, wisdom, and love of God in his dealings, in every condition that is needful for him, it will be a matter of rejoicing. And yet how few rich, godly men can you persuade not to take care and use all lawful means so that they are not made poor and low! And in most cases, the truth is, it would be their sin not to do so. It is our business to hold our ground and to secure ourselves as well as we can; if God changes our condition, we are to rejoice in it. If the temptations mentioned here befall us, we may have cause to rejoice; but not if we fall into them by neglecting our duty.

2. Temptations are taken in two ways:

(1.) Passively and merely materially, for things that are, or in some cases may be, temptations; or:

(2.) Actively, for things that do entice to sin. James speaks of temptations in the first sense only. For having said, “Count it all joy … when you+ fall into temptations of many kinds” (verse 2), he adds in verse 12, “Blessed is the man who perseveres under temptation, because when he has stood the test, he will receive the crown of life.” But a man might say, “If this is so, then temptations are good, and from God.” “No,” says James. “Take temptation in the sense of something that entices and leads to sin, and in that sense God tempts no one; but each one is tempted by his own lust” (verses 13, 14). As if he said: “To have such temptations, to be tempted to sin, is not the blessed thing I mean. What is blessed is the enduring of afflictions God sends for the trial of our faith. So, though I must count it all joy when, by the will of God, I fall into various afflictions for my trial, which still have the matter of temptation in them, yet I am to use all care and diligence so that my lust is given no occasions or advantages to tempt me to sin.”

Obj. 2. “But was not our Savior Christ himself tempted? And is it evil to be brought into the same state and condition as he was? Indeed, it is not only said that he was tempted, but his being tempted is spoken of as something advantageous, which helps make him merciful as our high priest: ‘Because He Himself suffered when He was tempted, He is able to help those who are being tempted’ (Heb 2:17, 18). And he makes it the ground of a great promise to his disciples that they had ‘stood by Me in My temptations’ (Luke 22:28).”

Ans. It is true that our Savior was tempted. Yet his temptations are counted among the evils that befell him in the days of his flesh, things that came on him through the malice of the world and of its prince. He did not willfully throw himself into temptation; he said that would be to “tempt the Lord your God” (Matt 4:7). And indeed, to enter into any temptation willingly is to tempt God in a high degree. Now, our condition is such that, however much diligence and watchfulness we use, we are sure to be tempted, and to be made like Christ in that. This does not change the fact that it is our duty to do our utmost to keep from falling into temptations, and that for this reason: Christ had only the suffering part of temptation when he entered into it; we have the sinning part of it as well. When the prince of this world came to Christ, he had “no part in” him; but when he comes to us, he has a part in us. So in one effect of temptations, namely trials and disquiet, we are made like Christ, and so we are to rejoice as far as that is produced by any means. Yet by another effect, our being defiled and entangled, we are made unlike him; and so we are to seek by all means to avoid them. We never come off as Christ did. Who of us “enters into temptation” and is not defiled?

Obj. 3. “But what need is there for this great effort and carefulness? Is it not said that ‘God is faithful; He will not let us be tempted beyond what we can bear. But when we are tempted, He will also provide an escape’ (1 Cor 10:13)? And ‘the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from temptations’ (2 Pet 2:9). Why, then, do we need to be anxious not to enter into them?”

Ans. I very much question what help a man will have from God in his temptation if he willingly enters into it because he supposes God has promised to deliver him out of it. The Lord knows that, through the craft of Satan, the subtlety and malice of the world, and the deceitfulness of the sin that so easily besets us, we will enter into various temptations when we have done our utmost. In his love, care, tenderness, and faithfulness, he has provided such a sufficiency of grace for us that they will not wholly prevail to make an everlasting separation between him and our souls. Yet I have three things to say to this objection:

(1.) The man who willfully or negligently enters into temptation has no reason in the world to promise himself any help from God, or any deliverance from the temptation he has entered into. The promise is made to those whom temptations befall in their way, whether they will or not; not to those who willfully fall into them, who run out of their way to meet them. And so the devil (as is commonly observed), when he tempted our Savior, left out the words “in all your ways” from the text of Scripture he twisted to his purpose. The promise of deliverance is to those who are in their ways; and one principal way is to beware of temptation.

(2.) Though there is a sufficiency of grace provided for all the elect, so that they will not fall utterly from God through any temptation, yet it would make any gracious heart tremble to think what dishonor to God, what scandal to the gospel, what woeful darkness and disquiet they may bring on their own souls, though they do not perish. And those who are scared by nothing but fear of hell, on whom other considerations short of that have no influence, have more reason to fear it, in my judgment, than perhaps they realize.

(3.) To enter into temptation on this account is to venture on sin “so that grace may increase” (Rom 6:1, 2), which is the same thing as to “continue in sin”; and the apostle rejects the very thought of that with the greatest abhorrence. Is it not madness for a man willingly to let the ship he is in break on a rock, with the irrecoverable loss of his merchandise, because he supposes that he himself will swim safely to shore on a plank? Is it any less madness in a man who will risk the shipwreck of all his comfort, peace, and joy, and of as much of the glory of God and the honor of the gospel as he is entrusted with, merely on the supposition that his soul will still escape? A man would think these things did not deserve to be mentioned; and yet poor souls at times deceive themselves with things like these.

Chapter IV

Particular cases put forward for consideration—The first, answered in various particulars—Several signs of the state of a soul entering into temptation.

With these things laid down in general, I go on to consider three particular cases arising from the truth set forth. The first relates to the thing itself; the second to its time or season; and the last to our conduct with regard to preventing the evil we are treating of.

1. First, then, it may be asked: 1. How may a man know when he has entered into temptation? 2. What directions are to be given for preventing our entering into temptation? 3. What seasons are there in which a man may and ought to fear that an hour of temptation is at hand?

First Inquiry: How a Man May Know He Has Entered into Temptation

Our first inquiry is how a man may know whether he has entered into temptation or not. I say, then:

(1.) When a man is drawn into any sin, he may be sure that he has entered into temptation. All sin comes from temptation (Jas 1:14). Sin is a fruit that comes only from that root. However suddenly or violently a man is overtaken in or by any sin, yet it is from some temptation or other that he has been overtaken. So the apostle says in Gal 6:1. If a man is overtaken, caught in a fault, still he was tempted to it; for the apostle says, “watch yourself, or you also may be tempted,” that is, as that man was when he was overtaken, as it were, unawares. Men sometimes take no notice of this, to their great harm. When they are caught in a sin, they set themselves to repent of that sin, but they do not consider the temptation that caused it, so as to set themselves against that too and take care not to enter into it again. That is why they are quickly entangled by it again, though they have the greatest detestation of the sin itself that can be expressed. The man who would really win the victory over any sin must consider his temptations to it and strike at that root. Unless he is delivered from that, he will not be healed.

This is a folly that possesses many who nevertheless have a keen and living sense of sin. They are aware of their sins, but not of their temptations. They are displeased with the bitter fruit, but they cherish the poisonous root. So in the midst of their humbling themselves for sin, they will continue in the ways and the company, and in the pursuit of the ends, that occasioned that sin. Of this I will say more later.

(2.) Temptations have various degrees. Some rise to such a height, press on the soul so hard, torment and disquiet it so much, and fight so hard against all opposition made to them, that the man assaulted in this way cannot possibly doubt that he must wrestle with a special power of temptation. When a fever rages, a man knows he is sick, unless his disorder has made him delirious. Men’s lusts, as James tells us, “lure away and entice” them and seduce them to sin. But they do this by themselves, without any special urging, in a quieter, steadier, and calmer way. If they grow violent, if they drive the soul up and down and give it no rest, the soul may know that they have got the help of temptation.

Take an empty vessel and put it into some stream that is running toward the sea. It will certainly be carried there, according to the course and speed of the stream. But let strong winds rise upon it, and it will be driven violently against every bank and rock, until it is broken in pieces and swallowed up by the ocean. Men’s lusts will certainly carry them into eternal ruin (unless they are mortified in the death of Christ), but often without much noise, according to the course of the stream of their corruptions. But let the wind of strong temptations befall them, and they are driven into countless scandalous sins; and so, broken on every side, they are swallowed up in eternity. So it is with men in general, and so it is in particular cases. Hezekiah always had the root of pride in him. Yet it did not make him run up and down to show his treasure and his riches until he fell into temptation through the ambassadors of the king of Babylon. David too had that root. Yet he could keep back from numbering the people until Satan rose up and provoked him and urged him to do it. Judas was covetous from the beginning. Yet he did not plot to satisfy his covetousness by selling his Master until the devil entered into him, and by that Judas entered into temptation. The same may be said of Abraham, Jonah, Peter, and the rest. So when any lust or corruption whatever stirs up a tumult and disquiets the soul, and drives it violently toward sin, let the soul know that the lust has got the advantage of some outward temptation, though the soul does not yet see where. Or at least the lust has itself become a special temptation through some incitement or provocation that has befallen it, and it is to be watched more than usual.

(3.) Entering into temptation may be seen in its lesser degrees too. For instance, it is seen when the heart secretly begins to like the matter of the temptation, and is content to feed it and increase it by any ways it can without downright sin.

In particular, suppose a man begins to have a reputation for piety, wisdom, learning, or the like. He is much spoken of in that way; his heart is tickled to hear it, and his pride and ambition are moved by it. If this man now applies himself with all his strength to the things from which his reputation, esteem, and glory among men spring, with a secret eye to having them increased, he is entering into temptation. If he does not take heed, it will quickly make him a slave of lust. So it was with Jehu. He saw that his reputation for zeal was beginning to spread, and he gained honor by it. Jonadab comes his way, a good and holy man. “Now,” thinks Jehu, “I have an opportunity to grow in the honor of my zeal.” So he calls Jonadab to him and sets to work most earnestly. The things he did were good in themselves, but he had entered into temptation, and he served his lust in everything he did. So it is with many scholars. They find themselves esteemed and favored for their learning. This takes hold of the pride and ambition of their hearts. So they set themselves to study with all diligence day and night, which is a good thing in itself. But they do it to satisfy men’s thoughts and words about them, which they delight in; and so in all they do they make provision for the flesh, to gratify its lusts.

It is true that God often brings light out of this darkness and turns things to a better outcome. Perhaps a man has studied for a number of years with an eye on his lusts, his ambition, pride, and vainglory, rising early and going to bed late to satisfy them. Then God comes in with his grace, turns the soul to himself, plunders those Egyptian lusts, and so consecrates to the use of the tabernacle what was provided for idols.

Men may be entangled in this way in better things than learning, even in the profession of piety, in their labor in the ministry, and the like. Some men’s profession of religion is a snare to them. They are well thought of, and much honored on account of their profession of religion and strict walk. This often happens in the days in which we live, when everything is carried on by parties. Some find themselves, on the accounts mentioned, to be perhaps the darlings and “ingentia decora,” or the glory, of their party. If thoughts of this secretly creep into their hearts and move them to more than ordinary diligence and activity in their way and profession, they are entangled. Instead of aiming at more glory, they need to lie in the dust, with a sense of their own vileness. And this temptation is so subtle that often it needs no food to feed on except that the man entangled in it avoids all means and ways of honor and reputation, as long as it can whisper in his heart that this very avoidance is honorable. The same may be the condition of men, as I said, in preaching the gospel, in the work of the ministry. Many things in that work may bring them esteem: their ability, their plainness, their frequent preaching, their success. And in this sense all of these may be fuel for temptations. Let a man know, then, that when he likes what feeds his lust, and keeps it up by ways that are either good in themselves or not downright sinful, he has entered into temptation.

(4.) Suppose that through a man’s state or condition of life, or by any means whatever, it happens that his lust and some temptation meet with occasions and opportunities to provoke and stir it up. Let that man know, whether he perceives it or not, that he has certainly entered into temptation. I told you before that to enter into temptation is not merely to be tempted, but to be so under its power as to be entangled by it. Now, it is almost impossible for a man to have opportunities, occasions, and advantages suited to his lust and corruption and not be entangled. If ambassadors come from the king of Babylon, Hezekiah’s pride will throw him into temptation. If Hazael is king of Syria, his cruelty and ambition will make him rage savagely against Israel. If the priests come with their pieces of silver, Judas’s covetousness will instantly be at work to sell his Master. And many instances of the same kind may be given in the days in which we live. Some men think they can play at the hole of the asp and not be stung, touch pitch and not be defiled, take fire into their clothes and not be burned. But they will be mistaken. If your business, way of life, company, or anything else of that kind throws you upon such things, ways, and persons as suit your lust or corruption, know that you have entered into temptation; how you will come out, God only knows. Suppose a man who has any seeds of filthiness in his heart is engaged, in the course of his life, in light, empty, and foolish company. Whatever notice he takes of it, little, great, or none at all, he has undoubtedly entered into temptation. So it is with ambition in high places, with anger in a crowd of perplexing affairs, and with a polluted, corrupt imagination in empty company and in the reading of idle books or writings of vanity and folly. Fire and things that burn could more easily be brought to lie together without affecting each other than particular lusts and suitable objects or occasions for their exercise.

(5.) Suppose a man who used to have another frame is weakened, made negligent or formal in duty, so that he can omit duties, or content himself with a careless, lifeless performance of them, without delight, joy, or satisfaction to his soul. Let him know that, though he may not be aware of the particular disorder it consists in, he has entered into temptation in something or other; and in the end he will find it plain, to his trouble and peril. How many have we seen and known in our days who have fallen from a warm profession of religion into being negligent, careless, and indifferent in praying, reading, hearing, and the like! Give one instance of a man who has come off without a wound, and I dare say you may find a hundred against him who have shown that they were asleep on the top of the mast, that they were in the jaws of some vile temptation or other, which later brought forth bitter fruit in their lives and ways. From some few who return from folly we hear these mournful complaints every day: “Oh! I neglected private prayer; I did not meditate on the word or attend to hearing it, but rather despised these things. And yet I said I was rich and needed nothing. Little did I consider that this unclean lust was ripening in my heart, that this atheism and these abominations were brewing there.” This is a certain rule: if the heart of a man who has had another frame grows cold, negligent, or formal in the duties of the worship of God, whether as to their matter or their manner, one temptation or another has laid hold of him. The world, or pride, or uncleanness, or self-seeking, or malice and envy, or something or other has taken possession of his spirit. His hair is streaked with gray, though he does not perceive it. And this is to be observed as to the manner of duties as well as their matter. Men may keep up and frequent the duties of religion, as to their substance and matter, for many crooked reasons, especially to satisfy their consciences, when they have no heart for them and no life in them, as to the spirituality required in performing them. Sardis kept up the performance of duties and so had a reputation for being alive; but it lacked spiritual life in performing them, and so it was “dead” (Rev 3:1). It is as with disorders of the body. If a man finds his spirits faint, his heart oppressed, his head heavy, and his whole body out of sorts, then, though he does not yet actually burn with fever or rave, he will cry, “I fear I am entering into a fever, I am so out of order and unwell.” A man may do the same in this sickness of the soul. If he finds that his pulse does not beat rightly and evenly toward the duties of worship and communion with God, if his spirit is low and his heart faint in them, let him conclude that he has entered into temptation, though his lust does not yet burn or rage. It is high time for him to consider the particular causes of his disorder. If the head is heavy and slumbers in the things of grace, if the heart is cold in duties, evil lies at the door. And if such a soul escapes a great temptation to sin, yet it will not escape a great temptation by desertion. The bride cries, “I sleep” (Song 5:2), and says that she had taken off her robe and could not put it on again; she had no heart for duties and communion with Christ. What is the next news you have of her? “My beloved had turned and gone” (verse 6). Christ was gone, and she seeks him a long time and does not find him. There is such a fitness between the new nature worked and created in believers and the duties of the worship of God that they will not be parted or kept apart, unless it is by some disturbing disorder coming between them. The new creature feeds on these duties, is strengthened and increased by them, and finds sweetness in them. Indeed, in them it meets its God and Father. So of itself it cannot help delighting in them and desiring to be exercised in them, unless it is made sick by some temptation. This frame is described throughout the 119th Psalm. I say, the new creature is not thrown out of this frame and temper unless it is oppressed and disordered by one secret temptation or another. There are various other evidences of a soul’s entering into temptation, which it may discover by inquiring.

I set this forth to remove the false security we are prone to fall into, and to show what the special duty is that we are to apply ourselves to in the special seasons of temptation. For the man who has already entered into temptation is to apply himself to the means of getting free, not to labor to keep from entering in. How this may be done I will declare later.

Chapter V

The second case put forward, or inquiries resolved—What are the best directions for preventing entering into temptation—Those directions laid down—The directions given by our Savior: “Watch and pray”—What is included in them—(1.) A sense of the danger of temptation—(2.) That it is not in our power to keep ourselves—(3.) Faith in the promises of preservation—Prayer in particular—Watching.

Second Inquiry: Directions Against Entering into Temptation

Having seen the danger of entering into temptation, and having also uncovered the ways in which and the seasons when men usually do so, our second inquiry is this: what general directions may be given to preserve a soul from the condition that has been spoken of? And we see our Savior’s direction in the place mentioned before (Matt 26:41). He sums it all up in these two words, “Watch and pray.” I will labor a little to unfold them, and to show what is wrapped up and contained in them, both together and separately.

Watch and Pray, Taken Jointly

(1.) Included in them is a clear, abiding awareness of the great evil there is in entering into temptation. What a man watches and prays against he regards as evil to him, and to be avoided by all means.

This, then, is the first direction: Always bear in mind how great a danger it is for any soul to enter into temptation.

It is a woeful thing to consider how lightly most people think of this. As long as men can keep themselves from sin itself in open action, they are content; they hardly aim at more. All sorts of men will venture on any temptation in the world at any time. How young men will throw themselves into any company, any society! At first they are delighted with evil company, and then with the evil of the company! How useless are all warnings and exhortations to them to beware of such persons, who are debauched themselves, corrupters of others, and destroyers of souls! At first they will venture on the company while abhorring the thought of practicing its vices. But what is the outcome? Except for one here or there whom God snatches with a mighty hand from the jaws of destruction, they are all lost, and after a while they come to love the evil they abhorred at first. This open door to the ruin of souls is all too plain. And woeful experience makes it no less plain that it is almost impossible to fasten any fear or dread of temptation on many poor creatures who nevertheless profess a fear and abhorrence of sin. If only it were so with young men alone, those unaccustomed to the yoke of the Lord! But what sort of man is free from this folly in one thing or another? How many professing Christians I have known who would plead for their liberty, as they called it! They could hear anything, everything, all sorts of men, all men. They would try all things, whether these came to them in the way of God or not. On that account they would run to hear and attend to everyone who broached false and abominable opinions, every seducer, though most of the saints had branded him as such. For they had their liberty to hear such a one, they could do it; but they hated the opinions as much as anyone. What has been the outcome? I hardly ever knew any of them come off without a wound; most have had their faith overthrown. Let no man, then, claim to fear sin if he does not fear the temptation to it. They are too closely related to be separated. Satan has put them so close together that it is very hard for any man to put them apart. The man who delights in the root does not hate the fruit.

When men see that certain ways, company, courses, business, studies, and aims entangle them, make them cold and careless, act like water on coals to them, and make them disinclined to even and constant obedience in all things, sin lies at the door if they venture on them. It takes a tender frame of spirit to perform this duty, one aware of its own weakness and corruption, of the craft of Satan, of the evil of sin, and of the efficacy of temptation. And yet until we bring our hearts to this frame, through the considerations mentioned before or others like them that may be put forward, we will never free ourselves from sinful entanglements. Boldness toward temptation, springing from various pretexts, has, as is well known, ruined countless professing Christians in these days, and it still goes on throwing many down from their excellence. I have not the least hope of a more fruitful profession of religion among us until I see more fear of temptation. Sin will not seem great or heavy for long to anyone to whom temptations seem light or small.

This is the first thing wrapped up in this general direction: what is required of us is the daily exercise of our thoughts with an awareness of the great danger that lies in entering into temptation. Grief to the Spirit of God, disquiet in our own souls, loss of peace, and danger to our eternal welfare lie at the door. If the soul is not won over to keep this direction, all that follows will be of no value. Temptation despised will conquer. And if the heart is made tender and watchful here, half the work of securing good conduct is done. Let no man go any further who does not resolve to make use of this direction by keeping it conscientiously every day.

(2.) There is this in it too: it is not in our own power to keep and preserve ourselves from entering into temptation. That is why we are to pray to be preserved from it, because we cannot save ourselves.

This is another means of preservation. We have no strength to resist a temptation when it does come, when we have entered into it; we will fall under it unless God supplies us with sufficient grace. In the same way, to reckon that we have no power or wisdom to keep ourselves from entering into temptation, but must be kept by the power and wisdom of God, is a preserving principle (1 Pet 1:5). In all things we are “kept by God’s power.” Our Savior teaches us this, not only by directing us to pray that we will not be led into temptation, but also by praying for us himself that we may be kept from it: “I am not asking that You take them out of the world, but that You keep them from the evil” (John 17:15). That is, from the temptations of the world to evil, to sin: ἐκ τοῦ πονηροῦ, “out of the evil” that is in the world, which is temptation, for that is all that is evil in the world; or else from the evil one, who makes use of the world for temptation. Christ prays to his Father to keep us, and teaches us to pray that we may be kept in this way. It is not, then, a thing in our own power. The ways in which we enter into temptation are so many, so various, and so imperceptible; the means of it so efficacious and powerful; its entrances so deceitful, subtle, unnoticed, and plausible; our weakness and our unwatchfulness so beyond words, that we cannot keep or preserve ourselves from it in the least. We lack both the wisdom and the power for this work.

Let the heart, then, talk with itself like this: “I am poor and weak. Satan is subtle, cunning, and powerful, constantly watching for advantages against my soul. The world is earnest and pressing, and full of plausible pleas, countless pretexts, and ways of deceit. My own corruption is violent and in a tumult, enticing, entangling, conceiving sin, and warring in me, against me. The occasions and advantages of temptation are countless, in all that I do or suffer, in all my business and in all the persons I deal with. The first beginnings of temptation are unnoticed and plausible, so that, if I am left to myself, I will not know that I am ensnared until my bonds are made strong and sin has gained ground in my heart. Therefore I will rely on God alone for preservation, and I will continually look up to him for it.” This will make the soul always commit itself to the care of God and rest itself on him, and do nothing, undertake nothing, and so on, without asking his counsel. So a double advantage will come from keeping this direction, and both are of special use for the soul’s preservation from the evil it fears:

[1.] It engages the grace and compassion of God, who has called the fatherless and the helpless to rest on him. No soul that, sensing its need, rolled itself on him because of his gracious invitation ever lacked supplies.

[2.] It keeps the soul in a frame that is useful for its preservation in various ways. The man who looks to God for help in a right manner is both aware of his danger and conscientiously careful in using the means to preserve himself. How important these two things are in this case may easily be understood by those whose hearts are exercised in these things.

(3.) This also is in it: put faith to work on the promise of God for preservation. To believe that he will preserve us is a means of preservation. For God will certainly do this, or make a way for us to escape out of temptation if we fall into it while in such a believing frame. We are to pray for what God has promised. Our requests are to be governed by his promises and commands, which are of the same extent. Faith lays hold of the promises, and so finds relief in this case. James teaches us this in chapter 1:5–7. What we lack we must “ask God” for; but we “must ask in faith,” for otherwise we “should not expect to receive anything from the Lord.” This, then, is also in our Savior’s direction: that we put faith to work on the promises of God for our preservation from temptation. He has promised that he will keep us in all our ways; that we will be directed in a way in which, fools though we are, we will not go astray (Isa 35:8); that he will lead us, guide us, and deliver us from the evil one. Set faith to work on these promises of God, and expect a good and comforting outcome. It is not easy to conceive what a train of graces goes with faith when it goes out to meet Christ in the promises, nor what power for the preservation of the soul lies in this. But I have spoken of this elsewhere.4

Pray

(4.) Weigh these things separately, and first take prayer into consideration. To pray that we will not enter into temptation is a means of preserving us from it. All men who know anything of these things speak glorious things of this duty; and yet the truth is that not half of its excellence, power, and efficacy is known. It is not my business to speak of it in general. But this I say, for my present purpose: let the man who would be little in temptation be much in prayer. Prayer calls in the fitting help and aid that is laid up for us in Christ (Heb 4:16). It puts our souls into a frame of opposition to every temptation. When Paul had given instruction for taking up “the full armor of God,” so that we may resist and stand in a time of temptation, he adds this general close to the whole: “Pray in the Spirit at all times, with every kind of prayer and petition. To this end, keep watch with all perseverance in your+ prayers” (Eph 6:18).

Without this all the rest will have no efficacy for the end in view. So consider what weight he lays on it. “Pray … at all times”: that is, at all times and seasons, or always be ready and prepared to carry out that duty (Luke 18:1; Eph 6:18). “In the Spirit … with every kind of prayer and petition”: putting forth to God all kinds of desires that suit our condition, according to his will, in which we are helped by the Spirit. “To this end, keep watch”: so that we are not turned aside by anything whatever; and that not for a little while, but “with all perseverance,” a continuance drawn out to the utmost. So we will stand. The soul framed like this is in a sure position; and this is one of the means without which this work will not be done. If we do not abide in prayer, we will abide in cursed temptations. Let this, then, be another direction: abide in prayer, and that expressly for this purpose, that we “will not enter into temptation.” Let this be one part of our daily wrestling with God: that he would preserve our souls and keep our hearts and our ways, so that we are not entangled; that his good and wise providence will so order our ways and affairs that no pressing temptation befalls us; that he would give us diligence, carefulness, and watchfulness over our own ways. So we will be delivered when others are held fast with the cords of their own folly.

Chapter VI

Watching so that we do not enter into temptation—The nature and efficacy of that duty—Its first part, concerning the special seasons of temptation—The first season, in unusual prosperity—The second, in a slumber of grace—Third, a season of great spiritual enjoyment—The fourth, a season of self-confidence.

Watch the Seasons of Temptation

The other part of our Savior’s direction, namely to “watch,” is more general and extends to many particulars. I will fix on some things contained in it:

1. Watch the seasons in which men usually “enter into temptation.”

There are various seasons in which an hour of temptation is commonly at hand and will unavoidably seize the soul, unless it is delivered by mercy in the use of watchfulness. When we are in such a season, we are to be especially on our guard, so that we do not enter into the power of temptation or fall under it. Some of those seasons may be named:

(1.) A season of unusual outward prosperity usually comes with an hour of temptation. Prosperity and temptation go together; indeed, prosperity is a temptation, many temptations. That is because, without outstanding supplies of grace, it tends to put a soul into a frame and temper exposed to any temptation, and it provides fuel and food for them all. It has provision for lust and darts for Satan.

The wise man tells us that “the prosperity of fools will destroy them” (Prov 1:32). It hardens them in their way, makes them despise instruction, and makes them put far from them the evil day, whose terror should move them to amend. Without special help it has an inconceivably harmful influence on believers themselves. That is why Agur prays against riches, because of the temptation that comes with them: “Otherwise,” he says, “I may be full and deny You, saying, ‘Who is the Lord?’ ” (Prov 30:8, 9); that is, lest, being filled with them, he should forget the Lord, as God complains his people did (Hos 13:6). We know how David was mistaken in this case: “In prosperity I said, ‘I will never be shaken’ ” (Ps 30:6). All is well, and will be well. But what was at hand, what lay at the door, that David did not think of? “When You hid Your face, I was dismayed” (verse 7). God was ready to hide his face, and David to enter into a temptation of desertion, and he did not know it.

As to a prosperous condition, then: I will not run counter to Solomon’s counsel, “In the day of prosperity, rejoice” (Eccl 7:14). Rejoice in the God of your mercies, who does you good in his patience and forbearance, in spite of all your unworthiness. Yet I may add to it, from the same fountain of wisdom, “consider” too, lest evil lie at the door. A man in that state is in the midst of snares. Satan has many advantages against him. He forges darts out of all the man’s enjoyments; and if the man does not watch, he will be entangled before he knows it.

You lack what should steady and ballast your heart. Formality in religion will tend to creep up on you, and that lays the soul open to all temptations in their full power and strength. Satisfaction and delight in the comforts of created things, the poison of the soul, will tend to grow on you. In such a time be vigilant, be careful, or you will be overtaken. Job says that in his affliction “God has made my heart soft” (Job 23:16). There is a hardness, a lack of spiritual feeling that goes unnoticed, gathered in prosperity. If it is not watched against, it will expose the heart to the deceits of sin and the baits of Satan. “Watch and pray” in this season. Many men’s negligence in it has cost them dearly; their woeful experience cries out to us to take heed. Blessed is the man who fears always, but especially in a time of prosperity.

(2.) As was partly shown before, a time of the slumber of grace, of neglect in communion with God, of formality in duty, is a season to watch in, as one that certainly has some other temptation coming with it.

Let a soul in such a state wake up and look about him. His enemy is at hand, and he is ready to fall into a condition that may cost him dearly all the days of his life. His present state is bad enough in itself; but it is a sign of something worse that lies at the door. The disciples who were with Christ on the mount had not only a bodily drowsiness on them but a spiritual one. What does our Savior say to them? “Get up, watch and pray so that you+ will not enter into temptation.” We know how near one of them was to a bitter hour of temptation; and not watching as he ought, he immediately entered into it.

I mentioned before the case of the bride (Song 5:2–8). She slept; she was drowsy and unwilling to gird herself for a vigorous performance of duties, in lively, active communion with Christ. Before she knows it, she has lost her beloved. Then she moans, inquires, cries, and endures wounds, reproaches, and everything else before she finds him again. Consider your state and condition, then, O poor soul! Does your light burn dim? Or though it gives as great a blaze to others as before, do you no longer see the face of God in Christ by it as clearly as you once did (2 Cor 4:6)? Is your zeal cold? Or if it does the same works as before, is your heart no longer warmed in them with the love of God and love for God, so that you only go on in the course you have been in? Are you negligent in the duties of praying or hearing? Or if you keep them, do you no longer do it with the life and vigor you once did? Do you flag in your profession of religion? Or if you keep it up, are your wheels oiled by some crooked motives from within or without? Does your delight in the people of God grow faint and cold? Or is your love for them changing from what is purely spiritual into what is very fleshly, on account of their having principles and natural temperaments that suit yours, if not on worse foundations? If you are drowsing in such a condition as this, beware. You are falling into some woeful temptation that will break all your bones and give you wounds that will stay with you all the days of your life. Indeed, when you wake up, you will find that it has in fact laid hold of you already, though you did not perceive it. It has struck and wounded you, though you have not complained or sought relief or healing.

Such was the state of the church of Sardis (Rev 3:2). “What remains” was “about to die.” “Wake up,” says our Savior, “and strengthen” it, or something worse will befall you. If anyone who reads the word of this direction is in this condition, if he has any regard for his poor soul, let him wake up now, before he is entangled beyond recovery. Take this warning from God; do not despise it.

(3.) A season of great spiritual enjoyments is often turned, by the malice of Satan and the weakness of our hearts, into a season of danger in this matter of temptation.

We know how it was with Paul (2 Cor 12:7). He had glorious spiritual revelations of God and Jesus Christ. Immediately Satan falls on him; a messenger of Satan torments him, so that he earnestly begs for it to depart, and yet is left to struggle with it. At times God is pleased to give us special revelations of himself and his love, to fill the heart with his kindness. Christ takes us into the banqueting house and gives our hearts their fill of love. He does this by some notable work of his Spirit, overpowering us with a sense of love in the unspeakable privilege of adoption, and so fills our souls with joy unspeakable and glorious. A man would think this was the safest condition in the world. What soul does not cry with Peter on the mount, “It is good for us to be here,” to stay here forever? But very often some bitter temptation is at hand just then. Satan sees that, taken up with the joy before us, we quickly neglect many ways of approach to our souls, and in these he seeks and finds advantages against us. Is this, then, our state and condition? Does God at any time give us to drink from the rivers of pleasure at his right hand, and satisfy our souls with his kindness as with marrow and fatness? Let us not say, “We will never be shaken.” We do not know how soon God may hide his face, or a messenger of Satan may torment us.

Besides, a greater and worse deceit often lies in this business. Men cheat their souls with their own fancies instead of a sense of God’s love by the Holy Spirit. And when they are lifted up with their imaginations, it is beyond words how fearfully they are exposed to all kinds of temptations. How, then, can they find relief against their consciences in their own foolish fancies and deceits that they amuse themselves with? Do we not see such people every day, persons walking in the vanities and ways of this world, yet boasting of their sense of the love of God? Shall we believe them? Then we must not believe truth itself. And how woeful their condition must be!

(4.) A fourth season is a season of self-confidence; then temptation is usually at hand.

The case of Peter makes this clear: “I will not deny You. Even if all men deny You, I will not. Even if I were to die for it, I would not do it.” So the poor man spoke when he stood on the very brink of the temptation that cost him such bitter tears in the end. And this taught him to know himself so well all his days, and gave him such an understanding of the state of all believers, that when he had received more of the Spirit and of power, he still had less confidence. He saw it was right for others to have less too, and so he urges all men, “conduct yourselves in reverent fear during your stay as foreigners” (1 Pet 1:17); they are not to be confident and high as he was, lest they fall as he did. At the first trial he compares himself with others and boasts himself above them: “Even if all men forsake You, I will not.” He fears every man more than himself. But when our Savior afterward comes to him and puts the comparison to him directly, “Simon … do you love Me more than these?” (John 21:15), he has done with comparing himself with others, and only cries, “Yes, Lord, You know I love You.” He will lift himself up above others no more. Such a season often comes. Temptations are at large in the world, false doctrines with countless other allurements and provocations. Every one of us is ready to be very confident that we will not be caught by them. Even if all men fall into these follies, we would not. Surely we will never turn away from our walk with God; it is impossible that our hearts should be so dull. But the apostle says, “Do not be arrogant, but be afraid”; “the one who thinks he is standing firm should take heed not to fall.” Would you think that Peter, who had walked on the sea with Christ, had confessed him to be the Son of God, and had been with him on the mount when he heard the voice from the majestic glory, would instantly fall to cursing and swearing that he did not know him, at the word of a servant girl, when there was no legal inquiry after him and no proceedings against him or anyone in his condition? Let those who have any mind to beware of sin beware of self-confidence. And this is the first thing in our watching: to consider well the seasons in which temptation usually makes its approaches to the soul, and to be armed against them. And these are some of the seasons in which temptations are near at hand.

Chapter VII

Watch the Heart

The part of watchfulness against temptation that we have considered concerns the outward means, occasions, and advantages of temptation. Let us now go on to the part that concerns the heart itself, which temptation works on and entangles. Watching or keeping the heart, which we are bound to above all other keeping, also comes within the scope of this duty. For doing it rightly, take the following directions:

(1.) Let the man who would not enter into temptation labor to know his own heart, to be acquainted with his own spirit, his natural frame and temper, his lusts and corruptions, and his natural, sinful, or spiritual weaknesses. Then, finding where his weakness lies, he may be careful to keep at a distance from all occasions of sin.

Our Savior tells the disciples that “they did not know what spirit they were of”; under a pretense of zeal their spirit betrayed them into ambition and a desire for revenge. Had they known it, they would have watched over themselves. David tells us that he considered his ways: “I … kept myself from my iniquity” (Ps 18:23), the iniquity he was particularly prone to.

There are often advantages for temptations lying in men’s natural tempers and constitutions. Some are naturally gentle, easygoing, easily persuaded, and pliable. This is the noblest temper of nature, and the best and choicest ground for grace to grow in when it is well broken up and plowed. Yet if it is not watched over, it will be a means of being overtaken and entangled in temptation countless times. Others are earthy, contrary, and morose, so that envy, malice, selfishness, peevishness, harsh thoughts of others, and grumbling lie at the very door of their natures; they can hardly step out without being in the snare of one or another of these. Others are hot-tempered, and the like. Now, the man who would watch so that he does not enter into temptation needs to be acquainted with his own natural temper, so that he may continually watch over the treacheries that lie in it. Beware that you do not have a Jehu in you, who will make you drive furiously; or a Jonah in you, who will make you ready to grumble; or a David, who will make you hasty in your decisions, as he often was in the warmth and goodness of his natural temper. The man who does not watch this thoroughly, who is not precisely skilled in the knowledge of himself, will never be free from one temptation or another all his days.

Again, just as men have particular natural tempers which, according to how they are attended to and managed, prove either a great fomes [tinder] of sin or an advantage to the exercise of grace, so men may have particular lusts or corruptions which, through their natural constitution or upbringing, and other prejudices, have taken deep root and gained strength in them. This, too, must be found out by the man who would not enter into temptation. Unless he knows it, unless his eyes are always on it, unless he observes its workings, promptings, and advantages, it will continually entangle and ensnare him. This, then, is our sixth direction of this kind: labor to know your own frame and temper; what spirit you are of; what allies Satan has in your heart; where corruption is strong and where grace is weak; what stronghold lust has in your natural constitution; and the like. How many have had all their comforts blighted and their peace disturbed by their natural anger and peevishness! How many are made useless in the world by their contrariness and discontent! How many are disquieted even by their own gentleness and pliability! Be acquainted, then, with your own heart. Though it is deep, search it; though it is dark, inquire into it; though it gives all its disorders other names than they deserve, do not believe it. Men are utter strangers to themselves. They give flattering titles to their natural disorders. They strive to justify, cover over, or excuse the evils of their hearts that suit their natural tempers and constitutions, rather than to destroy them, and by these means they keep themselves from taking a clear and distinct view of them. If it were not so, they could not possibly hang in the same briers all their days without attempting to be delivered. Uselessness and scandal in professing Christians are branches that grow constantly on this root of not knowing their own frame and temper. And how few there are who will either study these things themselves or bear with those who would make them known to them!

(2.) When you know the state and condition of your heart as to the particulars mentioned, watch against all occasions and opportunities, employments, company, times alone, and business that tend to entangle your natural temper or provoke your corruption.

Perhaps there are some ways, some company, some business, that you never in your life escaped without suffering by them, more or less, because they are so suited to entice or provoke your corruption. Perhaps you are in a state and condition of life that ensnares you day by day, on account of your ambition, anger, discontent, or the like. If you have any love for your soul, it is time for you to wake up and to free yourself like a bird from the evil snare. Peter will not be in a hurry to come to the high priest’s hall again; nor would David walk again on the roof of his house when he should have been in the high places of the field. But the particulars of this point are so various, and of such different kinds for different persons, that it is impossible to list them (Prov 4:14, 15). No small part of the wisdom that consists in ordering our conduct rightly lies in this. Since we have so little power over our hearts once they meet with provocations that suit them, we are to keep the two apart, as a man would keep fire apart from the parts of the house he lives in that will burn.

(3.) Be sure to lay up provision in store against the approach of any temptation.

This also belongs to our watchfulness over our hearts. You+ will say, “What provision is meant, and where is it to be laid up?” Our hearts, as our Savior says, are our treasury. There we lay up whatever we have, good or bad, and from there we draw it for our use (Matt 12:35). It is in the heart, then, that provision is to be laid up against temptation. When an enemy comes near a fort or castle to besiege and take it, if he finds it well manned and furnished with provisions for a siege, and so able to hold out, he often withdraws and does not assault it. If Satan, the prince of this world, comes and finds our hearts fortified against his batteries and provided to hold out, he not only departs, but, as James says, he flees: “he will flee from you+” (Jas 4:7). As for the provision to be laid up, it is what is provided for us in the gospel. Gospel provisions will do this work; that is, keep the heart full of a sense of the love of God in Christ. This is the greatest preservative against the power of temptation in the world. Joseph had this, and so at the first appearance of a temptation he cries out, “So how could I do such a great evil and sin against God?” And that is the end of the temptation as far as he is concerned; it takes no hold on him, but departs. He was furnished with such a ready sense of the love of God that temptation could not stand before it (Gen 39:9). “Christ’s love compels us,” says the apostle, to live for him (2 Cor 5:14), and so, as a consequence, to withstand temptation. A man may, indeed he ought to, lay in provisions of the law too: fear of death, hell, and punishment, with the terror of the Lord in them. But these are far more easily conquered than the other; in fact, they will never stand alone against a vigorous assault. They are conquered in convicted persons every day. Hearts stored with them will struggle for a while, but they quickly give up. But store the heart with a sense of the love of God in Christ, with the eternal design of his grace, with a taste of the blood of Christ and his love in shedding it. Get a relish of the privileges we have by it: our adoption, justification, and acceptance with God. Fill the heart with thoughts of the beauty of holiness, as Christ designed it to be the end, outcome, and effect of his death. Then, in an ordinary course of walking with God, you will have great peace and security from the disturbance of temptations. When men can live and plod on in their profession of religion and are not able to say when they ever had any living sense of the love of God, or of the privileges we have in the blood of Christ, I do not know what they can have to keep them from falling into snares. The apostle tells us that the “peace of God” φρουρήσει τὰς καρδίας, “will guard your+ hearts” (Phil 4:7). Φρουρά is a military word, a garrison; so φρουρήσει means “will keep as in a garrison.” Now, a garrison has two things that go with it: first, it is exposed to the assaults of its enemies; secondly, there is safety in it from their attacks. So it is with our souls. They are exposed to temptations and assaulted continually; but if there is a garrison in them, or if they are kept as in a garrison, temptation will not enter, and so we will not enter into temptation. Now, how is this done? He says, “The peace of God will do it.” What is this “peace of God”? A sense of his love and favor in Jesus Christ. Let this abide in you+, and it will garrison you+ against all assaults whatever. Besides, there is something here in a special way that is also in all the rest of the directions: the thing itself stands in direct opposition to all the ways and means temptation can use to approach our souls. Striving to obtain and keep a sense of the love of God in Christ, by its very nature, heads off all the workings and insinuations of temptation. Let this be a third direction, then, in our watching against temptation: lay up a store of gospel provisions, which may make the soul a fortified place against all the assaults of temptation.

(4.) At the first approach of any temptation, since we are all tempted, the following directions are also suited to carry on the work of watching that we are pursuing:

[1.] Be always awake, so that you may make an early discovery of your temptation, and know it for what it is. Most men do not see their enemy until they are wounded by him. Indeed, others may at times see them deeply engaged while they themselves have no sense of it at all. They sleep without any sense of danger until others come and wake them by telling them that their house is on fire. Temptation in the impersonal sense, as it denotes a way, thing, or matter that is or may be used for the ends of temptation, is not easily discovered. Few take notice of it until it is too late and they find themselves entangled, if not wounded. Watch, then, to understand in good time the snares laid for you, to understand the advantages your enemies have against you before they gain strength and power, before they are incorporated with your lusts and have distilled poison into your soul.

[2.] Consider the aim and tendency of the temptation, whatever it is, and of all who are concerned in it. Those who actively join in your temptation are Satan and your own lusts. As for your own lust, I have shown elsewhere what it aims at in all its workings and enticings. It never rises up without intending the worst of evils. Every working of it would be a full-formed enmity against God. So look on it, in its first attempts, as your mortal enemy, whatever pretexts may be made. “I hate” it, says the apostle (Rom 7:15), that is, the working of lust in me. “I hate it; it is the greatest enemy I have. Oh, that it were killed and destroyed! Oh, that I were delivered out of its power!” Know, then, that in the first attempt or assault of any temptation, the most cursed, sworn enemy is at hand and is attacking you, and that for your utter ruin; so that it would be the greatest madness in the world to throw yourself into his arms to be destroyed. But I have spoken of this in my discourse on Mortification.

Has Satan, who has a share in every temptation, any friendlier aim and intention toward you? To beguile you as a serpent and to devour you as a lion is the friendship he owes you. I will add only this: the sin against the law that he tempts you to is not the thing he aims at. His design lies against your share in the gospel. He would make sin only a bridge to get over to better ground, from which to assault you as to your share in Christ. The one who today perhaps will say, “You may venture on sin, because you have a share in Christ,” will tomorrow tell you, effectually, that you have none, because you have done so.

[3.] Meet your temptation at its entrance with thoughts of faith concerning Christ on the cross; this will make it sink before you. Allow no talks, no dispute with it, if you would not enter into it. Say, “ ‘Christ Jesus, who died’: he died for such sins as these.” This is called taking up “the shield of faith, with which you+ can extinguish all the flaming arrows of the evil one” (Eph 6:16). Faith does it by laying hold of Christ crucified, of his love in that, and of what he suffered for sin there. Let your temptation be what it will, whether to sin, or to fear or doubt because of sin, or about your state and condition; it cannot stand before faith lifting up the standard of the cross. We know what means the Papists, who have lost the power of faith, use to keep up the form. They will sign themselves with the sign of the cross, or make crosses in the air, and by virtue of that work done they think they will scare away the devil. To put faith to work on Christ crucified is really to sign ourselves with the sign of the cross, and by it we will overcome the wicked one (1 Pet 5:9).

[4.] Suppose the soul has been overtaken by temptation and entangled unawares, so that it is now too late to resist its first entrances. What shall such a soul do so that it is not plunged into it and carried away by its power?

1st. Do as Paul did: beg God again and again to “take it away” from you (2 Cor 12:8). If you continue in this, you will certainly either be speedily delivered out of it, or receive sufficient grace not to be utterly defeated by it. Only, as I partly said before, do not occupy your thoughts so much with the things you are tempted to, which often raises further entanglements; but set yourself against the temptation itself. Pray against the temptation, that it may depart; and when that is taken away, the things themselves may be considered more calmly.

2dly. Flee to Christ in a special way, as one who was tempted, and beg him to give you help in this “time of need.” The apostle teaches us this in Heb 2:18: “Because He Himself suffered when He was tempted, He is able to help those who are being tempted.” This is its meaning: “When you+ are tempted and ready to faint, when you+ need help, and must have it or you+ die, put faith to work in a special way on Christ as he was tempted. That is, consider that he was tempted himself, that he suffered by it, and that he conquered all temptations; and that not merely on his own account, since he submitted to be tempted for our sakes, but for us.” (He conquered in and by himself, but for us.) And draw help from him; indeed, expect it (Heb 4:15, 16). Lie down at his feet, make your complaint known to him, beg his help, and it will not be in vain.

3dly. Look to Him who has promised deliverance. Consider that he is faithful and will not let you be tempted beyond what you can bear. Consider that he has promised a comforting outcome to these trials and temptations. Call to mind all the promises of help and deliverance he has made, and ponder them in your heart. And rest on this, that God has countless ways you do not know of to bring you deliverance. For instance:

(1st.) He can send an affliction that will mortify your heart to the matter of the temptation, whatever it is. Then what was before a sweet morsel under the tongue will have no taste or relish for you; your desire for it will be killed. So it was with David. Or:

(2dly.) He can, by some providence, change the whole state of things from which your temptation arises, and so take the fuel from the fire and make it go out of itself. So it was with the same David in the day of battle. Or:

(3dly.) He can crush Satan under your feet, so that he will not dare to suggest anything more to your harm (the God of peace will do it), and you will hear from him no more. Or:

(4thly.) He can give you such a supply of grace that you may be freed, though not from the temptation itself, yet from its tendency and danger. So it was with Paul. Or:

(5thly.) He can give you such a comforting persuasion of good success in the outcome that you will have refreshment in your trials and be kept from the trouble of the temptation. So it was with the same Paul. Or:

(6thly.) He can utterly remove it and make you a complete conqueror. And he has countless other ways of keeping you from entering into temptation so as to be defeated by it.

4thly. Consider where the temptation that has overtaken you has made its entrance, and by what means, and repair that breach with all speed. Stop up the passage the waters have made to get in. Deal with your soul like a wise physician. Inquire when, how, and by what means you fell into this disorder. If you find that negligence, carelessness, or failure to keep watch over yourself lay at the bottom of it, fix your soul there. Bewail it before the Lord, repair that breach, and then go on to the work that lies before you.

Chapter VIII

The last general direction, Rev 3:10: Watch against temptation by constantly “keeping the word of Christ’s patience”—What that word is—How it is kept—How keeping it will keep us from the power of temptation.

The Last General Direction: Keep the Word of Christ’s Patience

The directions dwelt on in the previous chapters are partly given to us, in their various particulars, here and there in the Scripture, and partly arise from the nature of the thing itself. One general direction remains, which takes in all that went before and adds many more particulars to them. It contains a proven antidote against the poison of temptation, a remedy that Christ himself has marked with a seal of efficacy and success. It is given us in Rev 3:10, in the words of our Savior himself to the church of Philadelphia: “Because you have kept the word of My patience,” he says, “I will also keep you from the hour of temptation that is about to come upon the whole world, to test those who dwell on the earth.” Christ is “the same yesterday and today and forever.” As he dealt with the church of Philadelphia, so he will deal with us. If we “keep the word of his patience,” he will “keep us from the hour of temptation.” Since, then, this is a way of rolling the whole care of this weighty matter on him who is able to bear it, it calls for our special consideration.

So I will show (1.) what it is to “keep the word of Christ’s patience,” so that we may know how to do our duty; and (2.) how this will be a means of our preservation, which will establish us in the faith of Christ’s promise.

(1.) The word of Christ is the word of the gospel: the word he revealed from the bosom of the Father; the word of the Word; the word spoken in time by the eternal Word. So it is called “the word of Christ” (Col 3:16), or “the gospel of Christ” (Rom 1:16; 1 Cor 9:12), and “the doctrine of Christ” (Heb 6:1). It is “of Christ” as its author (Heb 1:1, 2), and as concerning him, its chief subject or matter (2 Cor 1:20). Now, this word is called “the word of Christ’s patience,” or tolerance and forbearance, on account of the patience and long-suffering that the Lord Christ exercises toward the world, and toward all persons in it, in administering it. He does so both actively and passively, in bearing with men and in enduring things from them:

[1.] He is patient toward his saints; he bears with them, and suffers from them. He “is patient with us” (2 Pet 3:9), that is, with those who believe. The gospel is the word of Christ’s patience even to believers. A soul acquainted with the gospel knows that no attribute of Christ is made more glorious in it than his patience. He bears with so many unkindnesses, so many groundless breaches, so many neglects of his love, so many affronts to his grace, so many violations of commitments; and this shows his gospel to be not only the word of his grace, but also the word of his patience. He also suffers from them in all the reproaches they bring on his name and ways; and he suffers in them, for “in all their distress, He too is afflicted.”

[2.] Toward his elect who are not yet effectually called. He stands waiting at the door of their hearts and knocks to be let in (Rev 3:20). He deals with them by every means, and still stands and waits until his head is “drenched with dew,” his hair “with the dampness of the night” (Song 5:2), enduring the cold and discomforts of the night so that he may be let in when his morning comes. Often for a long season they scorn him in his person, persecute him in his saints and ways, and revile him in his word, while he stands at the door in the word of his patience, with his heart full of love toward their poor rebellious souls.

[3.] Toward the perishing world. That is why the time of his kingdom in this world is called the time of his “patience” (Rev 1:9). He “bore with great patience the vessels of His wrath” (Rom 9:22). As long as the gospel is administered in the world, he is patient toward its men, until the saints in heaven and earth are astonished and cry out, “How long?” (Ps 13:1, 2; Rev 6:10). And the men of the world themselves mock him as if he were an idol (2 Pet 3:4). He endures bitter things from them in his name, ways, worship, saints, promises, and threats, in all that concerns his honor and love; and yet he passes them by, lets them alone, and does them good. Nor will he cut short this way of dealing until the gospel is preached no more. Patience must go with the gospel.

Now, this is the word that is to be kept, so that we may be kept from “the hour of temptation.”

(2.) Three things are implied in keeping this word: 1. knowledge; 2. valuing it; 3. obedience.

[1.] Knowledge. The man who will keep this word must know it and be acquainted with it in four ways: 1st, as a word of grace and mercy, to save him; 2dly, as a word of holiness and purity, to sanctify him; 3dly, as a word of liberty and power, to ennoble him and set him free; 4thly, as a word of consolation, to support him in every condition.

1st. As a word of grace and mercy, able to save us. “It is the power of God for salvation” (Rom 1:16); “the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation” (Titus 2:11); “the word of His grace, which can build you+ up and give you+ an inheritance among all who are sanctified” (Acts 20:32); “the word … which can save your+ souls” (Jas 1:21). When the word of the gospel is known as a word of mercy, grace, and pardon, as the only evidence for life, as the deed that conveys an eternal inheritance; when the soul finds it to be such to itself, it will strive to keep it.

2dly. As a word of holiness and purity, able to sanctify him. “You+ are already clean because of the word I have spoken to you+,” says our Savior (John 15:3). His prayer in chapter 17:17 is to the same end. The man who does not know the word of Christ’s patience as a sanctifying, cleansing word, in its power on his own soul, neither knows it nor keeps it. The empty profession of religion in our days does not know one step toward this duty; and that is why most are so overpowered by temptations. Men full of self, of the world, of fury, ambition, and almost all unclean lusts, still talk of keeping the word of Christ! See 1 Pet 1:2; 2 Tim 2:19.

3dly. As a word of liberty and power, to ennoble him and set him free. It sets him free not only from the guilt of sin and from wrath, for it does that as a word of grace and mercy; and not only from the power of sin, for it does that as a word of holiness; but also from all outward regard for men or the world that might entangle or enslave him. It declares us to be Christ’s freemen, in bondage to no one (John 8:32; 1 Cor 7:23). It does not free us from due subjection to superiors, nor from any duty, nor does it free us to any sin (1 Pet 2:16). But in two respects it is a word of freedom, liberty, largeness of mind, power, and deliverance from bondage:

(1st.) With respect to conscience in the worship of God (Gal 5:1).

(2dly.) With respect to ignoble, slavish regard for the men or things of the world in the course of our pilgrimage. The gospel gives a free, large, and noble spirit, in subjection to God and to no one else. In it is given a spirit “not … of fear, but of power, love, and a sound mind” (2 Tim 1:7), a mind “without being frightened in any way” (Phil 1:28), not swayed by any side interest whatever. Nothing is more unworthy of the gospel than a mind in bondage to persons or things, prostituting itself to the lusts of men or the frights of the world. And the man who knows the word of Christ’s patience in this way, really and in power, is by that very knowledge freed from countless, unspeakable temptations.

4thly. As a word of consolation, to support him in every condition and to be a full portion when he lacks everything. It is a word that comes with “an inexpressible and glorious joy.” It gives support, relief, refreshment, satisfaction, peace, consolation, joy, boasting, and glory in every condition whatever. To know the word of Christ’s patience in this way, to know the gospel in this way, is the first part, and a great part, of this condition of our preservation from the hour and power of temptation.

[2.] Valuing what is known in this way belongs to the keeping of this word. It is to be kept as a treasure. Τὴν καλὴν παρακαταθήκην, that excellent “depositum” [deposit, the thing entrusted], that is, the word of the gospel: “Keep it,” says the apostle, “with the help of the Holy Spirit” (2 Tim 1:14). And, “hold firmly to the faithful word” (Titus 1:9). It is a good treasure, a faithful word; hold it firmly. It is a word that takes in the whole cause of Christ in the world. To value that as our chief treasure is to keep the word of Christ’s patience. Those who would have Christ’s regard in the time of temptation are not to be regardless of the things that concern him.

[3.] Obedience. Personal obedience, in the keeping of all the commands of Christ without exception, is the keeping of his word (John 14:15). Close adherence to Christ in holiness and obedience in all things, at the very time when the opposition the gospel of Christ meets in the world makes it notably the word of his patience, is the life and soul of the duty required.

Now, all these are to be carried on with such intensity of mind and spirit, such care of heart, and such diligence of the whole person, as to make up a keeping of this word; and it evidently includes all these considerations.

We have arrived, then, at the sum of this safeguarding duty, this condition of freedom from the power of temptation. Suppose a man is duly acquainted with the gospel in its excellences, as a word of mercy, holiness, liberty, and consolation to him. He values it, in all that concerns it, as his choicest and only treasure. He makes it his business and the work of his life to give himself up to it, obeying it in all things, and especially when opposition and apostasy try the patience of Christ to the utmost. That man will be preserved from the hour of temptation.

This takes in all that went before, and it excludes all other ways of reaching the end in view. Let no man think that without this he will be kept for one hour from entering into temptation; wherever he fails in it, there temptation enters. That this will be a sure preservative may appear from the following considerations:

(1.) It has the promise of preservation, and this alone has it. It is solemnly promised on this account to the church of Philadelphia, in the place mentioned. When a great trial and temptation was to come on the world at the opening of the seventh seal, a charge is given for the preservation of God’s sealed ones (Rev 7:3), and they are described as those who keep the word of Christ; for the promise is that it should be so.

Now, in every promise there are three things to be considered: [1.] the faithfulness of the Father, who gives it; [2.] the grace of the Son, which is its matter; [3.] the power and efficacy of the Holy Spirit, who puts the promise into effect. And all these are pledged for the preservation of such persons from the hour of temptation.

[1.] The faithfulness of God goes with the promise. Our deliverance rests on this account (1 Cor 10:13). Though we are tempted, yet we will be kept from the hour of temptation; it will not grow too strong for us. What comes on us we will be able to bear, and what would be too hard for us we will escape. But what assurance do we have of this? Even the faithfulness of God: “God is faithful; He will not let you+ …,” and so on. And in what is God’s faithfulness seen and exercised? “He who promised is faithful” (Heb 10:23); his faithfulness consists in his fulfilling his promises. “He remains faithful, for He cannot deny Himself” (2 Tim 2:13). So by being under the promise we have the faithfulness of God pledged for our preservation.

[2.] In every promise of the covenant there is the grace of the Son; that is the subject matter of all the promises. “I will keep you.” How? “By my grace with you.” So whatever help the grace of Christ can give, a soul that has a right to this promise will enjoy it in the hour of temptation. Paul’s temptation rose very high; it was likely to have come to its prevailing hour. He “pleaded with the Lord,” that is, the Lord Jesus Christ, for help (2 Cor 12:8), and received this answer from him: “My grace is sufficient for you” (verse 9). That it was the Lord Christ and his grace he had especially to deal with is evident from the close of that verse: “I will boast all the more gladly in my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may rest on me,” or, “that the efficacy of the grace of Christ in preserving me may be made evident.” So too Heb 2:18.

[3.] The efficacy of the Spirit goes with the promises. He is called “the Holy Spirit of promise,” not only because he is promised by Christ, but also because he effectually makes good the promise and brings it to fulfillment in our souls. He too, then, is pledged to preserve the soul that walks according to the rule laid down. See Isa 59:21. So where the promise is, there is all this help. The faithfulness of the Father, the grace of the Son, and the power of the Spirit are all pledged to our preservation.

(2.) This constant, complete keeping of Christ’s word of patience will keep the heart and soul in such a frame that no prevailing temptation, by virtue of any advantages whatever, can seize on it so as to prevail against it totally. So David prays, “May integrity and uprightness preserve me” (Ps 25:21). This integrity and uprightness is the Old Testament’s keeping of the word of Christ: close walking with God in all things. Now, how can they preserve a man? Why, by keeping his heart in such a frame, so defended on every side, that no evil can approach him or take hold of him. Let a man fail in his integrity, and he has an open place for temptation to enter (Isa 57:21). To keep the word of Christ is to keep it in all things, as has been shown. This exercises grace in all the faculties of the soul and surrounds it with the full armor of God. The understanding is full of light; the affections are full of love and holiness. Let the wind blow from whatever quarter it will, the soul is fenced and fortified. Let the enemy assault when he pleases, or by what means he pleases, everything in the soul of such a man is on guard; “How could I do such a great evil and sin against God?” is at hand. Deliverance and security arise from this source especially on two accounts:

[1.] By the mortification of the heart to the matter of temptations. The prevailing of any temptation comes from this: the heart is ready to join with its matter. There are lusts within suited to the proposals of the world or of Satan from without. That is why James traces all temptations back to our own lusts (1:14), because temptations either proceed from them or are made effectual by them, as has been declared. Why do terror or threats turn us aside from due constancy in doing our duty? Is it not because there is unmortified, fleshly fear remaining in us, which raises a tumult in such a season? Why do the allurements of the world and compromises with men entangle us? Is it not because our affections are entangled with the things and considerations set before us? Now, keeping the word of Christ’s patience in the way declared keeps the heart mortified to these things, and so it is not easily entangled by them. The apostle says, “I have been crucified with Christ” (Gal 2:20). The man who keeps close to Christ is crucified with him, and is dead to all the desires of the flesh and the world; as it is more fully in chapter 6:14. Here the match is broken off, and all love, all entangling love, is dissolved. The heart is crucified to the world and all things in it. Now, the matter of almost all temptations is taken from the world; its men or its things make them up. “As to these things,” says the apostle (and so it is with everyone who keeps the word of Christ), “I am crucified to them. My heart is mortified to them. I have no desire for them, no affection toward them, and no delight in them; and they are crucified to me. The crowns, glories, thrones, pleasures, and profits of the world: I see nothing desirable in them. The lusts and sensual pleasures, the love, regard, and honors of men, name and reputation among them: they are all a thing of nothing. I have no value or esteem for them.” This soul is safeguarded from the assaults of many temptations. When Achan saw “a beautiful cloak from Babylon, two hundred shekels of silver, and a bar of gold,” first he “coveted them,” and then he “took them” (Josh 7:21). Temptation subtly spreads before men’s eyes the Babylonian cloak of favor, praise, and peace, the silver of pleasure or profit, and the golden contentments of the flesh. If there is something alive and unmortified in them that will immediately fall to coveting, then whatever fear of punishment may follow, the heart or the hand will be put forth to iniquity.

This, then, is where the security of the frame described lies: it is always accompanied by a mortified heart, crucified to the things that are the matter of our temptations. Without this it is utterly impossible for us to be preserved for one moment when any temptation befalls us. If liking and love for the things offered, slipped in, and commended in the temptation are alive and active in us, we will not be able to resist and stand.

[2.] In this frame the heart is filled with better things and their excellence, so far as to be fortified against the matter of any temptation. See what resolve this brings Paul to (Phil 3:8): all things are “loss” and “rubbish” to him. Who would go out of his way to get his arms full of loss and rubbish? And where does he get this estimate of the most desirable things in the world? From his precious estimate of the excellence of Christ. So too in verse 10: when the soul is exercised in communion with Christ and in walking with him, he drinks new wine, and cannot desire the old things of the world, for he says, “The new is better.” He tastes every day how gracious the Lord is, and so he does not long for the sweetness of forbidden things, which indeed have none. The man who makes it his business to eat daily from the tree of life will have no appetite for other fruit, though the tree that bore it seems to stand in the middle of paradise. The bride makes this the means of her preservation: the excellence above all other desirable things that she found in Christ and his graces by daily communion. Let a soul exercise itself in communion with Christ in the good things of the gospel, pardon of sin, fruits of holiness, hope of glory, peace with God, joy in the Holy Spirit, dominion over sin, and it will have a mighty preservative against all temptations. As the full soul loathes honey, so a soul filled with fleshly, earthly, sensual contentments finds no relish or flavor in the sweetest spiritual things. In the same way, the man who is satisfied with the kindness of God as with marrow and fatness, who is entertained every day at the banquet of wine, of aged wine, finely refined, has a holy contempt for the baits and allurements that lie in prevailing temptations, and is safe.

(3.) The man who keeps the word of Christ’s patience in this way is always furnished with preserving considerations and preserving principles, moral and real advantages for his preservation.

[1.] He is furnished with preserving considerations, which powerfully influence his soul in walking diligently with Christ. Besides the sense of duty that is always on him, he considers:

1st. How much Christ, whom his soul loves, is concerned in him and in his careful walking. He considers that the presence of Christ is with him and his eye on him; that Christ weighs his heart and ways, as one greatly concerned in how he conducts himself in a time of trial. Christ shows that he does so in Rev 2:19–23. Christ considers everything, what is acceptable and what is to be rejected. The man knows that Christ is concerned for his own honor, that his name not be spoken evil of because of him. He knows that Christ is concerned in love for his soul, having this design on him, to “present” him “holy, unblemished, and blameless in His presence” (Col 1:22); and his Spirit is grieved where he is interrupted in this work. He knows that Christ is concerned on account of his gospel, its progress and acceptance in the world; its beauty would be smeared, its good things reviled, and its progress stopped, if such a man were overcome. And he knows that Christ is concerned in his love for others, who are grievously caused to stumble, and perhaps ruined, by the failures of such men. When Hymenaeus and Philetus fell, they overthrew the faith of some. So when intricate, perplexing, entangling temptations arise, public, private, or personal, such a soul, exercised in keeping the word of Christ’s patience, says: “Shall I now be careless? Shall I be negligent? Shall I go along with the world and its ways? Oh, what thoughts of heart he has concerning me, whose eye is on me! Shall I despise his honor, scorn his love, trample his gospel in the mud under the feet of men, and turn others aside from his ways? Shall a man like me flee, and give up resisting? It cannot be.” There is no man who keeps the word of the patience of Christ who is not full of this soul-pressing consideration. It dwells on his heart and spirit, and the love of Christ compels him to keep his heart and ways in this way (2 Cor 5:14).

2dly. The great consideration of the temptations of Christ on his behalf, and of the victory Christ won in all assaults for his sake and his good, dwells on his spirit too. The prince of this world came upon him; the glory of this world was shown to him; everything in earth or hell that has either allurement or terror in it was set before him, to turn him aside from the work of mediation he had undertaken for us. He calls this whole life of his the time of his “temptations.” But he resisted all, conquered all, and has become a Captain of salvation to those who obey him. “And,” says the soul, “shall this temptation, these arguings, this plausible pretext, this sloth, this self-love, this sensuality, this bait of the world, turn me aside and prevail over me, so that I desert him who went before me, for my good, in the ways of all the temptations his holy nature was exposed to?”

3dly. Dark thoughts of the loss of love, of the smiles of Christ’s face, also often exercise such a soul. He knows what it is to enjoy the favor of Christ, to have a sense of his love, to be accepted in his approaches to him, and to converse with him. And perhaps he has at times been at some loss in this, and so knows also what it is to be in the dark, at a distance from him. See how the bride behaves in such a case (Song 3:4). When she had found him again, she held him; she would not let him go; she would lose him no more.

[2.] The man who keeps the word of Christ’s patience has preserving principles by which he is moved. Some of them may be mentioned:

1st. In all things he lives by faith, and is moved by it in all his ways (Gal 2:20). Now, faith, when put to use, has the power of preservation from temptation joined to it on two accounts:

(1st.) Because it empties the soul of its own wisdom, understanding, and fullness, so that it may act in the wisdom and fullness of Christ. The only counsel for preservation in trials and temptations lies in the wise man’s words, “Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding” (Prov 3:5). This is the work of faith; it is faith; it is to live by faith. The great failing of men in trials is their leaning toward, or leaning on, their own understanding and counsel. What is the outcome of it? “His vigorous stride is shortened, and his own counsel throws him down” (Job 18:7). First he is hampered, and then he is thrown down, and all by his own counsel, until he comes to be ashamed of it, as Ephraim was (Hos 10:6). Whenever in our trials we consult our own understandings and listen to our own reasonings, though they seem good and to tend toward our preservation, the principle of living by faith is stifled, and in the end we will be thrown down by our own counsels. Now, nothing can empty the heart of this self-fullness but faith: living by it, not living to ourselves, but having Christ live in us by our living by faith on him.

(2dly.) Faith, making the soul poor, empty, helpless, and destitute in itself, enlists the heart, will, and power of Jesus Christ for its help. I have spoken of this more fully elsewhere.

2dly. Love for the saints, with care that they do not suffer on our account, is a great preserving principle in a time of temptations and trials. How powerful this was in David he declares in that earnest prayer: “May those who hope in You not be ashamed through me, O Lord God of Hosts; may those who seek You not be dishonored through me, O God of Israel” (Ps 69:6). That is, “Oh, let me not fail in such a way that those for whom I would lay down my life are put to shame, spoken evil of, dishonored, reviled, and despised on my account, for my failings.” A selfish soul, whose love is turned wholly inward, will never hold out in a time of trial.

Many other considerations and principles that go with those who keep the word of Christ’s patience in the way described above might be listed; but I will be content to have pointed to these.

And now will it be easy to determine why so many in our days are overcome in the time of trial, why the hour of temptation comes on them and bears them down before it, more or less? Is it not because, among the great multitude of professing Christians we have, there are few who keep the word of the patience of Christ? If we willfully neglect or throw away our share in the promise of preservation, is it any wonder if we are not preserved? An hour of temptation has come upon the world to test those who dwell in it. It puts forth its power and efficacy in various ways. There is no way or thing in which it may not be seen working and showing itself. It appears in worldliness; in sensuality; in loose conduct; in neglect of spiritual duties, private and public; in foolish, loose, devilish opinions; in haughtiness and ambition; in envy and wrath; in strife and quarreling, revenge, and selfishness; in atheism and contempt of God. These are only branches of the same root, bitter streams of the same fountain, nourished by peace, prosperity, false security, the apostasies of professing Christians, and the like. And, alas! how many fall daily under the power of this temptation in general! How few keep their garments girded about them and undefiled! And if some urgent, particular temptation befalls anyone, what instances have we of almost anyone escaping? May we not describe our condition as the apostle described that of the Corinthians under an outward affliction: “many among you+ are weak and sick, and a number of you+ have fallen asleep”? Some are wounded, some defiled, many utterly lost. What is the spring and fountain of this sad state of things? Is it not what has been said? We do not keep the word of Christ’s patience in close walking with him in all things, and so we lose the benefit of the promise given and joined to it.

If I set out to give instances of this, of professing Christians falling short of keeping the word of Christ, it would be a long work. These four headings would take in most of them. First, conformity to the world, from which Christ has redeemed us, in almost all things, with joy and delight in indiscriminate compromises with the men of the world. Secondly, neglect of the duties Christ has commanded, from close meditation to public ordinances. Thirdly, strife, variance, and quarreling among ourselves, woeful judging and despising of one another on account of things foreign to the bond of communion between the saints. Fourthly, self-fullness in principles and selfishness in ends. Now, where these things are, are men not fleshly? Is the word of Christ’s patience effectual in them? Will they be preserved? They will not.

Would you+, then, be preserved and kept from the hour of temptation? Would you+ watch against entering into it? Then take the following cautions, drawn from what has been set down in this chapter:

1. Beware of leaning on deceitful helps, such as:

(1.) Your+ own counsels, understandings, and reasonings. However plausibly you+ argue with them in your+ own defense, they will leave you+ and betray you+. When the temptation rises to any height, they will all turn around, take the side of your+ enemy, and plead as much for the matter of the temptation, whatever it is, as they pleaded before against its end and outcome.

(2.) The most vigorous efforts, by prayer, fasting, and other such means, against the particular lust, corruption, or temptation you+ are exercised with and have to deal with. This will not help you+ if in the meantime there are neglects on other accounts. A man may be heard wrestling, crying, and striving over some particular temptation, and then immediately fall into worldly ways, worldly compromises, looseness, and negligence in other things. It is righteous of Jesus Christ to leave such a man to the hour of temptation.

(3.) The general security of the saints’ perseverance and preservation from total apostasy. Every guarantee God gives us is good in its kind and for the purpose for which it is given to us. But to use it for another end than the one it is given for is not good or profitable. To use the general assurance of preservation from total apostasy to hold up the spirit in the face of a particular temptation will not benefit the soul in the end; for in spite of that, this or that temptation may prevail. Many comfort themselves with this until they find themselves in the depth of perplexities.

2. Apply yourselves+ to this great preservative, faithfully keeping the word of Christ’s patience in the midst of all trials and temptations:

(1.) In particular, consider wisely where the word of Christ’s patience is most likely to suffer in the days in which we live and the seasons that pass over us, and so set yourselves+ vigorously to keep it in that particular above all. You+ will say, “How shall we know where the word of Christ’s patience is likely to suffer in any season?” I answer: consider what works he is especially doing in any season; neglect of his word with regard to them is where his word is likely to suffer. For instance, the works of Christ in which he has been especially engaged in our days and seasons seem to be these:

[1.] Pouring contempt on the great men and great things of the world, with all its enjoyments. He has laid bare the nakedness of all earthly things by overturning, overturning, overturning both men and things, to make way for the things that cannot be shaken.

[2.] Acknowledging the lot of his own inheritance in a distinguishing way, putting a difference between the precious and the vile, and making his people dwell alone, not reckoned among the nations.

[3.] Being near to faith and prayer, honoring them above all the strength and counsels of the sons of men.

[4.] Recovering his ordinances and institutions from the fleshly administration they were in bondage under through the lusts of men, and bringing them forth in the beauty and the power of the Spirit.

In such a season, then, where must the special neglect of the word of Christ’s patience lie? Is it not in setting a value on the world and its things, which he has stained and trampled underfoot? Is it not in slighting his special lot, his people, and lumping them together with the men of the world? Is it not in leaning on our own counsels and understandings? Is it not in defiling his ordinances by giving the outer court of the temple to be trampled by unsanctified persons? Let us be watchful, then, and keep the word of the patience of Christ in these things, if we love our own preservation.

(2.) In this frame, press the Lord Jesus Christ with his blessed promises, and with all the considerations that may take and hold the King in his galleries, that may work on the heart of our blessed and merciful High Priest to give fitting help in time of need.

Chapter IX

A general exhortation to the duty prescribed.

A General Exhortation

Having now gone through the considerations of the duty of watching so that we do not enter into temptation, I suppose I need not add motives for keeping it. Those who are not moved by their own sad experiences, or by the importance of the duty as laid down at the beginning of this discourse, must be left by me to the further patience of God. I will only close the whole with a general exhortation to those who are in any measure prepared for it by considering what has been said. Suppose you+ went into a hospital and saw many persons lying sick and weak, sore and wounded, with many foul diseases and disorders. Suppose you+ asked them how they fell into this condition, and they all agreed in telling you+ that this or that thing was the occasion of it: “That is how I got my wound,” says one; “And my disease,” says another. Would it not make you+ a little careful how you+ dealt with that thing or place, and what you+ had to do with it? Surely it would. Suppose you+ went to a dungeon and saw many miserable creatures bound in chains for a coming day of execution. Suppose you+ asked by what way and means they were brought into that condition, and they all fixed on one and the same thing. Would you+ not take care to avoid it? So it is with entering into temptation. Ah! how many poor, miserable, spiritually wounded souls we have everywhere! One is wounded by one sin, another by another; one falls into filthiness of the flesh, another into filthiness of the spirit. Ask them now how they came into this state and condition. They must all answer, “Alas! we entered into temptation; we fell into cursed snares and entanglements; and that has brought us into the woeful condition you+ see!” Indeed, if a man could look into the dungeons of hell and see the poor damned souls who lie bound in chains of darkness, and hear their cries, what would he be taught? What do they say? Are they not cursing their tempters, and the temptations they entered into? And shall we be negligent in this? Solomon tells us that the naive man who follows the adulteress “does not know that the dead are there,” that “her house sinks down to death, and her tracks to the departed spirits” (and he repeats this three times); and that is why he ventures on her snares. If you+ knew what has been done by entering into temptation, perhaps you+ would be more watchful and careful. Men may think they will do well enough in spite of it. But “Can a man embrace fire and his clothes not be burned? Can a man walk on hot coals without scorching his feet?” (Prov 6:27, 28). No such thing; men do not come out of their temptation without wounds, burns, and scars. I do not know any place in the world where there is more need to press this exhortation than in this place. Go to our various colleges and ask after this or that young man. What is the answer in the case of many? “Ah! that one was very promising for a season, but he fell into bad company, and he is quite lost. That one had some good beginnings of religion, and we had great expectations of him; but he has fallen into temptation.” And so it is in other places. “That one was useful and humble, and adorned the gospel; but now he is so woefully entangled with the world that he has become all self, and has no sap or savor. That one was humble and zealous; but he has been promoted, and has lost his first love and ways.” Oh! how full the world is, how full this place is, of these woeful examples, to say nothing of the countless poor creatures who have fallen into temptation through delusions in religion! And is it not time for us to wake up before it is too late, to watch against the first risings of sin, the first attempts of Satan, and all the ways by which he has made his approaches to us, however harmless they may be in themselves?

Do we not have experience of our weakness, our folly, and the invincible power of temptation once it has got within us? As for this duty I have dwelt on, take these considerations:

1. If you+ neglect it, since it is the only means prescribed by our Savior, you+ will certainly enter into temptation, and just as certainly fall into sin. Do not flatter yourselves+. Some of you+ are “old disciples” and have a great abhorrence of sin. You+ think it impossible that you+ should ever be seduced in this way or that. But “the one (whoever he is) who thinks he is standing firm should be careful not to fall.” It is not any grace received, not any experience gained, not any resolve made use of, that will preserve you+ from any evil, unless you+ stand on your+ watch. “What I say to you+,” says Christ, “I say to everyone: Keep watch!” Perhaps you+ have had some good success for a time in your+ careless frame. But wake up, and wonder at God’s tenderness and patience, or evil lies at the door. If you+ will not perform this duty, whoever you+ are, you+ will be tempted and defiled one way or another, in one thing or another, in spiritual or fleshly wickedness. And what will be the end of it? Remember Peter!

2. Consider that you+ are always under the eye of Christ, the great captain of our salvation, who has commanded us to watch in this way and to pray that we will not enter into temptation. What do you+ think are the thoughts and the heart of Christ when he sees a temptation hurrying toward us, a storm rising around us, and we are fast asleep? Does it not grieve him to see us expose ourselves to danger like this, after he has given us warning upon warning? While he was in the days of his flesh, he considered his temptation while it was still coming, and armed himself against it. “The prince of this world is coming,” he says, but “he has nothing in Me.” And shall we be negligent under his eye? Only think that you see him coming to you as he came to Peter when he was asleep in the garden, with the same rebuke: “Were you not able to keep watch for one hour?” Would it not grieve you to be rebuked like that, or to hear him thundering against your neglect from heaven, as he did against the church of Sardis (Rev 3:2)?

3. Consider this. If you neglect this duty, and so fall into temptation, as you certainly will, then when you are entangled God may also bring some heavy affliction or judgment on you. Because of your entanglement you will not be able to see it as anything other than evidence of his anger and hatred. And then what will you do with your temptation and your affliction together? All your bones will be broken, and your peace and strength will be gone in a moment. This may seem only a noise of words for now; but if it is ever your condition, you will find it full of woe and bitterness. Oh, then, let us strive to keep our spirits unentangled, avoiding every appearance of evil and all ways that lead to it; especially all ways, business, company, and employments that we have already found harmful to us.
