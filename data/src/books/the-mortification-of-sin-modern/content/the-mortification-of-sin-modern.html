Preface

Christian Reader,

I will tell you in a few words the reasons that won my consent to publish the discourse that follows. The chief place among them belongs to the present state and condition of most of those who profess the faith. The visible evidence of the state of their hearts and spirits shows a great inability to deal with the temptations that surround them, both from the peace they have in the world and from the divisions they have among themselves. I am sure this is of such great importance that if this book only moves others to press on men’s consciences more effectually the work of considering their ways, and to give clearer direction for reaching the end proposed, I will think well of my part in this undertaking. A second reason was the dangerous mistakes I have observed in some men who have lately taken it upon themselves to give directions for the mortification of sin. Being unacquainted with the mystery of the gospel and the efficacy of the death of Christ, they have once again laid the yoke of a self-made mortification on the necks of their disciples, a yoke which neither they nor their forefathers were ever able to bear. The mortification they cry up and press on others matches that of the gospel neither in its nature, its subject, its causes, its means, nor its effects. It constantly produces the sad results of superstition, self-righteousness, and anxiety of conscience in those who take up the burden that is so bound on them.

What is set out here in weakness will, I humbly hope, agree with the spirit and the letter of the gospel, and with the experience of those who know what it is to walk with God according to the terms of the covenant of grace. So if not this, then certainly something of this kind is very necessary at this time, to promote and further this work of gospel mortification in the hearts of believers, and to direct them in paths that are safe, where they may find rest for their souls. I have something to add about what concerns me in particular. I preached on this subject with some encouraging success, through the grace of Him who supplied seed to the sower. Several people in whose hearts are the ways of God then pressed me to publish what I had preached, with such additions and changes as I judged necessary. While their desires urged me on, I remembered a debt in which I have now stood for some years to several noble and worthy Christian friends: a treatise on Communion with God, promised to them some time ago. So I thought that if I could not pay off the greater debt with this,1 I might perhaps offer them this discourse of being at odds with themselves as interest for their patience over the one on peace and communion with God. Besides this, I considered that providence had engaged me in the public debate of several controversies in religion, and that this might seem to call for something of another, more generally useful kind, the fruit of choice and not of necessity. For these and similar reasons this short discourse is brought out to public view, and now presented to you. I hope I may honestly say that my heart’s desire to God, and the chief aim of my life in the place where the good providence of God has set me, is this: that mortification and holiness in all things may be promoted in my own heart and in the hearts and ways of others, to the glory of God, so that the gospel of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ may be adorned in all things. To that end, if this little discourse (and this is the whole account I will give of why it is published) may in any way be useful to the least of the saints, it will be regarded as an answer to the weak prayers with which it is sent out by its unworthy author,

J. OWEN.

Chapter I

The foundation of the whole discourse that follows laid in Rom 8:13. The words of the apostle explained. The certain connection between true mortification and salvation. Mortification the work of believers. The Spirit the principal efficient cause of it. What is meant by “the body” in the words of the apostle. What by “the deeds of the body.” Life, in what sense promised to this duty.

I want what I have to contribute by way of direction for carrying on the work of mortification in believers to have order and clarity. So I will lay its foundation in these words of the apostle, “if by the Spirit you+ put to death the deeds of the body, you+ will live” (Rom 8:13). And I will make the whole an application of the great gospel truth and mystery contained in them.

The apostle has summed up his doctrine of justification by faith, and the blessed state and condition of those who by grace share in it (verses 1, 2, 3 of this chapter). Now he goes on to apply it to the holiness and comfort of believers.

Among his arguments and motives for holiness, the verse I mentioned contains one taken from the opposite outcomes and effects of holiness and of sin: “If you+ live according to the flesh, you+ will die.” What it is to “live according to the flesh,” and what it is to “die,” is not my present aim and business. So I will explain them no further than they fit in with the sense of the later words of the verse, as proposed above.

In the words chosen especially as the foundation of the discourse that follows, there is:

1. A duty prescribed: “Put to death the deeds of the body.”

2. The persons to whom it is prescribed: “You+,” “if you+ put to death.”

3. A promise attached to that duty: “You+ will live.”

4. The cause or means by which this duty is performed, the Spirit: “If by the Spirit you+.”

5. The conditional form of the whole statement, in which the duty, the means, and the promise are contained: “If you+,” and so on.

The first thing we meet in the words, as they stand in the whole statement, is the conditional word, εἰ δὲ, “But if.” In statements like this, a condition may mean one of two things:

[1.] The uncertainty of the outcome, the thing promised, as far as those to whom the duty is prescribed are concerned. This is the case where the condition is absolutely necessary to the outcome, and does not itself depend on any fixed cause known to the person to whom it is prescribed. So we say, “If we live, we will do such a thing.” This cannot be what the conditional means in this place. Of the persons to whom these words are spoken, it is said in verse 1 of the same chapter that there is “no condemnation” for them.

[2.] The certainty of the link and connection between the things spoken of. So we say to a sick man, “If you take this medicine, or use this remedy, you will be well.” All we mean to express is the certainty of the connection between the medicine or remedy and health. And this is its use here. This conditional word points to the certain connection between putting to death the deeds of the body and living.

Now, things are connected in many ways: as cause and effect, or as the way and means to an end. The connection between mortification and life is not one of cause and effect, properly and strictly speaking, for “the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Rom 6:23). It is a connection of means and end. God has appointed this means for reaching that end, which he has freely promised. Means, though necessary, are rightly subordinate to an end that is freely promised. A gift, and a cause in the receiver that earns it, cannot stand together. So what this statement means, as a conditional, is this: there is a certain, unfailing connection between true mortification and eternal life. If you use this means, you will obtain that end; if you mortify, you will live. And here lies the main motive and force behind the duty prescribed.

(2.) The next thing we meet in the words is the persons to whom this duty is prescribed. They are expressed in the word “you+,” which in the original is included in the verb θανατοῦτε, “if you+ put to death.” That is, you+ believers; you+ for whom there is “no condemnation” (verse 1); you+ who are not in the flesh but in the Spirit (verse 9); who are given life by the Spirit of Christ (verses 10, 11). To you+ this duty is prescribed. To press this duty directly on anyone else is a clear fruit of the superstition and self-righteousness that the world is full of, the great work and aim of devout men who are ignorant of the gospel (Rom 10:3, 4; John 15:5). Now, this description of the persons, joined with the prescription of the duty, is the main foundation of the discourse that follows, as it stands in this thesis or proposition:

The choicest believers, who are certainly freed from the condemning power of sin, ought still to make it their business all their days to mortify the indwelling power of sin.

(3.) The principal efficient cause of performing this duty is the Spirit: εἰ δὲ πνεύματι, “If by the Spirit.” The Spirit here is the Spirit mentioned in verse 11: the Spirit of Christ, the Spirit of God, who “lives in” us (verse 9), who gives us life (verse 11); the Holy Spirit (verse 14); the “Spirit of sonship” (verse 15); the Spirit who “intercedes for us” (verse 26). All other ways of mortification are vain; all helps leave us helpless. It must be done by the Spirit. Men, as the apostle suggests (Rom 9:30, 31, 32), may attempt this work on other principles, by means and advantages offered for other reasons, as they always have done and still do. But, he says, this is the work of the Spirit; it is to be done by him alone, and by no other power can it be brought about. Mortification by one’s own strength, carried on by ways of one’s own invention, for the end of one’s own righteousness, is the soul and substance of all false religion in the world. And this is a second principle of the discourse that follows.

(4.) The duty itself, “Put to death the deeds of the body,” is to be considered next.

Three things are to be asked here:

  1. What is meant by the body.
  2. What by the deeds of the body.
  3. What by mortifying them.

[1.] The body at the end of the verse is the same as the flesh at the beginning: “If you+ live according to the flesh, you+ will die; but if … you+ put to death the deeds of the body,” that is, of the flesh. It is what the apostle has been speaking of all along under the name of the flesh. This is clear from the contrast between the Spirit and the flesh that he carries on before and after. The body, then, here means the corruption and depravity of our natures, of which the body is in large part the seat and instrument; by this corruption the very members of the body are made servants to unrighteousness (Rom 6:19). What is meant is indwelling sin, the corrupted flesh or lust. Many reasons might be given for this figure of speech, which I will not dwell on now. The “body” here is the same as the παλαιὸς ἄνθρωπος and the σῶμα τῆς ἁμαρτίας, the “old man” and the “body of sin” (Rom 6:6). Or it may stand, by naming a part for the whole, for the whole person considered as corrupted, the seat of lusts and disordered affections.

[2.] The deeds of the body. The word is πράξεις, which indeed means chiefly the outward actions, “the acts of the flesh,” as they are called, τὰ ἔργα τῆς σαρκὸς (Gal 5:19), which are there said to be “obvious,” and are listed. Now, though only the outward deeds are named here, the inward and immediate causes are chiefly meant. The “axe is to be laid to the root of the tree”: the deeds of the flesh are to be put to death in the causes from which they spring. The apostle calls them deeds because every lust tends toward them. Though it only conceives and miscarries, it aims to bring forth a full-grown sin.

In chapter 7 and at the beginning of this chapter he has dealt with indwelling lust and sin as the fountain and principle of all sinful actions. Here he speaks of its destruction under the name of the effects it produces. πράξεις τοῦ σώματος [the deeds of the body] amount to the same as φρόνημα τῆς σαρκὸς, the “wisdom of the flesh” (Rom 8:6), by the same kind of figure as before. Or they are like the παθήματα and ἐπιθυμίαι, the “passions and lusts of the flesh” (Gal 5:24), from which its deeds and fruits arise. And in this sense the body is used in verse 10: “your body is dead because of sin.”

[3.] To mortify. The Greek is εἰ θανατοῦτε, “if you+ put to death.” It is a figure taken from putting any living thing to death. To kill a man, or any living thing, is to take away the source of all its strength, vigor, and power, so that it can no longer act or do anything of its own. So it is here. Indwelling sin is pictured as a person, a living person, called “the old man,” with all his faculties and qualities: his wisdom, craft, cunning, and strength. This, says the apostle, must be killed, put to death, mortified. That is, the Spirit must take away its power, life, vigor, and strength to produce its effects. By the merit of Christ’s cross, and with his death as the pattern of ours, sin has indeed already been utterly put to death. So our “old man” is said to have been “crucified with Him” (Rom 6:6), and we ourselves to have “died with Christ” (verse 8). The work is also truly begun in us at regeneration (Rom 6:3, 4, 5), when a principle that opposes sin and works to destroy it (Gal 5:17) is planted in our hearts. But the whole work must be carried on by degrees toward perfection all our days. More will be said of this as we go on.

What the apostle means by this command, then, is this: it is the constant duty of believers to put to death the indwelling sin that remains in their mortal bodies, so that it has no life or power to bring forth the works or deeds of the flesh.

(5.) The promise attached to this duty is life: “You+ will live.” The life promised is set against the death threatened in the clause before, “If you+ live according to the flesh, you+ will die.” The same apostle puts it this way elsewhere: from the flesh you+ “will reap corruption” (Gal 6:8), or destruction from God. Now, perhaps the word means not only eternal life, but also the spiritual life in Christ that we have here. Not its essence and being, which believers already enjoy, but its joy, comfort, and vigor. As the apostle says in another case, “now I live, if you+ stand firm” (1 Thess 3:8); that is, “Now my life will do me good; I will have joy and comfort in my life.” So, “You+ will live: you+ will lead a good, vigorous, spiritual life, full of comfort, while you+ are here, and obtain eternal life hereafter.”

Taking for granted what was said before about the connection between mortification and eternal life, as of means and end, I will add only this, as a second motive to the duty prescribed:

The vigor, and power, and comfort of our spiritual life depend on the mortification of the deeds of the flesh.

Chapter II

The principal assertion about the necessity of mortification set out to be confirmed. Mortification the duty of the best believers, Col 3:5. 1 Cor 9:27. Indwelling sin always remains: no perfection in this life. Phil 3:12. 1 Cor 13:12. 2 Pet 3:18. Gal 5:17 etc. The activity of remaining sin in believers: Rom 7:23. Jas 4:5. Heb 12:1. Its fruitfulness and tendency. Every lust aims at the height of its kind. The Spirit and the new nature given to contend against indwelling sin. Gal 5:17. 2 Pet 1:4, 5. Rom 7:23. The fearful outcome of neglecting mortification. Rev 3:2. Heb 3:13. The first general principle of the whole discourse confirmed by this. The lack of this duty lamented.

Having laid this foundation, a brief confirmation of the main conclusions mentioned above will lead me to what I chiefly intend. The first is:

That the choicest believers, who are certainly freed from the condemning power of sin, ought still to make it their business all their days to mortify the indwelling power of sin.

So the apostle says, “Put to death, therefore, the components of your+ earthly nature” (Col 3:5). To whom is he speaking? To those who had been “raised with Christ” (verse 1); who had “died” with him (verse 3); whose life Christ was, and who would “appear with Him in glory” (verse 4). You+ must mortify. Make it your+ daily work. Be always at it while you+ live. Do not stop for a day. Be killing sin, or it will be killing you+. Your+ having died with Christ by virtue of his death, and been made alive with him, will not excuse you+ from this work. And our Savior tells us how his Father deals with every branch in him that bears fruit, every true and living branch: “He prunes to make it even more fruitful” (John 15:2). He prunes it, and not for a day or two, but as long as it is a branch in this world. And the apostle tells you+ what his own practice was: “I discipline my body and bring it into subjection” (1 Cor 9:27). I do it daily, he says; it is the work of my life; I never leave it off; this is my business. If this was the work and business of Paul, who was raised so incomparably far above the ordinary measure of believers in grace, light, revelations, experiences, privileges, and comforts, on what possible ground can we claim to be excused from this work and duty while we are in this world? A short account of the reasons for this may be given:

1. Indwelling sin always remains while we are in this world; so it must always be mortified. I will not deal now with the vain, foolish, and ignorant disputes of men about keeping the commands of God perfectly, about perfection in this life, and about being wholly and perfectly dead to sin. It is more than likely that the men who hold those abominations never knew what it is to keep any one of God’s commands. They are so far below perfection of degree that they have never reached perfection of parts in obedience, or sincere obedience in all things. That is why many in our days who have talked of perfection have been wiser, and have claimed that it consists in knowing no difference between good and evil. Not that they are perfect in the things we call good, but that everything is alike to them, and the height of wickedness is their perfection. Others have found a new way to it, by denying original, indwelling sin, and by bringing down the spiritual demands of the law of God to suit men’s fleshly hearts. They have shown clearly enough that they do not know the life of Christ and its power in believers, and they have also invented a new righteousness that the gospel knows nothing of, puffed up without reason by their fleshly minds. As for us, who dare not be wise beyond what is written, nor boast of work done in another man’s territory, of what God has not done for us, we say that indwelling sin lives in us, in some measure and degree, while we are in this world. We dare not speak as if we “have already obtained all this, or have already been made perfect” (Phil 3:12). Our “inner self is being renewed day by day” as long as we live here (2 Cor 4:16), and as the new is renewed, so the old suffers breaches and decays. While we are here we “know in part” (1 Cor 13:12). There is a darkness remaining in us, to be removed by degrees as we “grow in the … knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ” (2 Pet 3:18). And the flesh craves what is contrary to the Spirit, so that we cannot do what we want (Gal 5:17). So we fall short in our obedience as well as in our light (1 John 1:8). We have a “body of death” (Rom 7:24), from which we are not delivered except by the death of our bodies (Phil 3:21). Now, since it is our duty to mortify, to be killing sin while it is in us, we must be at work. The man appointed to kill an enemy, if he stops striking before the other stops living, does only half his work (Gal 6:9; Heb 12:1; 2 Cor 7:1).

2. Sin does not merely remain in us. It is always active, always working to bring forth the deeds of the flesh. If sin left us alone, we might leave sin alone. But sin is never less quiet than when it seems most quiet, and its waters are usually deepest where they are still. So our plans against it must be vigorous at all times and in every condition, even where we least suspect it. Sin not only remains in us; there is “another law at work in my members, warring against the law of my mind” (Rom 7:23), and “the spirit that dwells in us yearns with envy” (Jas 4:5). It is always at work. “The flesh craves what is contrary to the Spirit” (Gal 5:17). Lust is forever tempting and conceiving sin (Jas 1:14). In every moral act it is doing one of three things: inclining us to evil, holding us back from good, or unsettling our spirit for communion with God. It inclines us to evil: “I keep on doing the evil I do not want to do,” says the apostle (Rom 7:19). Why? “Nothing good lives in me, that is, in my flesh.” It holds us back from good: “I do not do the good I want to do” (verse 19). That is, for the same reason, either I do not do it, or I do not do it as I should; all my holy things are defiled by this sin. “They oppose each other, so that you+ cannot do what you+ want” (Gal 5:17). And it unsettles our spirit; that is why it is called “the sin that so easily entangles” (Heb 12:1), and why the apostle complains of it so bitterly in Romans 7. So sin is always acting, always conceiving, always seducing and tempting. Who can say he has ever had anything to do with God, or done anything for God, without indwelling sin having a hand in corrupting what he did? And it will keep up this trade, more or less, all our days. If sin is always acting, then unless we are always putting it to death, we are lost. The man who stands still and takes blow after blow without resisting will undoubtedly be conquered in the end. If sin is subtle, watchful, strong, and always at work killing our souls, while we are lazy, careless, and foolish about destroying it, can we expect things to end well? Not a day passes but sin either defeats us or is defeated, prevails or is prevailed against; and so it will be as long as we live in this world. I will release from this duty the man who can bring sin to terms, to a truce in this war. If sin will spare him for one day, in one single duty (provided he understands how spiritual obedience is and how subtle sin is), let him say to his soul, as far as this duty goes, “Soul, take your rest.” The saints, whose souls long to be delivered from sin’s harassing rebellion, know there is no safety against it except constant warfare.

3. Sin will not only be striving, acting, rebelling, troubling, and disturbing. If it is left alone, if it is not continually mortified, it will bring forth great, cursed, scandalous, soul-destroying sins. The apostle tells us what its works and fruits are: “The acts of the flesh are obvious: adultery, sexual immorality, impurity, and debauchery; idolatry and sorcery; hatred, discord, jealousy, and rage; rivalries, divisions, factions, and envy; murders, drunkenness, orgies, and the like” (Gal 5:19–21). You+ know what it did in David and several others. Sin always aims at the utmost. Every time it rises up to tempt or entice, if it could have its own way, it would go on to the utmost sin of that kind. Every unclean thought or glance would be adultery if it could. Every covetous desire would be oppression, and every thought of unbelief would be atheism, if it were allowed to grow to its full head. Men may reach the point where sin is not heard speaking a scandalous word in their hearts, that is, urging them to some great sin with scandal in its mouth. Yet every rising of lust, if it had its way, would come to the height of wickedness: it is like the grave, which is never satisfied. And here lies no small part of the deceitfulness of sin, by which it succeeds in hardening men, and so in ruining them (Heb 3:13). It is modest, as it were, in its first stirrings and suggestions. But once it has gained a footing in the heart by them, it holds its ground firmly and presses on to further degrees of the same kind. This new activity and pressing forward makes the soul take little notice of how far it has already gone in falling away from God. It thinks all is fairly well if there is no further progress. And so far as the soul is made insensible to any sin, that is, without the sense of it the gospel requires, so far it is hardened. But sin keeps pressing forward, because it has no limit short of utterly forsaking God and opposing him. That it moves toward its height by degrees, holding the ground it has gained by hardness, comes not from its nature but from its deceitfulness. Now nothing can prevent this but mortification. That withers the root and strikes at the head of sin every hour, so that whatever it aims at, it is thwarted. There is not the best saint in the world who, if he gave up this duty, would not fall into as many cursed sins as anyone of his kind ever did.

4. This is one main reason why the Spirit and the new nature are given to us: so that we may have a principle within us by which to oppose sin and lust. “The flesh craves what is contrary to the Spirit.” Well, and what then? Why, the Spirit also craves “what is contrary to the flesh” (Gal 5:17). There is an inclination in the Spirit, or the spiritual new nature, to act against the flesh, just as there is in the flesh to act against the Spirit. So also 2 Pet 1:4, 5: it is our sharing in the divine nature that lets us escape the corruption in the world caused by lust. And in Rom 7:23 there is a law of the mind as well as a law of the members. Now, when two fighters are engaged, it is, first, the most unjust and unreasonable thing in the world to tie one up and keep him from doing his utmost, and to leave the other free to wound him as he pleases. And, secondly, it is the most foolish thing in the world to tie up the one who fights for our eternal condition and to leave alone the one who seeks and violently attempts our everlasting ruin. The fight is for our lives and souls. Not to be daily using the Spirit and the new nature to mortify sin is to neglect the excellent help God has given us against our greatest enemy. If we neglect to make use of what we have received, God may justly hold back his hand from giving us more. His graces, as well as his gifts, are given to us to use, exercise, and trade with. Not to be daily mortifying sin is to sin against the goodness, kindness, wisdom, grace, and love of God, who has supplied us with a principle for doing it.

5. Neglect of this duty throws the soul into a condition exactly opposite to the one the apostle says was his: “Though our outer self is wasting away, yet our inner self is being renewed day by day” (2 Cor 4:16). In such men the inner self wastes away, and the outer self is renewed day by day. Sin is like the house of David, and grace like the house of Saul. Exercise and success are the two main things that nourish grace in the heart. When grace is allowed to lie still, it withers and decays; the things of it are “about to die” (Rev 3:2), and sin gains ground toward hardening the heart (Heb 3:13). This is what I mean: when this duty is left undone, grace withers, lust flourishes, and the frame of the heart grows worse and worse. And the Lord knows what desperate and fearful outcomes this has had with many. Where sin, through the neglect of mortification, wins a considerable victory, it breaks the bones of the soul (Ps 31:10; Ps 51:8), and makes a man weak, sick, and ready to die (Ps 38:3–5), so that he cannot look up (Ps 40:12; Isa 33:24). And when poor creatures will take blow after blow, wound after wound, defeat after defeat, and never rouse themselves to vigorous resistance, can they expect anything but to be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin, and for their souls to bleed to death (2 John 8)? Indeed, it is a sad thing to consider the fearful outcomes of this neglect, which lie before our eyes every day. Do we not see those whom we knew as humble, soft-hearted, broken-hearted Christians, tender and afraid to offend, zealous for God and all his ways, his Sabbaths and ordinances, now grown earthly, carnal, cold, wrathful, and conformed to the men of the world and the things of the world, through neglecting to watch over this duty? They are a scandal to religion and a fearful temptation to those who know them. The truth is this. Between placing mortification in a rigid, stubborn frame of spirit, which is for the most part earthly, legalistic, censorious, and partial, and consistent with wrath, envy, malice, and pride, on the one hand, and false claims of liberty, grace, and I know not what, on the other, true gospel mortification is almost lost among us. More of this later.

6. It is our duty to be “perfecting holiness in the fear of God” (2 Cor 7:1); to “grow in the grace” every day (1 Pet 2:2; 2 Pet 3:18); to have our “inner self … renewed day by day” (2 Cor 4:16). Now, this cannot be done without mortifying sin daily. Sin sets its strength against every act of holiness, and against every degree of it we grow to. Let no man think he is making any progress in holiness who does not walk over the bellies of his lusts. The man who does not kill sin in his way takes no steps toward his journey’s end. The man who meets no opposition from it, and who does not set himself to mortify it in every particular, is at peace with it, not dying to it.

This, then, is the first general principle of the discourse that follows. In the cross of Christ, if I may put it so, all sin, every sin, has been put to death by merit. A real foundation for the mortification of all sin has been laid in our first conversion, by conviction of sin, humbling for sin, and the planting of a new principle that opposes sin and works to destroy it. And yet, in spite of all this, sin still so remains, so acts and works in the best of believers while they live in this world, that the constant, daily mortification of it is their duty all their days. Before I go on to consider the next principle, I cannot help complaining in passing about many who profess the faith in these days. Instead of bringing forth the great and evident fruits of mortification that are expected, they hardly bear any leaves of it. There is indeed a broad light that has fallen on the men of this generation, and with it many spiritual gifts have been given. These, with some other things, have wonderfully widened the bounds of those who profess the faith and of the profession of faith itself; both have multiplied and increased greatly. So there is a noise of religion and religious duties in every corner, and preaching in abundance. And it is not empty, light, trivial, and vain, as it used to be, but has a good measure of spiritual gift. If you measure the number of believers by light, gifts, and profession of faith, the church may well say, “Who has begotten these for me?” But if you measure them by this great distinguishing grace of Christians, perhaps you will find their number not so multiplied. Hardly one can be found of those who owe their conversion to these days of light, who talk and profess at a level of spirituality that few in former days knew anything of (I will not judge them, but perhaps they are boasting of what the Lord has done in them), who does not give evidence of a miserably unmortified heart. If wasting time, idleness, unprofitableness in men’s callings, envy, strife, discord, rivalry, wrath, pride, worldliness, and selfishness (1 Cor 1) are the badges of Christians, we have them on us and among us in abundance. And if it is so with those who have much light, and light that we hope is saving, what shall we say of some who want to be counted religious, and yet despise gospel light? As for the duty we have in hand, they know nothing more of it than men denying themselves at times in outward comforts, which is one of its outermost branches, and which even so they seldom practice. May the good Lord send out a spirit of mortification to cure our disorders, or we are in a sad condition!

There are two evils that certainly attend every unmortified professing Christian: the first in himself, the second in respect of others.

1. In himself. Let him claim what he will, he has light thoughts of sin, at least of sins of daily weakness. The root of an unmortified life is swallowing sin without bitterness in the heart. When a man has trained his imagination to think of grace and mercy in such a way that he can swallow and digest daily sins without bitterness, that man is on the very brink of turning the grace of God into a license for sin, and of being hardened by the deceitfulness of sin. Nor is there any greater evidence in the world of a false and rotten heart than to carry on such a trade. To use the blood of Christ, which is given to cleanse us (1 John 1:7; Titus 2:14); the exaltation of Christ, which is to give us repentance (Acts 5:31); and the doctrine of grace, which teaches us to renounce all ungodliness (Titus 2:11, 12), to favor sin, is a rebellion that in the end will break the bones. Through this door most of the professing Christians who have fallen away in our days have gone out from us. For a while most of them were under conviction. This kept them to their duties and brought them to profess the faith, so that they “escaped the corruption of the world through the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ” (2 Pet 2:20). But having become acquainted with the doctrine of the gospel, and being weary of duty, for which they had no principle within, they began to allow themselves many neglects on the strength of the doctrine of grace. Now, once this evil had taken hold of them, they quickly tumbled into perdition.

2. To others. It has an evil influence on them in two ways:

(1.) It hardens them, by making them believe that they are in as good a condition as the best professing Christians. Whatever they see in such men is so stained by the lack of this mortification that it is worth nothing to them. These men have a zeal for religion, but it goes with a lack of patience and of righteousness in all things. They give up extravagance, but are worldly. They separate from the world, but live wholly for themselves, taking no care to show loving-kindness on the earth. Or they talk spiritually and live vainly; speak of communion with God and are in every way conformed to the world; boast of the forgiveness of sin and never forgive others. And with such thoughts poor creatures harden their hearts in their unregenerate state.

(2.) They deceive them, by making them believe that if they can come up to the condition of such men, all will be well with them. So it becomes an easy thing to have the great temptation of a reputation in religion to wrestle with, when they may go far beyond such men in what appears outwardly, and yet fall short of eternal life. But of these things, and of all the evils of an unmortified walk, more later.

Chapter III

The second general principle of the means of mortification set out to be confirmed. The Spirit the only author of this work. The emptiness of popish mortification exposed. Many of the means they use not appointed by God. Those appointed by him abused. The mistakes of others in this business. The Spirit is promised to believers for this work. Ezek 11:19; 36:26. All that we receive from Christ is by the Spirit. How the Spirit mortifies sin, Gal 5:19, 20, 21, 22, 23. The various ways he works to this end set out. How it is both his work and our duty.

The next principle concerns the great sovereign cause of the mortification we are dealing with. In the words laid down as the foundation of this discourse, it is said to be the Spirit, that is, the Holy Spirit, as was shown.

He alone is sufficient for this work. All ways and means without him are worth nothing, and he is the great efficient cause of it: he works in us as he pleases.

1. Men seek other remedies in vain; they will not be healed by them. It is well known how many ways have been prescribed for this, to have sin mortified. The greatest part of popish religion, of what looks most like religion in what they profess, consists of mistaken ways and means of mortification. This is the pretense of their rough garments, by which they deceive. Their vows, orders, fastings, and penances are all built on this ground; they are all for the mortifying of sin. Their preaching, sermons, and books of devotion all look this way. So those who interpret the locusts that came out of the bottomless pit (Rev 9:3) as the friars of the Roman church (these locusts are said to torment men, so that “men will seek death and will not find it,” verse 6) think the friars did it by their stinging sermons. By these they convicted men of sin, but being unable to show them the remedy for healing and mortifying it, they kept them in such constant anguish and terror, and such trouble of conscience, that they longed to die. This, I say, is the substance and glory of their religion. But they labor to mortify dead creatures, ignorant of the nature and end of the work, and they mix poison into it by their belief that it earns merit, yes, supererogation (as they call their unnecessary merit, with a proud, outlandish title). So their glory is their shame. But more of them and their mortification later, in ch. 7.

It is well known that the ways and means for mortifying sin that they invented are still urged and prescribed, for the same end, by some who should have more light and knowledge of the gospel. Directions of this kind have lately been given by some, and eagerly snatched up by others who profess themselves Protestants, directions that would have suited popish devotees three or four hundred years ago. Such outward efforts, such bodily exercises, such self-performances, such merely legalistic duties, without the least mention of Christ or his Spirit, are varnished over with inflated, empty words as the only means and expedients for the mortification of sin. They reveal a deep-rooted unfamiliarity with the power of God and the mystery of the gospel. The thought of this was one motive for publishing this plain discourse.

Now, among other reasons, these are why the Papists, for all their efforts, can never truly mortify a single sin:

(1.) Many of the ways and means they use and insist on for this end were never appointed by God for that purpose. (Now, nothing in religion has any efficacy to bring about an end except from God’s appointing it to that purpose.) Such are their rough garments, their vows, penances, scourgings, their course of monastic life, and the like. Of all these God will say, “Who has required this of you+?” and, “They worship Me in vain; they teach as doctrine the precepts of men.” Of the same nature are various self-inflicted hardships urged by others.

(2.) The things that God has appointed as means are not used by them in their proper place and order: such as prayer, fasting, keeping vigil, meditation, and the like. These have their use in the business at hand. But while they are all to be regarded as streams, they regard them as the fountain. These means bring about the end only as means, under the Spirit and faith; but they think these do it by virtue of the work done. If they fast so much, and pray so much, and keep their hours and times, the work is done. As the apostle says of some in another case, they are “always learning but never able to come to a knowledge of the truth”; so these are always mortifying, but never come to any sound mortification. In a word, they have various means to mortify the natural man, as to the natural life we lead here, but none to mortify lust or corruption.

This is the general mistake of men ignorant of the gospel about this matter, and it lies at the bottom of a great deal of the superstition and will-worship that has been brought into the world. What horrible ways of wasting their own bodies some of the ancient founders of monastic devotion practiced! What violence they did to nature! What extremes of suffering they put themselves through! Search their ways and principles to the bottom, and you will find that it had no other root than this mistake: attempting rigid mortification, they fell upon the natural man instead of the corrupt old man, upon the body in which we live instead of the body of death.

Nor will the natural popery that is in others do it. Men are stung by the guilt of a sin that has prevailed over them. At once they promise themselves and God that they will do it no more. They watch over themselves and pray for a while, until this heat grows cold and the sense of sin wears off. Then mortification goes too, and sin returns to its former rule. Duties are excellent food for a healthy soul; they are no medicine for a sick soul. He who turns his food into his medicine must not expect it to do much. Spiritually sick men cannot sweat out their disease by working. But this is the way of men who deceive their own souls, as we shall see later.

That none of these ways is sufficient is clear from the nature of the work itself that has to be done. It is a work that requires so many things working together in it that no self-effort can reach it, and it is of such a kind that almighty power is needed to accomplish it, as will be shown later.

It is, then, the work of the Spirit. For:

(1.) He is promised by God to be given to us to do this work. The taking away of the heart of stone, that is, the stubborn, proud, rebellious, unbelieving heart, is in general the work of mortification we are dealing with. Now this is always promised to be done by the Spirit (Ezek 11:19; 36:26): “I will put My Spirit within you+ … I will remove your+ heart of stone.” And it is by the Spirit of God that this work is done when all means fail (Isa 57:17, 18).

(2.) We have all our mortification from the gift of Christ, and all the gifts of Christ are imparted and given to us by the Spirit of Christ: “Without Christ we can do nothing” (John 15:5). All supplies and relief, in the beginning, growth, and exercise of any grace whatever, come from him by the Spirit, by whom alone he works in and on believers. From him we have our mortification. He is “exalted … as Prince and Savior, in order to grant repentance” to us (Acts 5:31), and our mortification is no small part of our repentance. How does he do it? Having “received from the Father the promised Holy Spirit,” he sends him out for that end (Acts 2:33). You+ know the many promises he made of sending the Spirit, “vicariam navare operam,” as Tertullian puts it, to do the work he had to accomplish in us.

Answering one or two questions will now bring me closer to what I chiefly intend.

The first is:

Q. How does the Spirit mortify sin?

I answer, in general, in three ways.

A. 1. By causing our hearts to abound in grace and the fruits that are contrary to the flesh, to its fruits, and to their principles. So the apostle sets the fruits of the flesh against the fruits of the Spirit. The fruits of the flesh, he says, are such and such (Gal 5:19, 20); but the fruits of the Spirit, he says, are quite contrary, of an entirely different kind (verses 22, 23). Yes, but if these are in us and abound, may not the others abound too? No, he says (verse 24): “Those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires.” But how? Why, by “living in the Spirit and walking by the Spirit” (verse 25); that is, by these graces of the Spirit abounding in us, and by our walking according to them. For, says the apostle, “they are opposed to each other” (verse 17), so that they cannot both be in the same person in any intense or high degree. This “renewal by the Holy Spirit,” as it is called (Titus 3:5), is one great way of mortification. He causes us to grow, thrive, flourish, and abound in those graces which are contrary, opposite, and destructive to all the fruits of the flesh, and to the quiet or thriving of indwelling sin itself.

[2.] By a real and direct working of his own power on the root and settled habit of sin, to weaken, destroy, and take it away. That is why he is called “a Spirit of judgment and a Spirit of fire” (Isa 4:4), really consuming and destroying our lusts. He takes away the heart of stone by almighty power; for as he begins the work in its kind, so he carries it on in its degrees. He is the fire that burns up the very root of lust.

[3.] He brings the cross of Christ into the heart of a sinner by faith, and gives us communion with Christ in his death and fellowship in his sufferings. Of how he does this, more later.

Q. If this is the work of the Spirit alone, why are we exhorted to it? Since only the Spirit of God can do it, let the work be left wholly to him.

A. 1. It is the work of the Spirit in no other way than all the graces and good works in us are his. He “works in you+ to will and to act on behalf of His good purpose” (Phil 2:13). He works “all our works in us” (Isa 26:12), and he will “powerfully fulfill” the “work of faith” (2 Thess 1:11; Col 2:12). He causes us to pray, and is “a Spirit of grace and prayer” (Rom 8:26; Zech 12:10). And yet we are exhorted, and are to be exhorted, to all these.

[2.] He does not work our mortification in us in such a way that it ceases to be an act of our obedience. The Holy Spirit works in us and on us as we are fit to be worked in and on; that is, so as to preserve our own liberty and free obedience. He works on our understandings, wills, consciences, and affections in keeping with their own natures. He works in us and with us, not against us or without us. So his help is an encouragement, making the work easier, and no excuse for neglecting the work itself. And indeed I might here lament the endless, foolish labor of poor souls who, convicted of sin and unable to stand against the power of their convictions, set themselves by countless harassing ways and duties to keep sin down; but being strangers to the Spirit of God, they do it all in vain. They fight without victory, have war without peace, and are in slavery all their days. They spend their strength on what is not bread, and their labor on what does not satisfy.

This is the saddest warfare that any poor creature can be engaged in. A soul under the power of conviction from the law is pressed to fight against sin, but has no strength for the fight. They cannot help fighting, and they can never conquer. They are like men thrust onto the swords of their enemies on purpose to be slain. The law drives them on, and sin beats them back. Sometimes, indeed, they think they have defeated sin, when they have only raised a dust so that they cannot see it. That is, they stir up their natural feelings of fear, sorrow, and anguish, and this makes them believe that sin is conquered when it has not been touched. By the time they have cooled, they must go back to the battle; and the lust they thought was slain turns out to have had no wound.

And if things are so sad with those who labor and strive and yet do not enter the kingdom, what is the condition of those who despise all this? They are continually under the power and rule of sin, and love to have it so, and are troubled at nothing except that they cannot make enough provision for the flesh, to fulfill its lusts.

Chapter IV

The last principle: the usefulness of mortification. The vigor and comfort of our spiritual life depend on our mortification. In what sense. Not absolutely and necessarily. Ps 88. Heman’s condition. Not as the next and immediate cause. As a means: by removing what is contrary. The desperate effects of unmortified lust: it weakens the soul; Ps 38:3, 8. in various ways: and darkens it. All graces improved by the mortification of sin. The best evidence of sincerity.

The last principle I will dwell on (passing over, first, the necessity of mortification for life, and, second, the certainty of life upon mortification) is this:

That the life, vigor, and comfort of our spiritual life depend greatly on our mortification of sin.

Strength and comfort, power and peace, in our walk with God, are the things we desire. If any of us were seriously asked what it is that troubles us, we would have to put it under one of these heads: either we lack strength or power, vigor and life, in our obedience, in our walk with God; or we lack peace, comfort, and consolation in it. Whatever may happen to a believer that does not belong under one of these two heads does not deserve to be mentioned in the days of our complaints. Now, all these depend greatly on a constant course of mortification. Concerning this, observe:

1. I do not say they come from it as though they were necessarily tied to it. A man may be kept in a constant course of mortification all his days, and yet perhaps never enjoy one good day of peace and consolation. So it was with Heman (Ps 88). His life was a life of constant mortification and walking with God, yet terrors and wounds were his portion all his days. But God singled out Heman, a choice friend, to make him an example to those who would later be in distress. Can you complain if it is no different with you than it was with Heman, that eminent servant of God? And this will be his praise to the end of the world. God makes it his prerogative to speak peace and consolation (Isa 57:18, 19). “I will do that work,” says God; “I will restore comfort to him” (verse 18). But how? By a direct work of the new creation: “I create it,” says God. The use of means to obtain peace is ours; the giving of it is God’s prerogative.

2. In the ways God has instituted to give us life, vigor, courage, and consolation, mortification is not one of the immediate causes. It is the privileges of our adoption, made known to our souls, that give us these things directly. The Spirit’s testifying “with our spirit that we are God’s children,” his giving us a new name and a white stone, adoption and justification (that is, as we sense and know them): these are the immediate causes, in the hand of the Spirit, of these things. But this I do say:

3. In our ordinary walk with God, and in the ordinary course of his dealing with us, the vigor and comfort of our spiritual lives depend greatly on our mortification. It is not only a “causa sine qua non” [a cause without which the thing does not come about], but something that has an effectual influence on them. For:

As a Means, by Removing the Contrary

1. First, this alone keeps sin from robbing us of both the one and the other. Every unmortified sin will certainly do two things:

(1.) It will weaken the soul and rob it of its vigor.

(2.) It will darken the soul and rob it of its comfort and peace.

(1.) It weakens the soul and robs it of its strength. When David had for a while harbored an unmortified lust in his heart, it broke all his bones and left him no spiritual strength. So he complained that he was sick, weak, wounded, faint. “There is no soundness in me,” he says (Ps 38:3); “I am feeble and badly crushed” (verse 8); yes, “I cannot look up” (Ps 40:12). An unmortified lust will drink up the spirit and all the vigor of the soul, and weaken it for all duties. For:

[1.] It disorders and unsettles the heart itself, by entangling its affections. It turns the heart away from the spiritual frame that is required for vigorous communion with God. It lays hold on the affections and makes its object loved and desirable, and so drives out the love of the Father (1 John 2:15; 3:17). The soul then cannot say honestly and truly to God, “You are my portion,” for it has something else that it loves. Fear, desire, and hope, the choice affections of the soul, which should be full of God, will in one way or another be entangled with it.

[2.] It fills the thoughts with schemes about it. Thoughts are the great suppliers of the soul, bringing in provision to satisfy its affections. If sin remains unmortified in the heart, they must again and again be making provision for the flesh, to fulfill its lusts. They must polish, adorn, and dress up the objects of the flesh, and bring them home to give satisfaction. And in the service of a defiled imagination they are able to do this beyond all expression.

[3.] It breaks out and actually hinders duty. The ambitious man must be scheming, the worldly man must be working or planning, and the sensual, vain person must be providing himself with empty pleasures, when they should be engaged in the worship of God.

If it were my present business to set out the breaches, ruin, weakness, and desolation that one unmortified lust will bring on a soul, this discourse would have to be extended far beyond what I intend.

(2) As sin weakens, so it darkens the soul. It is a cloud, a thick cloud, that spreads itself over the face of the soul and cuts off all the beams of God’s love and favor. It takes away all sense of the privilege of our adoption; and if the soul begins to gather up thoughts of consolation, sin quickly scatters them. Of this, more later.

Now, in this respect the vigor and power of our spiritual life depend on our mortification: it is the only means of removing what will allow us neither the one nor the other. Men who are sick and wounded under the power of lust try many ways of getting help. They cry to God when their troubled thoughts overwhelm them; even to God they cry, but are not delivered. In vain they try many remedies; “there is no healing.” So in Hos 5:13, “Ephraim saw his sickness and Judah his wound,” and they tried various remedies. Nothing will do until they come (verse 15) to “admit their guilt.” Men may see their sickness and wounds; but if they do not apply the right remedies, they will not be cured.

2. Secondly, mortification prunes all the graces of God and makes room for them to grow in our hearts. The life and vigor of our spiritual lives consist in the vigor and flourishing of the plants of grace in our hearts. Now, you may see this in a garden. Let a precious herb be planted, and let the ground be left untilled and weeds grow around it. Perhaps it will still live, but it will be a poor, withering, useless thing. You must look and search for it, and sometimes can hardly find it; and when you do, you can hardly tell whether it is the plant you are looking for or not; and suppose it is, you can make no use of it at all. But let another of the same kind be set in the ground, naturally as barren and poor as the other, and let it be well weeded and everything harmful and hurtful removed from it: it flourishes and thrives. You can see it at your first look into the garden, and have it for your use whenever you please. So it is with the graces of the Spirit that are planted in our hearts. It is true, they are still there; they remain in a heart where mortification is somewhat neglected. But they are “about to die” (Rev 3:2); they are withering and decaying. The heart is like the sluggard’s field, so overgrown with weeds that you can hardly see the good grain. Such a man may search for faith, love, and zeal, and hardly be able to find any. And if he does find that these graces are still there, alive and sincere, yet they are so weak, so clogged with lusts, that they are of very little use. They remain, indeed, but are ready to die. But now let the heart be cleansed by mortification. Let the weeds of lust be constantly and daily rooted up (as they spring up daily, nature being their proper soil). Let room be made for grace to thrive and flourish. How every grace will then play its part, and be ready for every use and purpose!

3. Thirdly, as to our peace: just as nothing has any evidence of sincerity without it, so I know of nothing that has such evidence of sincerity in it, and that is no small foundation of our peace. Mortification is the soul’s vigorous opposition to self, and in it sincerity is most evident.

Chapter V

The main aim of the whole discourse set out. The first main case of conscience stated. What it is to mortify any sin, considered negatively. Not the utter destruction of it in this life. Not disguising it. Not the improvement of any natural principle. Not diverting it. Not an occasional conquest. Occasional conquests of sin, what they are and when they come. When sin breaks out, in a time of danger or trouble.

With these things laid down first, I come to my main purpose: to handle some questions or practical cases that arise in this business of the mortification of sin in believers. The first, which is the head of all the rest, and to which they all come back, may be considered as set out in the following proposition:

Suppose a man is a true believer, and yet finds in himself a powerful indwelling sin. It leads him captive to its law, consumes his heart with trouble, perplexes his thoughts, weakens his soul for duties of communion with God, and disquiets him as to peace. Perhaps it also defiles his conscience and exposes him to being hardened by the deceitfulness of sin. What shall he do? What course shall he take and hold to in order to mortify this sin, lust, disorder, or corruption, to such a degree that, though it is not utterly destroyed, yet in his contest with it he may be enabled to keep up power, strength, and peace in communion with God?

In answer to this important question, I will do these things:

1. Show what it is to mortify any sin, both negatively and positively, so that we are not mistaken in the foundation.

2. Give general directions about the things without which it will be utterly impossible for anyone to get any sin truly and spiritually mortified.

3. Draw out the particular ways in which this is to be done. Throughout, I keep in mind that I am treating not the doctrine of mortification in general, but only the particular case proposed above.

I. What It Is to Mortify a Sin

1. Negatively: What It Is Not

1. To mortify a sin is not to kill it outright, to root it out and destroy it so that it has no hold or home in our hearts at all. That is indeed the aim, but it is not to be accomplished in this life. No man who truly sets himself to mortify a sin aims at anything less than its utter destruction, so that it leaves neither root nor fruit in the heart or the life. He would kill it so completely that it would never move or stir again, never cry or call, seduce or tempt, for all eternity. Its not being at all is what he aims at. By the Spirit and grace of Christ a man may indeed win a wonderful success and an outstanding victory over a sin, so that he triumphs over it almost constantly. Yet its utter killing and destruction, so that it no longer exists, is not to be expected in this life. Paul assures us of this: “Not that I have already obtained all this, or have already been made perfect” (Phil 3:12). He was a choice saint, a pattern for believers. In faith and love and all the fruit of the Spirit he had no equal in the world, and so he can count himself among the “perfect” compared with others (verse 15). Yet he had not “obtained,” he was not “perfect,” but was pressing on. He still had a lowly body, as we do, which must be transformed at last by the great power of Christ (verse 21). This is what we would like. But God sees that it is best for us to be complete in nothing in ourselves, so that in all things we might be complete in Christ (Col 2:10). And that is best for us.

(2.) I think I hardly need to say that it is not the disguising of a sin. When a man gives up the practice of a sin for some outward reasons, men may perhaps look on him as a changed man. But God knows that to his former iniquity he has added cursed hypocrisy, and has found a safer road to hell than he was on before. He has gotten a different heart than he had, one that is more cunning; not a new heart, one that is more holy.

(3.) The mortification of sin does not consist in the improvement of a quiet, calm nature. Some men have an advantage from their natural constitution, in that they are not exposed to such violence of unruly passions and turbulent affections as many others are. Let these men cultivate and improve their natural frame and disposition by discipline, reflection, and prudence, and they may seem to themselves and others very mortified men, when perhaps their hearts are a stagnant cesspool of all abominations. One man is perhaps never troubled so much in all his life with anger and passion, nor troubles others so much, as another is almost every day; and yet the latter has done more to mortify the sin than the former. Let such persons not test their mortification by things to which their natural disposition gives no life or vigor. Let them bring themselves to self-denial, unbelief, envy, or some such spiritual sin, and they will get a better view of themselves.

(4.) A sin is not mortified when it is only diverted. Simon Magus gave up his sorceries for a while, but the greed and ambition that drove him to them remained, and would have found another way to act. So Peter tells him, “I see that you are poisoned by bitterness” (Acts 8:23). In other words: “For all the profession of faith you have made, for all that you have given up your sorceries, your lust is as powerful in you as ever. It is the same lust; only its streams have been turned aside. It now shows itself another way, but it is the same old poison of bitterness.” A man may be aware of a lust and set himself against its outbreaks. He may take care that it does not break out as it used to, while all the time he lets the same corrupt habit vent itself some other way. He is like a man who heals over a running sore and thinks himself cured, while his flesh festers with the same infection and breaks out in another place. This kind of diversion, with the changes that come with it, often happens to men for reasons that have nothing to do with grace. A change in a man’s way of life, his relationships, his interests, or his plans may bring it about. Even the changes that come over a man’s body naturally as he grows older may produce such changes. Old men do not usually go on chasing youthful lusts, though they have never put one of them to death. It is the same with trading one lust for another, leaving the service of one to serve another. A man who exchanges pride for worldliness, sensuality for Pharisaism, or vanity about himself for contempt of others, should not think he has mortified the sin he seems to have left. He has changed his master, but he is a servant still.

(5.) Occasional conquests of sin do not amount to mortifying it.

There are two occasions or seasons in which a man who is contending with a sin may seem to himself to have mortified it.

(1.) When it has had some sad outbreak, disturbing his peace, terrifying his conscience, making him dread scandal, and plainly provoking God. This awakens and stirs up all that is in the man. It dismays him and fills him with abhorrence of sin, and of himself for it. It sends him to God, and makes him cry out as for his life, abhor his lust as hell, and set himself against it. The whole man, spiritual and natural, being now awake, sin pulls in its head and does not appear, but lies as if dead before him. It is like a man who has crept up to an army in the night and killed a leading officer. At once the guards wake, men are roused, and a strict search is made for the enemy. In the meantime, until the noise and tumult are over, he hides himself or lies like one who is dead, yet firmly resolved to do the same mischief again at a similar opportunity. See how the Corinthians, over the sin among them, mustered themselves to surprise and destroy it (2 Cor 7:11). So it is in a person when his lust has made a breach in his conscience, his quiet, perhaps his reputation, by some outbreak of actual sin. Earnestness, indignation, longing, alarm, and revenge are all set to work about it and against it, and lust is quiet for a season, run down before them. But when the hurry is over and the search is past, the thief appears again alive and is as busy as ever at his work.

(2.) In a time of some judgment, calamity, or pressing affliction. The heart is then taken up with thoughts and plans for escaping the present troubles, fears, and dangers. This, a person under conviction concludes, can be done only by giving up sin, which brings peace with God. It is the anger of God in every affliction that stings a person under conviction. To be free of it, men at such times resolve against their sins. Sin shall never again have any place in them; they will never again give themselves up to its service. Accordingly sin is quiet, does not stir, and seems to be mortified. This is not because it has received a single wound, but only because the soul has filled its faculties, through which sin should exert itself, with thoughts that do not agree with sin’s movements; and when these thoughts are laid aside, sin returns to its former life and vigor. So those in Ps 78:32–38 are a full example and description of this frame of spirit I speak of:

“In spite of all this, they kept on sinning; despite His wonderful works, they did not believe.”

“So He ended their days in futility, and their years in sudden terror.”

“When He slew them, they would seek Him; they returned and searched early for God.”

“And they remembered that God was their Rock, that God Most High was their Redeemer.”

“But they deceived Him with their mouths, and lied to Him with their tongues.”

“Their hearts were disloyal to Him, and they were unfaithful to His covenant.”

I do not doubt at all that when they sought, and returned, and searched early for God, they did it with full purpose of heart to give up their sins. It is expressed in the word “returned”: to turn or return to the Lord is to give up sin. They did this “early,” that is, with earnestness and diligence. Yet for all this their sin was unmortified (verses 36, 37). And this is the state of many humblings in days of affliction, and a great deceit in the hearts of believers themselves often lies here.

In these and many other ways poor souls deceive themselves and suppose they have mortified their lusts, when the lusts are alive and mighty, and break out on every occasion, to their disturbance and unrest.

2. Positively: What It Is

What it is to mortify a sin in general must be considered next. This will prepare the way further for particular directions.

The mortification of a lust consists of three things.

1. It is an habitual weakening of the lust. Every lust is a depraved habit or disposition that continually inclines the heart to evil. That is why the man who has no lust truly mortified is described this way: “every inclination of the thoughts of his heart was altogether evil all the time” (Gen 6:5). He is always under the power of a strong bent and inclination to sin. A natural man is not perpetually chasing some one lust night and day, and the reason is that he has many lusts to serve, every one crying out to be satisfied. So he is carried along in great variety, but in general he always leans toward satisfying self.

We will suppose, then, that the lust or disorder whose mortification we are asking about is in itself a strong, deeply rooted, habitual inclination and bent of the will and affections toward some act that is sinful in its matter, though not desired formally as sin; and it is always stirring up imaginations, thoughts, and schemes about its object. Hence men are said to have their hearts set on evil; the bent of their spirits lies toward it, to make “provision for the flesh” (Rom 13:14). And a sinful, depraved habit differs here, as in many other things, from all natural or moral habits whatever. Those incline the soul gently and in a way that suits it, but sinful habits drive it with violence and force.2 That is why lusts are said to fight or wage “war against your soul” (1 Pet 2:11); to rebel or rise up in war, with the strategy and opposition usual in war (Rom 7:23); and to lead captive, that is, effectually to take prisoners after winning the battle. All these are works of great violence and force.

I could show fully, from the description of it in Romans 7, how it darkens the mind, extinguishes convictions, dethrones reason, interrupts the power and influence of any considerations brought to restrain it, and breaks through everything into a flame. But that is not my business now. The first thing in mortification, then, is the weakening of this habit of sin or lust, so that it does not rise up, conceive, stir up tumult, provoke, entice, and disquiet with the violence, earnestness, and frequency it naturally tends to (Jas 1:14, 15).

I want to give one caution or rule by the way. It is this: every lust, by its own nature, inclines and drives to sin equally and universally; yet this must be granted with two limitations:

(1.) One lust, or a lust in one man, may receive many incidental boosts, heightenings, and reinforcements. These may give it life, power, and vigor far beyond what another lust has, or what the same lust (that is, of the same kind and nature) has in another man. A lust may fall in with a man’s natural constitution and temperament, with a suitable way of life, or with opportunities; or Satan may have got a fit handle on it to manage it, as he has a thousand ways of doing. Then that lust grows violent and forceful above others, or more than the same lust in another man. Then its fumes so darken the mind that, though a man knows the same things as before, they have no power or influence on his will; instead, corrupt affections and passions are set free by it.

But above all, lust gets strength by temptation. When a suitable temptation falls in with a lust, it gives it new life, vigor, power, violence, and rage, which it did not seem to have before or to be capable of. Examples of this could be multiplied; but proving this observation is the aim of part of another treatise.

(2.) Some lusts are far more felt and noticeable in their violent workings than others. Paul puts a difference between sexual impurity and all other sins: “Flee from sexual immorality. Every other sin a man can commit is outside his body, but he who sins sexually sins against his own body” (1 Cor 6:18). So the stirrings of that sin are more felt and more noticeable than those of others. Yet perhaps the love of the world, or something like it, is no less habitually dominant in a person than that sin; it just does not cause so great a commotion in the whole man.

And for this reason some men may pass, in their own thoughts and in the eyes of the world, for mortified men. Yet lust is no less dominant in them than in those who cry out in dismay at its troubling turmoil, yes, than in those whom its power has hurried into scandalous sins. The only difference is that their lusts are in and about things that do not raise such a tumult in the soul. They are occupied with them in a calmer frame of spirit, because the very fabric of their nature is not so closely concerned in them as in some others.

I say, then, that the first thing in mortification is the weakening of this habit, so that it does not drive and stir up tumult as before, does not entice and draw aside, does not disquiet and perplex. It is the killing of its life, vigor, promptness, and readiness to be stirring. This is called having “crucified the flesh with its passions and desires” (Gal 5:24): that is, taking away its blood and spirits, which give it strength and power. It is the wasting away of the body of death “day by day” (2 Cor 4:16).

Think of a man nailed to the cross. At first he struggles and strives and cries out with great strength and might. But as his blood and spirits drain away, his strivings grow faint and rare, his cries low and hoarse, scarcely to be heard. So it is when a man first sets on a lust or disorder to deal with it. It struggles with great violence to break loose; it cries out with earnestness and impatience to be satisfied and relieved. But when mortification lets out its blood and spirits, it moves rarely and tamely, cries out seldom, and is scarcely heard in the heart. Sometimes it may have a dying pang that gives an appearance of great vigor and strength, but this is quickly over, especially if it is kept from any considerable success. The apostle describes this throughout the whole chapter, and especially in Rom 6:6.

Sin, he says, is “crucified”; it is fastened to the cross. To what end? “So that the body of sin might be rendered powerless”: the power of sin weakened and abolished little by little, “that we should no longer be slaves to sin”; that is, so that sin might not incline and drive us with such force as to make us its slaves, as it has done before. And this is said not only of carnal and sensual affections, or desires for worldly things (the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life), but also of the flesh as it is in the mind and will, in that opposition to God which is in us by nature. Whatever the nature of the troubling disorder, whatever ways it shows itself, whether by driving to evil or by hindering from good, the rule is the same. Unless this is done effectually, all later struggling will not reach the end aimed at. A man may beat down the bitter fruit from an evil tree until he is weary; while the root remains in strength and vigor, beating down the present fruit will not stop it from bringing forth more. This is the folly of some men. They set themselves with all earnestness and diligence against the visible outbreaks of lust, but they leave the principle and root untouched, perhaps not even searched out, and so they make little or no progress in this work of mortification.

2. Mortification consists in constant fighting and contending against sin. To be able always to be laying heavy blows on sin is no small degree of mortification. When sin is strong and vigorous, the soul is scarcely able to make any headway against it. It sighs and groans and mourns and is troubled, as David speaks of himself, but it seldom pursues sin. David complains that his sins had taken fast hold of him, so that he could not look up (Ps 40:12). How little, then, was he able to fight against it! Now, several things are required for this fighting against sin and are included in it.

(1.) It is required that a man know he has such an enemy to deal with: that he take notice of it and consider it a real enemy, one to be destroyed by every possible means. As I said before, the contest is vigorous and dangerous; it is about the things of eternity. So when men have slight and passing thoughts of their lusts, it is no great sign that they are mortified, or that they are on the way to their mortification. This is every man’s “knowing the plague of his own heart” (1 Kgs 8:38), without which no other work can be done. It is to be feared that very many have little knowledge of the chief enemy they carry about in their bosoms. This makes them ready to justify themselves and impatient of reproof or admonition, not knowing they are in any danger (2 Chr 16:10).

(2.) The beginning of this warfare is to labor to become acquainted with the ways, wiles, methods, advantages, and occasions of its success. This is how men deal with enemies. They search out their counsels and plans, weigh their aims, and consider how and by what means they have prevailed before, so that they may be forestalled. The greatest skill in generalship consists in this. Take this away, and all waging of war, in which human wisdom and industry are most fully employed, would be brutish. So do those who truly mortify lust deal with it. Not only when it is actually troubling, enticing, and seducing them, but in their private hours, they consider: “This is our enemy. This is his way and progress; these are his advantages; this is how he has prevailed, and this is what he will do if he is not forestalled.” So David says, “My sin is always before me” (Ps 51:3). Indeed, one of the choicest and most excellent parts of practical spiritual wisdom consists in finding out the subtleties, schemes, and depths of any indwelling sin. It is to consider and know where its greatest strength lies; what advantage it tends to make of occasions, opportunities, and temptations; what its pleas, pretenses, and reasonings are; what its stratagems, disguises, and excuses are. It is to set the wisdom of the Spirit against the craft of the old man; to trace this serpent in all its turnings and windings; to be able to say, at its most secret workings (imperceptible to an ordinary frame of heart), “This is your old way and course; I know what you are aiming at.” And so to be always in readiness is a good part of our warfare.

(3.) The height of this contest is to load it daily with all the things that will be mentioned later, which are grievous, killing, and destructive to it. Such a man never thinks his lust is dead because it is quiet, but still labors to give it new wounds and new blows every day. So does the apostle (Col 3:5).

Now, while the soul is in this condition, while it is dealing with sin like this, it is certainly uppermost; sin is under the sword and dying.

3. Mortification consists in success. Frequent success against any lust is another part and evidence of mortification. By success I do not mean a mere thwarting of sin, so that it is not brought forth or accomplished. I mean a victory over it, and a pursuit of it to complete conquest. For instance, when the heart finds sin at work at any time, seducing and forming imaginations to make provision for the flesh, to fulfill its lusts, it instantly arrests sin, brings it to the law of God and the love of Christ, condemns it, and follows it with execution to the uttermost.

Now, I say, when a man comes to this state and condition, then sin is mortified in some considerable measure: when lust is weakened in its root and principle, so that its stirrings and actions are fewer and weaker than before, and cannot hinder his duty or interrupt his peace; when he can, in a quiet, calm frame of spirit, find out sin and fight against it and have success against it. And then, for all its opposition, a man may have peace with God all his days.

To these heads, then, I refer the mortification aimed at, that is, the mortification of any one perplexing disorder through which the general depravity and corruption of our nature tries to exert and put itself forth.

(1.) First, the foundation of it is the weakening of its indwelling disposition, by which it inclines, entices, and drives to evil, rebels, opposes, and fights against God. This is done by the implanting, the settled dwelling, and the cherishing of a principle of grace that stands in direct opposition to it and destroys it. So pride is weakened by the implanting and growth of humility, anger by patience, impurity by purity of mind and conscience, love of this world by heavenly-mindedness. These are graces of the Spirit, or the same habitual grace acting in various ways by the Holy Spirit, according to the variety or diversity of the objects it is exercised about; just as the others are different lusts, or the same natural corruption acting in various ways, according to the various advantages and occasions it meets with.

(2.) A second thing required is the promptness, eagerness, vigor of the Spirit, or the new man, in contending with and cheerfully fighting against the lust in question, by all the ways and with all the means appointed for it, constantly using the help provided against its stirrings and workings.

(3.) Success, in various degrees, attends these two. If the disorder does not have an unconquerable advantage from its natural situation, this success may possibly reach such a complete conquest that the soul never again perceptibly feels its opposition. And in any case it will certainly rise to a granting of peace to the conscience, according to the terms of the covenant of grace.

II. General Rules

Next we come to consider the ways and means by which a soul may go about the mortification of any particular lust and sin that Satan takes advantage of to disquiet and weaken him.

But first some general considerations must be laid down about certain principles and foundations of this work. Without them no man in the world can attain to the mortification of any sin, however much he is stirred by convictions and resolved to mortify it.

These are the general rules and principles without which no sin will ever be mortified:

First Rule: Only a Believer Can Mortify Sin

1. Unless a man is a believer, that is, one truly grafted into Christ, he can never mortify a single sin. I do not say unless he knows himself to be one, but unless he really is one. Mortification is the work of believers: “if by the Spirit you+ put to death,” and so on (Rom 8:13), that is, you+ believers, for whom there is no condemnation (verse 1). They alone are urged to it: “Put to death, therefore, the components of your+ earthly nature” (Col 3:5). Who is to mortify? You+ who “have been raised with Christ” (verse 1), whose “life is now hidden with Christ in God” (verse 3), who “will appear with Him in glory” (verse 4). An unregenerate man may do something like it, but the work itself, in a way acceptable to God, he can never perform. You+ know what a picture of it some of the philosophers drew: Seneca, Cicero, Epictetus. What moving discourses they have about contempt of the world and of self, and about governing and conquering all excessive affections and passions! Yet the lives of most of them showed that their maxims differed as much from true mortification as the sun painted on a signpost differs from the sun in the sky; they had neither light nor heat. Their own Lucian shows well enough what they all were. There is no death of sin without the death of Christ. You+ know what attempts at it are made by the Papists, in their vows, penances, and satisfactions. I dare say of them (I mean as many of them as act on the principles of their church, as they call it) what Paul says of Israel concerning righteousness (Rom 9:31, 32): they have pursued mortification, but they have not attained it. Why not? “Because their pursuit was not by faith, but as if it were by works of the law.” The same is the state and condition of all among ourselves who, in obedience to their convictions and awakened consciences, try to give up sin. They pursue it, but they do not attain it.

It is true that it is, and it will be, required of every person whatever who hears the law or the gospel preached that he mortify sin. It is his duty, but it is not his immediate duty. It is his duty to do it, but to do it in God’s way. Suppose you require your servant to pay a sum of money for you in a certain place, but first to go and collect it in another. It is his duty to pay the money as appointed, and you will blame him if he does not. Yet that was not his immediate duty; he was first to collect it, as you directed. So it is in this case. Sin is to be mortified, but something must be done first to enable us to do it.

I have proved that it is the Spirit alone who can mortify sin. He is promised to do it, and all other means without him are empty and vain. How then shall a man who does not have the Spirit mortify sin? A man could more easily see without eyes or speak without a tongue than truly mortify one sin without the Spirit. Now, how is the Spirit obtained? He is the Spirit of Christ. As the apostle says, “if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to Christ” (Rom 8:9). So if we are Christ’s and have a share in him, we have the Spirit, and only so do we have power for mortification. The apostle argues this at length: “So then, those who are in the flesh cannot please God” (Rom 8:8). This is the inference and conclusion he draws from his previous discussion of our natural state and condition, and of the enmity toward God and his law that we have in it. If we are in the flesh, if we do not have the Spirit, we cannot do anything that would please God. But what is our deliverance from this condition? Verse 9: “You+, however, are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, if the Spirit of God lives in you+”; that is, you+ believers, who have the Spirit of Christ, are not in the flesh. There is no way of deliverance from the state and condition of being in the flesh except by the Spirit of Christ. And what if this Spirit of Christ is in you+? Why, then you+ are mortified. Verse 10: “your+ body is dead because of sin,” or to it; mortification is carried on; the new man is made alive to righteousness. The apostle proves this in verse 11 from the union we have with Christ by the Spirit, which will produce workings in us that match what the Spirit worked in him. All attempts, then, at mortifying any lust without a share in Christ are vain. Many men are galled with and for sin, when the arrows of Christ for conviction have been made sharp in their hearts by the preaching of the word or by some affliction. They set themselves vigorously against this or that particular lust, the one that has most disquieted or perplexed their consciences. But, poor creatures! they labor in the fire, and their work is burned up. When the Spirit of Christ comes to this work, “He will be like a refiner’s fire, like a launderer’s soap,” and he will purify men like gold and silver (Mal 3:2, 3). He will take away their dross and tin, their filth and blood, as in Isa 4:4. But men must be gold and silver at bottom, or refining will do them no good. The prophet gives us the sad outcome of wicked men’s utmost attempts at mortification, by whatever means God gives them: “The bellows blow fiercely, blasting away the lead with fire. The refining proceeds in vain. … They are called rejected silver, because the Lord has rejected them” (Jer 6:29, 30). And what is the reason for this? Verse 28: they were “bronze and iron” when they were put into the furnace. Men may refine bronze and iron for a long time before they will become good silver.

I say, then, that mortification is not the present business of unregenerate men. God does not call them to it yet. Conversion is their work, the conversion of the whole soul, not the mortification of this or that particular lust. You+ would laugh at a man you+ saw putting up a great building without taking any care for a foundation; especially if you+ saw him so foolish that, after a thousand experiences of what he built one day falling down the next, he still kept on in the same course. So it is with persons under conviction. They plainly see that the ground they gain against sin one day they lose the next, yet they go on in the same road, without asking where the destructive flaw in their progress lies. When the Jews, convicted of their sin, “were cut to the heart” and cried out, “What shall we do?” (Acts 2:37), what did Peter direct them to do? Did he tell them to go and mortify their pride, wrath, malice, cruelty, and the like? No; he knew that was not their present work. He called them to conversion and faith in Christ in general (verse 38). Let men first be thoroughly converted, and then, as they “look on the One they have pierced,” humbling and mortification will follow. So when John came preaching repentance and conversion, he said, “The axe is already laid at the root of the trees” (Matt 3:10). The Pharisees had been laying on heavy burdens, imposing tedious duties and rigid means of mortification, in fastings, washings, and the like, all in vain. John says: the doctrine of conversion is for you+; the axe in my hand is laid to the root. And our Savior tells us what is to be done in this case. He says, “Are grapes gathered from thornbushes?” (Matt 7:16). But suppose a thornbush is well pruned and cut, and pains are taken with it? Yes, but it will never bear figs (verses 17, 18); every tree must bring forth fruit according to its own kind. What, then, is to be done? He tells us: “Make a tree good and its fruit will be good” (Matt 12:33). The root must be dealt with and the nature of the tree changed, or no good fruit will be brought forth.

This is what I am aiming at: unless a man is regenerate, unless he is a believer, all the attempts he can make at mortification are to no purpose, however fair-seeming and promising they may be. So are all the means he can use, however diligently, earnestly, and watchfully he follows them, and with whatever striving of mind and spirit. In vain will he try many remedies; he will not be healed. Yes, several desperate evils attend the attempt to perform this duty by persons under conviction who are nothing more than convicted:

(1.) First, the mind and soul are taken up with what is not the man’s proper business, and so he is diverted from what is. God lays hold of some sin in him by his word and his judgments. He galls his conscience, disquiets his heart, and deprives him of his rest. Now other diversions will not serve; he must apply himself to the work before him. The business at hand is to awaken the whole man to consider the state and condition he is in, so that he may be brought home to God. Instead, he sets himself to mortify the sin that galls him. This comes purely from self-love, from a wish to be freed from his trouble, and has nothing to do with the work he is called to; and so he is diverted from it. So God tells us of Ephraim, when he says, “I will spread My net over them; I will bring them down like birds of the air. I will chastise them” (Hos 7:12): he caught them, entangled them, and convinced them that they could not escape. Yet he says of them, “They turn, but not to the Most High.” They set themselves to give up sin, but not in the way God called for it, by conversion of the whole man. So men are diverted from coming to God by the most glorious ways they can settle on for coming to him. And this is one of the most common deceits by which men ruin their own souls. I wish that some whose trade it is to whitewash flimsy walls in the things of God did not teach this deceit and lead the people astray by their ignorance. What do men do, what are they often directed to do, when their consciences are galled by sin, and disquiet from the Lord has laid hold of them? Is not the sum of what they apply themselves to this: to give up in practice the sin whose fruits perplex them, and to make headway against it? And is not the gospel aim of their convictions lost by it? Here men stay and perish.

(2.) Secondly, this duty is a good thing in itself, in its proper place; it is a duty that gives evidence of sincerity and brings peace home to the conscience. So when a man finds himself really engaged in it, his mind and heart set against this or that sin, with purpose and resolution to have nothing more to do with it, he is ready to conclude that his state and condition are good, and so he deludes his own soul. For:

(1.) His conscience has been made sick with sin, and he could find no rest. Then he should go to the great Physician of souls and get healing in his blood. Instead, by this engagement against sin the man pacifies and quiets his conscience, and settles down without going to Christ at all. Ah! how many poor souls are deluded like this to eternity! “When Ephraim saw his sickness …, then Ephraim … sent to the great king” (Hos 5:13), and that kept him away from God. The whole bundle of the popish religion is made up of designs and schemes to pacify conscience without Christ, all described by the apostle in Rom 10:3.

(2.) By this means men satisfy themselves that their state and condition are good, since they are doing a work that is good in itself, and they are not doing it to be seen. They know they wish the work to be done in sincerity, and so they are hardened in a kind of self-righteousness.

(3.) When a man has been deluded like this for a time and has deceived his own soul, he finds over a long course of life that his sin is in fact not mortified, or that if he has changed one sin he has gotten another. Then at length he begins to think that all struggling is in vain and that he will never be able to prevail; he is building a dam against water that keeps rising on him. So he gives up, as one who despairs of any success, and yields himself to the power of sin and to the habit of formality he has acquired.

And this is the usual outcome for persons who attempt to mortify sin without first obtaining a share in Christ. It deludes them, hardens them, destroys them. That is why we see that there are usually no more vile and desperate sinners in the world than those who were put on this course by conviction, found it fruitless, and abandoned it without Christ being revealed to them. And this is the substance of the religion and godliness of the choicest formalists in the world, and of all those in the Roman synagogue who are driven to mortification, as they drive Indians to baptism or cattle to water. I say, then, that mortification is the work of believers, and of believers only. To kill sin is the work of living men. Where men are dead (as all unbelievers, the best of them, are dead), sin is alive, and will live.

2. It is the work of faith, the work that belongs to faith alone. Now, if there is a work to be done that only one instrument can accomplish, it is the greatest madness for anyone who does not have that instrument to try to do it. And it is faith that purifies the heart (Acts 15:9); or, as Peter puts it, we purify our souls “by obedience to the truth through the Spirit” (1 Pet 1:22). Without faith it will not be done.

What has been said is enough, I suppose, to make good my first general rule: Be sure to get a share in Christ if you intend to mortify any sin; without it, it will never be done.

Objection. You+ will say, “What, then, would you have unregenerate men do who are convicted of the evil of sin? Shall they stop striving against sin, live dissolutely, give their lusts free rein, and be as bad as the worst of men? That would be a way to throw the whole world into confusion, to bring everything into darkness, to open the floodgates of lust, and to lay the reins on men’s necks, so that they rush into every sin with delight and greed, like a horse charging into battle.”

Answer. 1. God forbid! We must regard it as a great work of the wisdom, goodness, and love of God that by many ways and means he is pleased to restrain the sons of men from running out into all the excess and wild living that the depravity of their nature would carry them into with violence. By whatever way this is done, it comes from the care, kindness, and goodness of God; without it the whole earth would be a hell of sin and confusion.

(2.) There is a special convicting power in the word, which God is often pleased to put forth, to the wounding, dismaying, and in some sense humbling of sinners, even though they are never converted. And the word is to be preached though it has this effect, yet not for this end. So let the word be preached and men’s sins be rebuked: lust will be restrained, and some opposition will be made against sin, though that is not the effect aimed at.

(3.) Though this is the work of the word and the Spirit, and good in itself, yet it is not profitable or effective for the main end in those in whom it is worked. They are still poisoned by bitterness and under the power of darkness.

(4.) Let men know that it is their duty, but in its proper place. I do not take men off mortification; I put them on conversion. The man who calls another away from mending a hole in the wall of his house to put out a fire that is consuming the whole building is not his enemy. Poor soul! It is not your sore finger but your wasting fever that you are to apply yourself to consider. You set yourself against a particular sin, and do not consider that you are nothing but sin.

Let me add this for those who are preachers of the word, or who intend, by the good hand of God, to take up that work. It is their duty to plead with men about their sins and to lay heavy blows on particular sins. But let them always remember to do it with the proper end of law and gospel in view: that is, to use the sin they speak against to uncover the state and condition the sinner is in. Otherwise, perhaps, they may bring men to formality and hypocrisy, but little of the true end of preaching the gospel will be achieved. It will do no good to beat a man off his drunkenness into a sober formality. A skillful master of the assemblies lays his axe to the root and always drives at the heart. To speak out against the particular sins of ignorant, unregenerate persons, such as the land is full of, is a good work. Yet though it may be done with great efficacy, vigor, and success, if all its effect is that they set themselves to the most diligent efforts to mortify the sins preached against, all that has been done is like beating an enemy in an open field and driving him into an impregnable castle, where he cannot be defeated. Whenever you have a sinner at an advantage, on account of any one sin whatever, if you have anything to take hold of him by, bring it to his state and condition, drive it home to the head, and deal with him there. To break men off particular sins and not to break their hearts is to rob ourselves of our advantages for dealing with them.

And here the Roman mortification is grievously at fault. They drive all sorts of persons to it, without the least consideration of whether they have a principle for it or not. Yes, they are so far from calling on men to believe, so that they may be able to mortify their lusts, that they call men to mortification instead of believing. The truth is, they know neither what it is to believe nor what mortification itself means. Faith with them is only a general assent to the doctrine taught in their church; and mortification is a man’s binding himself by a vow to some fixed course of life in which he denies himself some of the use of the things of this world, not without a considerable compensation. Such men know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God. Their boasting of their mortification is only glorying in their shame. Some casuists among ourselves overlook the necessity of regeneration and openly give this as a direction to all sorts of persons who complain of any sin or lust: that they should vow against it, at least for a time, a month or so. They seem to have a scrap of light in the mystery of the gospel, much like that of Nicodemus when he first came to Christ. They tell men to vow to abstain from their sin for a time. This commonly makes their lust more violent. Perhaps with great perplexity they keep their word; perhaps not, which increases their guilt and torment. Is their sin mortified at all by this? Do they find a conquest over it? Is their condition changed, even if they manage to give it up? Are they not still poisoned by bitterness? Is this not to make men make bricks, if not without straw, then, which is worse, without strength? What promise does any unregenerate man have to support him in this work? What help to perform it? Can sin be killed without a share in the death of Christ, or mortified without the Spirit? If such directions should succeed in changing men’s lives, as they seldom do, they still never reach the change of their hearts or of their condition. They may make men self-justifiers or hypocrites, not Christians. It often grieves me to see poor souls who have a zeal for God and a desire for their eternal welfare kept all their days by such directors and directions under a hard, burdensome, outward worship and service of God, with many fair-seeming efforts at mortification, in utter ignorance of the righteousness of Christ and unacquainted with his Spirit. I know too many persons and things of this kind. If God ever shines into their hearts to give them the knowledge of his glory in the face of his Son Jesus Christ, they will see the folly of their present way.

Chapter VIII

The second general rule proposed. Without sincerity in mortifying every lust, no lust will be mortified. Partial mortification always comes from a corrupt principle. The perplexity of temptation from a lust is often a chastening for other negligences.

Second Rule: Sincerity in a Universal Obedience

The second principle I propose for this purpose is this: Without sincerity and diligence in obedience in all things, no mortification of any one perplexing lust is to be obtained. The first rule concerned the person; this one concerns the thing itself. Let me explain this position a little.

A man finds that some lust brings him into the condition described before. It is powerful, strong, and turbulent; it leads him captive, harasses him, disquiets him, and takes away his peace. He cannot bear it. So he sets himself against it, prays against it, groans under it, and sighs to be delivered. But in the meantime, perhaps, he is loose and negligent in other duties: in constant communion with God, in reading, prayer, and meditation, and in other ways that are not of the same kind as the lust that troubles him. Let not that man think he will ever arrive at the mortification of the lust he is perplexed with. This is a condition that often befalls men in their pilgrimage. The Israelites, under a sense of their sin, drew near to God with great diligence and earnestness, with fasting and prayer (Isa 58). Much is said of their earnestness in the work. Verse 2: “Day after day they seek Me and delight to know My ways. They ask Me for righteous judgments; they delight in the nearness of God.” But God rejects it all. Their fast is a remedy that will not heal them. The reason given for it (verses 5–7) is that they were partial in this duty. They attended to it diligently, but in other duties they were negligent and careless. Take a man who has a “running sore” (it is the Scripture’s expression) that comes from an unhealthy state of body, brought on by intemperance and bad diet. Let him apply himself with all the diligence and skill he can to the cure of his sore. If he leaves the general state of his body in its disorders, his labor and trouble will be in vain. So will the attempts of the man who tries to stop a bloody flow of sin and filth in his soul, but is not equally careful of his whole spiritual health and constitution. For:

1. This kind of effort at mortification comes from a corrupt principle, ground, and foundation, so it will never come to a good end. The true and acceptable principles of mortification will be dealt with later. Hatred of sin as sin, and not only as something galling or disquieting, and a sense of the love of Christ in the cross, lie at the bottom of all true spiritual mortification. Now, it is certain that the effort I am speaking of comes from self-love. You set yourself with all diligence and earnestness to mortify some lust or sin. What is the reason for it? It disquiets you. It has taken away your peace. It fills your heart with sorrow, trouble, and fear. You have no rest because of it. Yes; but, friend, you have neglected prayer or reading. You have been vain and loose in your conduct in other things, things not of the same nature as the lust that perplexes you. These are no less sins and evils than the ones you groan under. Jesus Christ bled for them too. Why do you not set yourself against them too? If you hated sin as sin, every evil way, you would be no less watchful against everything that grieves and disquiets the Spirit of God than against what grieves and disquiets your own soul. It is plain that you fight against sin only because of the trouble it gives you. If your conscience could be quiet under it, you would let it alone. If it did not disquiet you, it would not be disquieted by you. Now, can you think that God will join in with such hypocritical efforts? That his Spirit will ever bear witness to the treachery and falseness of your spirit? Do you think he will relieve you of what perplexes you, so that you may be free for what grieves him no less? No. God says, “Here is a man I would never hear from again if he could be rid of this lust. Let him wrestle with this, or he is lost.” Let no man think he will do his own work if he will not do God’s. God’s work consists in obedience in all things; to be freed from the present perplexity is only their own. That is why the apostle says, “Let us cleanse ourselves from everything that defiles body and spirit, perfecting holiness in the fear of God” (2 Cor 7:1). If we will do anything, we must do everything. So what is accepted is not merely an intense opposition to this or that particular lust. It is a humble frame and disposition of heart in all things, watchful over every evil and for the doing of every duty.

2. How do you know that God has not allowed the lust that perplexes you to gain strength in you and power over you, to chasten you for your other negligences and your general lukewarmness in walking before him? Or at least to awaken you to consider your ways, so that you may make a thorough work and change in your whole course of walking with him? The rage and dominance of a particular lust is commonly the fruit and result of a careless, negligent course in general. That is so for two reasons:

(1) As its natural effect, if I may put it so. As I showed, lust in general lies in the heart of every man, even the best, as long as he lives. And do not think the Scripture speaks in vain when it says that lust is subtle, cunning, and crafty, that it seduces, entices, fights, and rebels. While a man keeps a diligent watch over his heart, its root and fountain, while above all keeping he keeps his heart, from which flow the springs of life and death, lust withers and dies in it. But suppose that through negligence it bursts out in some particular direction. It finds a passage to the thoughts through the affections, and from them and through them perhaps breaks out into open sin in his conduct. Then its strength bears in the direction it has found, and it presses mainly that way. Having found a passage, it then harasses and disquiets him, and it is not easily restrained. So a man may perhaps have to wrestle in sorrow all his days with what a strict watch over everything might easily have prevented.

(2) As I said, God often allows it in order to chasten our other negligences. With wicked men, he gives them up to one sin as the judgment of another,3 a greater as the punishment of a lesser, or to one that will hold them more firmly and securely, for a sin they might perhaps have been delivered from. In the same way, even with his own people, he may leave them, and does leave them, at times to some vexing disorder, either to prevent or to cure some other evil. So the messenger of Satan was let loose on Paul, to keep him “from becoming conceited” because of the “surpassingly great revelations.”4 Was it not a correction of Peter’s vain confidence that he was left to deny his Master? Now, if this is the state and condition of lust when it prevails, that God often allows it to prevail, at least to warn us and humble us, perhaps to chasten and correct us for our generally loose and careless walking, is it possible that the effect should be removed while the cause continues? That the particular lust should be mortified while the general course is not reformed? So the man who would really, thoroughly, and acceptably mortify any disquieting lust must take care to be equally diligent in every part of obedience. And let him know that every lust, every neglect of duty, is a burden to God,5 though only one is a burden to the man himself. As long as there is treachery in the heart, ready to indulge any negligence and not pressing on to all perfection in obedience, the soul is weak, since it does not give faith its whole work; and selfish, since it thinks more of the trouble of sin than of its filth and guilt; and it lives under a constant provocation of God. So it cannot expect any encouraging outcome in any spiritual duty it undertakes, much less in this one we are considering, which requires another principle and frame of spirit to accomplish it.

III. Particular Directions

With these general rules laid down, the next thing to propose is particular directions to guide the soul when it feels a disquieting lust or disorder, and this is the main thing I aim at. Some of these directions are preliminary and preparatory; in others the work itself is contained. The first kind are these:

First Direction: Consider the Dangerous Symptoms of the Lust

1. First, consider what dangerous symptoms come with your lust or accompany it, whether it has any deadly mark on it or not. If it has, extraordinary remedies must be used; an ordinary course of mortification will not do it.

You+ will say, “What are these dangerous marks and symptoms you mean, the desperate signs that come with an indwelling lust?” I will name some of them:

(1) Long standing. If it has lain corrupting in your heart for a long time, if you have allowed it to remain in power and prevail for some long season without vigorously trying to kill it and to heal the wounds you have received from it, your disorder is dangerous. Have you for some long season allowed worldliness, ambition, or greed for study to eat up other duties, the duties in which you ought to hold constant communion with God? Or have you allowed uncleanness to defile your heart with vain, foolish, and wicked imaginations for many days? Your lust has a dangerous symptom. So it was with David: “My wounds are foul and festering because of my sinful folly” (Ps 38:5). When a lust has lain long in the heart, corrupting, festering, eating away like a cancer, it brings the soul into a woeful condition. In such a case an ordinary course of humbling oneself will not do the work. Whatever the lust is, by this means it will work its way more or less into all the faculties of the soul and accustom the affections to its company and society. It grows familiar to the mind and conscience, so that they are not startled at it as at a strange thing, but are bold with it as with something they are used to. Yes, it gains such an advantage by this means that it often exerts itself and acts without being noticed at all, as seems to have been the case with Joseph when he swore by the life of Pharaoh. Unless some extraordinary course is taken, such a person has no ground in the world to expect that his latter end will be peace.

For, first, how will he be able to tell the difference between the long stay of an unmortified lust and the dominion of sin, which cannot befall a regenerate person? Secondly, how can he promise himself that it will ever be otherwise with him, or that his lust will stop raging and seducing? He sees it fixed and staying, as it has for many days, and it has gone through a variety of conditions with him. Perhaps it has been tried with mercies and afflictions, and those perhaps so remarkable that the soul could not help taking special notice of them. Perhaps it has weathered many a storm and come through a great variety of gifts in the ministry of the word. Will it be an easy thing, then, to evict a lodger who pleads a right of long possession? Old neglected wounds are often fatal, and always dangerous. Indwelling disorders grow sluggish and stubborn by continuing in ease and quiet. Lust is a lodger that will not easily be thrown out if it can plead time and some right of possession. As it never dies of itself, so if it is not killed daily it will always gather strength.

(2) Another dangerous symptom of a deadly disorder in the heart is this: the heart secretly pleads to excuse itself and keep up its peace while a lust remains, without a vigorous gospel effort to mortify it. There are several ways this may be done. I will name some of them:

[1.] When a man has thoughts about sin, perplexing thoughts, and instead of applying himself to destroy it, he searches his heart to see what evidence he can find that his condition is good in spite of that sin and lust, so that it may go well with him.

For a man to gather up his experiences of God, to call them to mind, collect them, consider, test, and make use of them, is an excellent thing. It is a duty practiced by all the saints and commended in the Old Testament and the New. This was David’s work when he mused in his heart and called to remembrance the former loving kindness of the Lord (Ps 77:6–9). This is the duty Paul sets us to practice (2 Cor 13:5). And as it is excellent in itself, so beauty is added to it by a fitting season: a time of trial or temptation, or of the heart’s disquiet about sin. Such a time is a setting of silver to set off this apple of gold, as Solomon speaks. But to do it for this purpose, to quiet a conscience that cries and calls for something else, is a desperate device of a heart in love with sin. Conscience deals with a man; God rebukes him for the sinful disorder of his heart. If, instead of applying himself to get that sin pardoned in the blood of Christ and mortified by his Spirit, he relieves himself with such other evidences as he has, or thinks he has, and so frees himself from under the yoke God was putting on his neck, his condition is very dangerous and his wound hardly curable. So the Jews, galled by their own consciences and by the convicting preaching of our Savior, held themselves up with this: that they were “Abraham’s children,” and accepted with God on that account. And so they excused themselves in all abominable wickedness, to their utter ruin.

This is, in some degree, a man blessing himself and saying that on one account or another he will have peace, “although he adds drunkenness to thirst.” Love of sin, and a low value on peace and on every taste of love from God, are wrapped up in such a frame. Such a man plainly shows that if he can only keep up a hope of escaping “the coming wrath,” he is well content to be unfruitful in the world, at any distance from God short of final separation. What can be expected from such a heart?

[2.] This deceit is also carried on by applying grace and mercy to a sin that is not mortified, or that a man is not sincerely trying to mortify. This is a sign of a heart greatly entangled with the love of sin. When a man has secret thoughts in his heart not unlike Naaman’s about his worship in the house of Rimmon,6 “In all other things I will walk with God, but in this one thing, may God be merciful to me,” his condition is sad. It is true that a resolution of this kind, to indulge oneself in any sin on account of mercy, seems to be, and as a course of life certainly is, altogether inconsistent with Christian sincerity. It is the badge of a hypocrite, and is to “turn the grace of our God into a license for immorality” (Jude 4). Yet I do not doubt that through Satan’s craft and their own remaining unbelief, the children of God themselves may sometimes be ensnared by this deceit of sin. Otherwise Paul would never have warned them against it as he does (Rom 6:1, 2). Indeed, nothing is more natural than for fleshly reasonings to grow loud and strong on this account. The flesh would gladly be indulged on account of grace. It stands ready to catch at every word spoken of mercy and to twist it to its own corrupt aims and purposes. To apply mercy, then, to a sin that is not vigorously mortified is to make the gospel serve the purpose of the flesh.

A deceitful heart will sometimes use these and many other ways and tricks to excuse itself in its abominations. Now suppose a man and his sin are in this condition: a secret liking for the sin prevails in his heart. His will is not wholly set on it, yet he has a faint wish toward it; he would practice it were it not for this or that consideration. And on this footing he relieves himself in other ways than by the mortification and pardon of it in the blood of Christ. That man’s “wounds are foul and festering,” and without speedy deliverance he will be at the door of death.

(3) Another dangerous symptom is frequent success in sin’s seduction, in gaining the will’s prevailing consent to it. This is what I mean: when the sin we are speaking of gets the consent of the will with some delight, then it has success, even if it is not actually carried out outwardly. For outward reasons a man may not be able to go along with sin to what James calls its being “full-grown” (Jas 1:14, 15), as far as the outward acts of sin go, while the will to sin may actually have been gained. Then, I say, it has success. Now, if any lust can prevail this far in any man’s soul, his condition may possibly be very bad, and he himself unregenerate. At any rate it cannot possibly be very good; it is dangerous. And it comes to the same thing whether this is done by the choice of the will or by inadvertence, for that inadvertence is itself in a manner chosen. When we are inadvertent and negligent where we are bound to be watchful and careful, that inadvertence does not make what we do as a result any less voluntary. Men do not choose and resolve to be negligent and inadvertent. Yet if they choose the things that will make them so, they choose inadvertence itself, as a thing may be chosen in its cause.

And let no man think that the evil of his heart is in any way lessened because, for the most part, he seems to be taken by surprise into the consent he seems to give to sin. It is his negligence in the duty of watching over his heart that betrays him into that surprise.

(4) When a man fights against his sin only with arguments from the outcome, or from the punishment due to it, it is a sign that sin has taken great possession of the will, and that in the heart there is an overflowing of wickedness. The man who opposes nothing to the seduction of sin and lust in his heart but fear of shame among men or of hell from God is well enough resolved to do the sin if no punishment came with it. And how that differs from living in the practice of sin, I do not know. Those who are Christ’s, and are moved in their obedience by gospel principles, have the death of Christ, the love of God, the detestable nature of sin, the preciousness of communion with God, and a deep-rooted hatred of sin as sin, to oppose to every seduction of sin, to all the workings, strivings, and fightings of lust in their hearts. So it was with Joseph. “How could I do such a great evil,” he says, “and sin against God”—my good and gracious God?7 And Paul: “Christ’s love compels us”;8 and, “Since we have these promises, let us cleanse ourselves from everything that defiles flesh and spirit.”9 But suppose a man is so under the power of his lust that he has nothing but the law to oppose it with. He cannot fight against it with gospel weapons, but deals with it entirely with hell and judgment, which are the proper weapons of the law. Then it is plain that sin has taken possession of his will and affections, prevailing over them and conquering them to a very great degree.

As far as this particular sin goes, such a person has thrown off the guidance of renewing grace, and is kept from ruin only by restraining grace. To that extent he has fallen from grace and returned under the power of the law. And can anyone think it is not a great provocation to Christ that men should throw off his easy and gentle yoke and rule, and put themselves under the iron yoke of the law, merely to indulge their lusts?

Test yourself by this too. When sin brings you to the point where you must either serve it and rush into folly at its command, like a horse charging into battle, or else take a stand against it to suppress it, what do you say to your soul? What do you argue with yourself? Is this all: “Hell will be the end of this course; vengeance will meet me and find me out”? It is time for you to look about you; evil lies at the door. Paul’s main argument to prove that sin will not have dominion over believers is that they are “not under law, but under grace” (Rom 6:14). If your fighting against sin is all on the grounds of the law, from the principles and motives of the law, what assurance can you reach that sin will not have dominion over you, which would be your ruin?

Yes, and know that this last line of defense will not hold out long. If your lust has driven you out of the stronger forts of the gospel, it will soon prevail against this one too. Do not suppose that such considerations will deliver you, when you have willingly given up to your enemy the helps and means of preservation that have a thousand times their strength. Rest assured of this: unless you quickly recover yourself from this condition, the thing you fear will come upon you. What gospel principles do not do, motives of the law cannot do.

(5) Another dangerous symptom is when it is likely that there is, or may be, something of judicial hardening, or at least of chastening punishment, in your lust as it disquiets you. I have no doubt at all that God sometimes leaves even some of his own under at least the perplexing power of some lust or sin, to correct them for former sins, negligence, and folly. That is why the church complained, “Why, O Lord, do You … harden our hearts from fearing You?” (Isa 63:17). No man questions that this is his way of dealing with unregenerate men. But how can a man know whether God’s chastening hand has any part in his being left to the disquiet of his disorder?

Answer. Examine your heart and your ways. What was the state and condition of your soul before you fell into the entanglements of the sin you now complain of so much? Had you been negligent in duties? Had you been living self-indulgently? Is the guilt of some great sin lying on you, unrepented of? A new sin may be allowed, as well as a new affliction sent, to bring an old sin to remembrance.

Have you received some outstanding mercy, protection, or deliverance that you did not make good use of, or were not thankful for? Or have you been exercised with some affliction without laboring for the end it was appointed for? Or have you failed to use the opportunities of glorifying God in your generation that he had graciously given you in his good providence? Or have you conformed yourself to the world and the men of it, through the abundance of temptations in the days you live in?

If you find that this has been your state, wake up and call on God. You are fast asleep in a storm of anger all around you.

(6) When your lust has already withstood particular dealings from God against it. This condition is described in Isa 57:17: “I was enraged by his sinful greed, so I struck him and hid My face in anger; yet he kept turning back to the desires of his heart.” God had dealt with them about their prevailing lust, and in several ways, by affliction and by withdrawing from them, but they held out against it all. This is a sad condition. Nothing but sheer sovereign grace (as God expresses it in the next verse) can relieve a man in it, and no man ought to promise himself that grace, or presume upon it. In his providential dealings God often meets with a man and speaks particularly to the evil of his heart, as he did with Joseph’s brothers over their selling him into Egypt. This makes the man reflect on his sin and judge himself for it in particular. God makes it the voice of the danger, affliction, trouble, or sickness he is in or under. Sometimes, in reading the word, God makes a man stop at something that cuts him to the heart and shakes him about his present condition. More often God meets with men in the hearing of the word preached, his great ordinance for conviction, conversion, and edification. In that ordinance God often hews men with the sword of his word. He strikes directly at their dearly loved lust, startles the sinner, and makes him pledge himself to mortify and give up the evil of his heart. Now, suppose his lust has taken such hold on him that it forces him to break these bonds of the Lord and throw off these cords. Suppose it overcomes these convictions and gets back into its old position, and can heal the wounds it received this way. That soul is in a sad condition.

The evils that come with such a frame of heart are beyond words. Every particular warning to a man in such a state is a priceless mercy. How then he despises God in them, when he holds out against them! And what infinite patience this is in God, that he does not cast off such a man and swear in his wrath that he will never enter into his rest!

These and many other evidences there are of a lust that is dangerous, if not deadly. As our Savior said of the evil spirit, “This kind cannot come out, except by prayer and fasting,” so I say of lusts of this kind. An ordinary course of mortification will not do it; extraordinary ways must be settled on.

This is the first particular direction: consider whether the lust or sin you are fighting with has any of these dangerous symptoms accompanying it.

Before I go on, I must give one caution by the way, so that no one is deceived by what has been said. I say that the things and evils mentioned above may befall true believers. But let no man who finds the same things in himself conclude from them that he is a true believer. These are the evils believers may fall into and be ensnared by, not the things that make a man a believer. A man might as well conclude that he is a believer because he is an adulterer, since David, a true believer, fell into adultery, as conclude it from the signs above, which are the evils of sin and Satan in the hearts of believers. The seventh chapter of Romans contains the description of a regenerate man. Suppose a man considers what is said there of his dark side, of his unregenerate part, of the indwelling power and violence of sin remaining in him, and because he finds the like in himself, concludes that he is a regenerate man. He will be mistaken in his reckoning. It is just as if you+ were to argue: a wise man may be sick and wounded, and may even do some things foolishly; therefore everyone who is sick and wounded and does things foolishly is a wise man. Or it is as if a poor, deformed creature heard someone speak of a beautiful person, and say that he had a mark or a scar that greatly disfigured him, and then concluded that because he himself has scars and moles and warts, he too is beautiful. If you+ want evidence that you+ are believers, it must come from the things that make men believers. The man who has these evils in himself may safely conclude, “If I am a believer, I am a most miserable one.” But to know that he is a believer, he must look for other evidence, if he wants peace.

Second Direction: A Clear Sense of the Guilt, Danger, and Evil of the Sin

The second direction is this: Get a clear and lasting sense on your mind and conscience of the (1) guilt, (2) danger, and (3) evil of the sin that perplexes you.

(1) Of its guilt. It is one of the deceits of a prevailing lust to make light of its own guilt. “Is it not a small one?” “When I bow down in the temple of Rimmon, may God be merciful to me in this thing.” “Though this is bad, it is not as bad as such and such an evil. Others of the people of God have been in such a frame. Yes, what dreadful sins in act some of them have fallen into!” There are countless ways in which sin turns the mind away from a right and proper grasp of its guilt. Its foul vapors darken the mind, so that it cannot judge things rightly. Perplexing reasonings, promises that make light of sin, raging desires, treacherous resolutions to give it up, hopes of mercy: all have their share in disturbing the mind as it considers the guilt of a prevailing lust. The prophet tells us that when lust comes to its height it will do this completely: “Promiscuity, wine, and new wine take away the heart” (Hos 4:11), the heart, that is, the understanding, as the word is often used in Scripture. And as these things accomplish this work to the full in unregenerate persons, so they do it in part in the regenerate also. Solomon tells you of the man who was enticed by the immoral woman that he was “among the simple”; he was “a young man lacking understanding” (Prov 7:7). And where did his folly show itself? Why, he says in verse 23, he went on “not knowing it will cost him his life.” He did not consider the guilt of the evil he was caught up in. And when the Lord gives a reason why his dealings with Ephraim had no better effect, he gives this account: “Ephraim has become like a silly dove, without heart” (Hos 7:11); he had no understanding of his own miserable condition. Would it have been possible for David to lie so long in the guilt of that abominable sin, if he had not had countless corrupt reasonings keeping him from taking a clear view of its ugliness and guilt in the mirror of the law? That is why the prophet who was sent to awaken him, in his dealings with him, shut off every loophole and pretense by his parable, so that David might come fully under a sense of its guilt. This is the natural result of lust in the heart: it darkens the mind so that it will not judge its guilt rightly. And it has many other ways of making light of itself that I will not dwell on now. So let this be the first care of the man who would mortify sin: to fix a right judgment of its guilt in his mind. To that end, take these considerations to help you:

(1.) In those who have inherent grace, the power of sin is weakened by it, so that sin will not have dominion over them as it has over others. Yet the guilt of the sin that still remains and abides is made heavier and greater by that grace: “What then shall we say? Shall we continue in sin so that grace may increase? Certainly not! How can we who died to sin live in it any longer?” (Rom 6:1, 2). “How can we who died?” The emphasis is on the word “we.” How can we do it, who, as he describes it afterward, have received grace from Christ to the contrary? If we do it, we are surely more evil than any. I will not dwell on what especially makes the sins of such persons heavier: how they sin against more love, mercy, grace, help, relief, means, and deliverances than others. But let this consideration stay in your mind: there is inconceivably more evil and guilt in the evil that remains in your heart than there would be in as much sin if you had no grace at all. Observe:

(2.) God sees a great deal of beauty and excellence in the desires of his servants’ hearts, more than in the most glorious works of other men, yes, more than in most of their own outward performances, which have a greater mixture of sin than the desires and longings of grace in the heart have. In the same way, God sees a great deal of evil in the working of lust in their hearts, yes, more than in the open, notorious acts of wicked men, or in many outward sins the saints may fall into, since more opposition is made against those, and more humbling generally follows them. So Christ, dealing with his declining children, goes to the root with them and sets aside what they profess: I know you (Rev 3:15); you are something quite different from what you profess, and this makes you abominable.

So let these things, and considerations like them, bring you to a clear sense of the guilt of your indwelling lust, so that there may be no room in your heart for thoughts that make light of it or excuse it, through which sin will gain strength and prevail before you know it.

(2) Consider its danger, which is of many kinds:

(1.) The danger of being hardened by its deceitfulness. The apostle presses this hard on the Hebrews: “Take heed, brothers, that none of you+ has a wicked heart of unbelief that turns away from the living God. But exhort one another daily, as long as it is called today, so that none of you+ may be hardened by sin’s deceitfulness” (Heb 3:12, 13). “Take heed,” he says: use every means, consider your+ temptations, watch diligently; there is a treachery, a deceit, in sin that tends to harden your+ hearts from the fear of God. The hardening mentioned here goes to the utmost, to utter hardness of heart. Sin tends toward it, and every disorder and lust will make at least some progress toward it. You who were tender, who used to melt under the word and under afflictions, will grow, as some have profanely put it, “sermon-proof and sickness-proof.” You who used to tremble at the presence of God, at thoughts of death and of appearing before him, when you had more assurance of his love than you have now, will have a hardness on your spirit that these things cannot move. Your soul and your sin will be spoken of and spoken to, and you will not be concerned at all. You will be able to go through duties, praying, hearing, and reading, and your heart not be affected in the least. Sin will grow a light thing to you; you will pass it by as a thing of nothing. This is what it will grow to. And what will be the end of such a condition? Can anything sadder befall you? Is it not enough to make any heart tremble, to think of being brought into a state where he has slight thoughts of sin? Slight thoughts of grace, of mercy, of the blood of Christ, of the law, heaven, and hell, all come in at the same time. Take heed: this is what your lust is working toward, the hardening of the heart, the searing of the conscience, the blinding of the mind, the numbing of the affections, and the deceiving of the whole soul.

(2.) The danger of some great temporal correction, which the Scripture calls “vengeance,” “judgment,” and “punishment.” Though God should not utterly cast you off for this abomination that lies in your heart, yet he will visit you with the rod (Ps 89:30–33). Though he pardons and forgives, he will take vengeance on your misdeeds. Oh, remember David and all his troubles! Look at him fleeing into the wilderness, and consider the hand of God on him. Is it nothing to you that God should kill your child in anger, ruin your estate in anger, break your bones in anger, let you become a scandal and a reproach in anger, kill you, destroy you, make you lie down in darkness, in anger? Is it nothing that he should punish, ruin, and undo others for your sake? Do not mistake me. I do not mean that God always sends all these things on his own in anger; God forbid! But this I say: when he does deal with you in this way, and your conscience bears witness with him to what your provocations have been, you will find his dealings full of bitterness to your soul. If you do not fear these things, I fear you are under hardness.

(3.) Loss of peace and strength all a man’s days. To have peace with God, and to have strength to walk before God, is the sum of the great promises of the covenant of grace. In these things lies the life of our souls. Without them in some measure of comfort, to live is to die. What good will our lives do us if we do not sometimes see the face of God in peace, if we do not have some strength to walk with him? Now an unmortified lust will certainly rob men’s souls of both of these. The case is so plain in David that nothing could be clearer. How often he complains on this account that his bones were broken, his soul disquieted, his wounds grievous! Take other instances: “I was enraged by his sinful greed, so I struck him and hid My face in anger” (Isa 57:17). What peace, I ask, does a soul have while God hides himself, or what strength while he strikes? “I will return to My place until they admit their guilt and seek My face” (Hos 5:15). He will leave them and hide his face; and what will become of their peace and strength? So if you have ever enjoyed peace with God, if his terrors have ever made you afraid, if you have ever had strength to walk with him, or have ever mourned in your prayer and been troubled because of your weakness, think of this danger that hangs over your head. Perhaps in only a little while you will see the face of God in peace no more. Perhaps by tomorrow you will not be able to pray, read, hear, or perform any duty with the least cheerfulness, life, or vigor. And perhaps you may never see a quiet hour as long as you live, but may carry broken bones about with you, full of pain and terror, all the days of your life. Yes, perhaps God will shoot his arrows at you and fill you with anguish and disquiet, with fears and perplexities. He may make you a terror and a horror to yourself and others, show you hell and wrath every moment, and frighten and scare you with sad thoughts of his hatred, so that your sore will run in the night season and your soul will refuse comfort. You may wish for death rather than life; yes, your soul may choose strangling. Consider this a little. Though God should not utterly destroy you, he might still cast you into this condition, where you will have vivid and living fears of your destruction. Accustom your heart to thoughts of this; let it know what is likely to be the outcome of its state. Do not leave this consideration until you have made your soul tremble within you.

(4.) There is the danger of eternal destruction. To handle this consideration properly, observe:

I. There is such a connection between continuing in sin and eternal destruction that, though God does resolve to deliver some from continuing in sin so that they may not be destroyed, he will deliver none from destruction who continue in sin. So as long as any man lies under the abiding power of sin, the threats of destruction and of everlasting separation from God are to be held out to him. So Heb 3:12; add to it Heb 10:38. This is the rule of God’s proceeding: if any man “turns away” from him, “shrinks back” through unbelief, “I will take no pleasure in him”; that is, God’s indignation will pursue him to destruction. So it is plainly in Gal 6:8.

II. The man who is so entangled under the power of any corruption, as described above, can have at that time no clear, prevailing evidence of his share in the covenant, by whose efficacy he might be delivered from the fear of destruction. So destruction from the Lord may rightly be a terror to him, and he may, and ought to, look on it as the end of his course and ways. “There is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus” (Rom 8:1). True; but who will have the comfort of this statement? Who may claim it for himself? “Those who walk according to the Spirit, and not according to the flesh.” But you+ will say, “Is this not persuading men to unbelief?” I answer, no. There are two judgments a man may make of himself: first, of his person; and, secondly, of his ways. It is the judgment of his ways, not of his person, that I am speaking of. Let a man get the best evidence he can for his person; still it is his duty to judge that an evil way will end in destruction. Not to do so is atheism. I do not say that in such a condition a man ought to throw away the evidences of his personal share in Christ. But I do say that he cannot keep them. There are two ways a man may condemn himself:

[1.] First, with regard to desert, when the soul concludes that it deserves to be cast out of the presence of God. This is so far from being a matter of unbelief that it is an effect of faith.

[2.] Secondly, with regard to the outcome and end, when the soul concludes that it will be damned. I do not say this is anyone’s duty, nor do I call anyone to it. But this I say: a man ought to conclude that the end of the way he is in is death, so that he may be stirred up to flee from it. And this is another consideration that ought to dwell on such a soul, if it wants to be freed from the entanglement of its lusts.

(3) Consider its evils I mean its present evils. Danger looks to what is to come; evil, to what is present. Some of the many evils that come with an unmortified lust may be mentioned:

(1.) It grieves the holy and blessed Spirit, who is given to believers to dwell in them and abide with them. So the apostle, urging them away from many lusts and sins (Eph 4:25–29), gives this as the great motive for it in verse 30: “Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, in whom you+ were sealed for the day of redemption.” Do not grieve that Spirit of God, he says, by whom you+ receive so many and such great benefits. He gives one outstanding and comprehensive instance of them: “sealed for the day of redemption.” He is grieved by it. A tender and loving friend is grieved at the unkindness of a friend he has done much for. So it is with this tender and loving Spirit, who has chosen our hearts as a home to dwell in, and there to do for us all that our souls desire. He is grieved when we harbor his enemies, the very ones he is to destroy, in our hearts with him. “He does not willingly afflict or grieve us” (Lam 3:33); and shall we grieve him daily? This is why he is sometimes said to be “vexed,” and sometimes “grieved in His heart,” to express the deepest sense of our provocation. Now, if there is anything of gracious nobleness of spirit left in the soul, if it is not utterly hardened by the deceitfulness of sin, this consideration will certainly affect it. Consider who and what you are; who the Spirit is who is grieved, what he has done for you, what he comes to your soul about, what he has already done in you; and be ashamed. Among those who walk with God there is no greater motive and spur to holiness in all things, and to keeping their hearts and spirits in all purity and cleanness, than this: the blessed Spirit has undertaken to dwell in them as temples of God, and to keep them fit for him who dwells in them in this way. He is continually considering what they make welcome in their hearts, and he rejoices when his temple is kept undefiled. It made the sin of Zimri far worse that he brought his adulteress into the congregation in the sight of Moses and the rest, who were weeping for the sins of the people (Num 25:6). And is it not far worse to favor a lust, or to allow it to stay in the heart, when it is harbored (as it must be, if we are believers) under the special eye and view of the Holy Spirit, who takes care to keep his tabernacle pure and holy?

(2.) The Lord Jesus is wounded afresh by it. His new creature in the heart is wounded; his love is thwarted; his adversary is gratified. A total abandoning of him through the deceitfulness of sin is “crucifying Him all over again and subjecting Him to open shame.” In the same way, every harboring of the sin he came to destroy wounds and grieves him.

(3.) It will take away a man’s usefulness in his generation. His works, his efforts, and his labors seldom receive blessing from God. If he is a preacher, God commonly blights his ministry, so that he labors in the fire and is not honored with any success or with doing any work for God. The same may be said of other callings. The world today is full of poor, withering professing Christians. How few there are who walk in any beauty or glory! How barren, how useless they are for the most part! Among the many reasons that may be given for this sad state, it may rightly be feared that this is not the least effectual one: many men harbor spirit-devouring lusts in their bosoms, which lie like worms at the root of their obedience and eat it away and weaken it day by day. All graces, and all the ways and means by which any graces may be exercised and increased, are harmed by this. And as for any success, God blights such men’s undertakings.

This, then, is my second direction, and it concerns the opposition to be made to lust as it habitually lives in the soul: keep these considerations, or ones like them, of its guilt, danger, and evil alive on your heart. Meditate much on these things. Make your heart dwell and stay on them. Bind your thoughts to these considerations. Do not let them go off or wander from them until they begin to have a powerful influence on your soul, until they make it tremble.

Third Direction: Load the Conscience with Its Guilt

This is my third direction:

Load your conscience with the guilt of it. Do not only consider that it has guilt, but load your conscience with the guilt of its actual outbreaks and disturbances.

To make the right use of this rule, I will give some particular directions.

1. First, follow God’s method in it: begin with generals, and so come down to particulars.

(1) Charge your conscience with the guilt that appears in this sin from the uprightness and holiness of the law. Bring God’s holy law into your conscience, lay your corruption beside it, and pray that you may be affected by it. Consider the holiness of the law, its spirituality, its fiery severity, its inwardness, its absoluteness, and see how you can stand before it. Be much, I say, in affecting your conscience with the terror of the Lord in the law, and with how righteous it is that every one of your transgressions should receive a just recompense. Perhaps your conscience will invent excuses and evasions to ward off the force of this consideration. It may say that the condemning power of the law does not belong to you, that you are set free from it, and the like; and so, though you do not conform to it, you need not be so much troubled by it. But:

[1.] Tell your conscience that it cannot produce any evidence worth anything that you are free from the condemning power of sin while your unmortified lust lies in your heart. Perhaps, then, the law may make good its claim against you to full dominion, and then you are a lost creature. So it is best to weigh to the utmost what it has to say.

Assuredly, the man who pleads in the most secret reserve of his heart that he is freed from the condemning power of the law, so as secretly to excuse himself in giving the least allowance to any sin or lust, cannot produce on gospel grounds any evidence that gives tolerable spiritual assurance that he is in fact rightly freed from what he claims to be delivered from.

[2.] Whatever the outcome, the law has a commission from God to seize transgressors wherever it finds them, and to bring them before his throne, where they must plead for themselves. This is your case now: the law has found you out, and it will bring you before God. If you can plead a pardon, well and good; if not, the law will do its work.

[3.] In any case, this is the proper work of the law: to uncover sin in its guilt, to awaken and humble the soul for it, to be a mirror that shows sin in its true colors. If you refuse to deal with the law on this account, it is not from faith, but from the hardness of your heart and the deceitfulness of sin.

This is a door through which too many professing Christians have gone out into open apostasy. They claimed such a deliverance from the law that they would no longer consult its guidance and direction; they would no longer measure their sin by it. Little by little, without their noticing, this principle passed from a notion in their heads to influence their practical judgment. Once it had taken possession there, it turned the will and the affections loose to every kind of abomination.

By such ways as these, then, I say, persuade your conscience to listen carefully to what the law says to you, in the name of the Lord, about your lust and corruption. Oh, if your ears are open, it will speak with a voice that will make you tremble, cast you to the ground, and fill you with dismay. If you ever mean to mortify your corruptions, you must tie your conscience to the law and shut it off from all evasions and excuses, until it acknowledges its guilt with a clear and thorough understanding; so that, as David says, your iniquity may be “always before” you.

(2) Bring your lust to the gospel, not for relief, but for further conviction of its guilt. Look on him whom you have pierced, and be in bitterness. Say to your soul: “What have I done? What love, what mercy, what blood, what grace I have despised and trampled on! Is this how I repay the Father for his love, the Son for his blood, the Holy Spirit for his grace? Is this how I repay the Lord? Have I defiled the heart that Christ died to wash, that the blessed Spirit has chosen to dwell in? And can I keep myself out of the dust? What can I say to the dear Lord Jesus? How shall I lift up my head with any boldness before him? Do I value communion with him so little that, for the sake of this vile lust, I have left him hardly any room in my heart? How shall I escape if I neglect such a great salvation? Meanwhile, what shall I say to the Lord? Love, mercy, grace, goodness, peace, joy, consolation: I have despised them all and counted them as nothing, so that I might harbor a lust in my heart.

“Have I been given a sight of God’s fatherly face, so that I might look on his face and provoke him to his face? Was my soul washed so that room might be made for new defilements? Shall I try to defeat the purpose of the death of Christ? Shall I daily grieve the Spirit by whom I am sealed for the day of redemption?” Keep your conscience occupied with this plea every day. See if it can stand before this added weight of its guilt. If this does not make it sink and melt in some measure, I fear your case is dangerous.

2. Secondly, come down to particulars. Under the general head of the gospel all its benefits are to be considered, such as redemption, justification, and the like. So in particular, consider how the love in them has been brought home to your own soul, to add weight to the guilt of your corruption. For example:

(1.) Consider the infinite patience and forbearance of God toward you in particular. Consider what advantages he might have taken against you, to make you a shame and a reproach in this world and an object of wrath forever. Consider how you have dealt treacherously and falsely with him time after time: you have flattered him with your lips, but broken all your promises and pledges, and that by means of the very sin you are now pursuing. Yet he has spared you time after time, although you seem to have boldly put it to the test how long he could hold out. And will you still sin against him? Will you still weary him, and burden him with your corruptions?

Have you not often been ready to conclude that it was utterly impossible for him to bear with you any longer; that he would cast you off and be gracious no more; that all his forbearance was used up, and hell and wrath were even now prepared for you? And yet, beyond all your expectation, he has returned with visits of love. And will you still go on provoking the eyes of his glory?

(2.) How often have you been at the very door of being hardened by the deceitfulness of sin, and by the infinitely rich grace of God been brought back to communion with him again?

Have you not found grace decaying; delight in duties, ordinances, prayer and meditation vanishing; inclinations to loose, careless living thriving; and those who before were entangled almost beyond recovery? Have you not found yourself caught up, and that with delight, in ways, societies, and companions that God abhors? And will you venture to the brink of hardness any more?

(3.) All God’s gracious dealings with you in his providence (deliverances, afflictions, mercies, enjoyments) should all have their place here. By these, I say, and similar means, load your conscience. Do not leave it until it is thoroughly affected with the guilt of your indwelling corruption, until it feels its wound and lies in the dust before the Lord. Unless this is done effectually, all other efforts are in vain. As long as the conscience has any means to lighten the guilt of sin, the soul will never vigorously attempt its mortification.

Fourth Direction: A Constant Longing for Deliverance

4. Fourth, being thus affected with your sin, next get a constant longing and panting after deliverance from its power. Do not let your heart be content for one moment with its present frame and condition. In natural and civil things, longing desires for anything are of no value or account except as far as they stir up the person who has them to a diligent use of means to bring about what he aims at. In spiritual things it is otherwise. Longing, yearning, and panting after deliverance is a grace in itself, and it has a mighty power to conform the soul to the likeness of the thing longed for. So the apostle, describing the repentance and godly sorrow of the Corinthians, counts this as one eminent grace then set to work: “what longing” (2 Cor 7:11). And in this case of indwelling sin and its power, what frame does he say he is in? (Rom 7:24). His heart breaks out with longing into a most passionate expression of desire for deliverance. Now, if this is the frame of the saints when they consider indwelling sin in general, how much more must it be heightened and increased when the troubling rage and power of some particular lust and corruption is added to it! Be sure of this: unless you long for deliverance, you will not have it.

This will make the heart watch for every chance to gain an advantage over its enemy, and ready to take hold of any help it is given to destroy it. Strong desires are the very life of the “praying at all times” that we are commanded in every condition, and in none is it more necessary than in this. They set faith and hope to work; they are the soul’s reaching out after the Lord.

So get your heart into a panting, longing frame: long for it, sigh for it, cry out for it. You+ know David’s example; I need not dwell on it.

Fifth Direction: Consider Whether It Is Rooted in the Nature

The fifth direction is this:

5. Fifth, consider whether the disorder that troubles you is not rooted in your nature, and cherished, inflamed, and heightened by your constitution. A tendency to some sins may no doubt lie in the natural temperament and disposition of men. In this case consider:

1. This does not in the least lessen the guilt of your sin.

Some, with open profaneness, will blame gross enormities on their temperament and disposition; and whether others do not also relieve themselves of the pressing guilt of their disorders by the same consideration, I do not know. It is from the fall, from the original corruption of our natures, that the fomes [tinder] and nourishment of any sin lives on in our natural temperament. David counts his being brought forth in iniquity and conceived in sin (Ps 51:5) as making the sin that followed worse, not a lessening or excuse of it. That you are especially inclined to some sinful disorder is only a special outbreak of original lust in your nature, which should especially abase and humble you.

2. What you must fix on here, as regards your walk with God, is this: so great an advantage is given to sin, and to Satan too, by your temperament and disposition, that without extraordinary watchfulness, care, and diligence they will certainly prevail against your soul. Thousands have been hurried headlong to hell on this account who otherwise might at least have gone at a gentler, less provoking, less harmful pace.

3. For the mortification of any disorder so rooted in a man’s nature, besides all the other ways and means already named or still to be discussed, there is one remedy especially suited to it. It is that of the apostle: “I discipline my body and bring it into subjection” (1 Cor 9:27). Bringing the body itself into subjection is an ordinance of God that tends to the mortification of sin. It checks the natural root of the disorder and withers it by taking away the richness of its soil. The Papists, men ignorant of the righteousness of Christ, of the work of his Spirit, and of the whole business in hand, have laid the whole weight and stress of mortification on voluntary services and penances that lead to the subjection of the body, knowing the true nature neither of sin nor of mortification. Perhaps because of this, it may on the other side be a temptation to some to neglect means of humbling that God himself approves and has appointed. Bringing the body into subjection in the case under discussion, by cutting short the natural appetite, by fasting, by keeping vigils, and the like, is no doubt acceptable to God, provided it is done within the following limits:

(1) The outward weakening and wearing down of the body must not be looked on as a good thing in itself, or as if any mortification consisted in it; that would bring us back under fleshly ordinances. It is only a means to the end proposed: the weakening of a disorder in its natural root and seat. A man may have a lean body and a lean soul together.

(2) The means by which this is done, fasting, keeping vigils, and the like, must not be looked on as things that can produce true mortification of any sin in themselves and by their own power. If they could, sin might be mortified in any unregenerate person in the world without any help of the Spirit. They are to be looked on only as ways by which the Spirit may, and sometimes does, put forth his strength to accomplish his own work, especially in the case mentioned. A lack of right understanding and due use of these and similar considerations has produced among the Papists a mortification better suited to horses and other beasts of the field than to believers.

This is the sum of what has been said: when the disorder we complain of seems to be rooted in our natural temperament and constitution, then, while we apply our souls to share in the blood and Spirit of Christ, we must also try to check the natural root of that disorder in God’s way.

Sixth Direction: Watch Against Its Occasions and Advantages

6. Sixth, consider what occasions and what advantages your disorder has taken to exert and show itself, and watch against them all. This is one part of the duty our blessed Savior commends to his disciples under the name of watching: “What I say to you+, I say to everyone: Keep watch!” (Mark 13:37). In Luke 21:34 it is, “Watch yourselves+, or your+ hearts will be weighed down.” Watch against every outbreak of your corruptions. I mean the duty David professed himself to be exercised in: “I have kept myself from my iniquity,” he says. He watched all the ways and workings of his iniquity, to head them off and to rise up against them. This is what we are called to under the name of “considering our ways.” Consider what ways, what companions, what opportunities, what studies, what business, what conditions have at any time given, or usually give, advantages to your disorders, and set yourself carefully against them all. Men will do this with their bodily weaknesses and disorders. The seasons, the diet, the air that have proved harmful will be avoided. Are the things of the soul of less importance? Know that the man who dares to toy with occasions of sin will dare to sin. He who will venture on temptations to wickedness will venture on wickedness. Hazael thought he would never be as wicked as the prophet told him he would be. To convince him, the prophet tells him no more than this: “You will be king over Aram.” If he will venture on temptations to cruelty, he will be cruel. Tell a man he will commit such and such sins, and he will be startled at it. If you can convince him that he will venture on the occasions and temptations to them, he will have little ground left for his confidence. There are many particular directions belonging to this head, which I will not go into now. But because this head is of no less importance than the whole doctrine treated here, I have dealt with it at length in another treatise, on entering into temptation.

Seventh Direction: Rise Mightily Against Its First Stirrings

7. Seventh, rise up mightily against the first stirrings of your disorder, its first conceptions; do not let it gain the least ground. Do not say, “This far it shall go, and no farther.” If it is allowed one step, it will take another. It is impossible to set bounds to sin. It is like water in a channel: if it once breaks out, it will have its course. It is easier to keep it from acting at all than to keep it within bounds. That is why James sets out the steps and progress of lust (ch. 1:14, 15), so that we may stop it at the entrance. Do you find your corruption beginning to entangle your thoughts? Rise up against it with all your strength, with no less indignation than if it had fully accomplished what it aims at. Consider what an unclean thought would have: it would have you roll yourself in folly and filth. Ask envy what it would have: murder and destruction are at the end of it. Set yourself against it with no less vigor than if it had utterly debased you into wickedness. Without this course you will not prevail. As sin gains ground in the affections, so that they delight in it, it gains ground also on the understanding, so that the understanding makes light of it.

Chapter XII

The eighth direction. Thinking much on the excellency of the majesty of God. How little we are acquainted with him, set out and considered.

Eighth Direction: Meditations That Fill the Soul with Self-Abasement

Eighth, use and exercise yourself in such meditations as may serve to fill you at all times with self-abasement and thoughts of your own vileness. For example:

1. Think much on the excellency of the majesty of God and your infinite, inconceivable distance from him. Many thoughts of it cannot help but fill you with a sense of your own vileness, and that strikes deep at the root of any indwelling sin. When Job comes to a clear sight of the greatness and excellency of God, he is filled with self-abhorrence and pressed down to humble himself (Job 42:5, 6). And into what state does the prophet Habakkuk say he was cast when he grasped the majesty of God? (ch. 3:16). “Awesome majesty surrounds Him,” says Job (Job 37:22). This is why the people of old thought that when they had seen God they would die. The Scripture is full of this self-abasing consideration. In respect of God it compares the men of the earth to “grasshoppers,” to “emptiness,” to “a speck of dust on the scales” (Isa 40:12–25). Be much in thoughts of this kind, to bring down the pride of your heart and to keep your soul humble within you. Nothing will make you less open to being taken in by the deceits of sin than such a frame of heart. Think greatly of the greatness of God.

2. Think much of how unacquainted you are with him. Though you know enough to keep you low and humble, how small a portion of him it is that you know! The thought of this cast that wise man into the view of himself which he expresses in Prov 30:2–4: “Surely I am the most brutish of men, and I lack the understanding of a man. I have not learned wisdom, and I have no knowledge of the Holy One. Who has ascended to heaven and come down? Who has gathered the wind in His hands? Who has bound up the waters in His cloak? Who has established all the ends of the earth? What is His name, and what is the name of His Son—tell me, if you know!” Labor with this too, to bring down the pride of your heart. What do you know of God? How small a portion it is! How immense he is in his nature! Can you look into the abyss of eternity without terror? You cannot bear the rays of his glorious being.

Because I regard this consideration as of great use in our walk with God, as far as it can be consistent (as it can be) with the boldness of sons toward a father that is given us in Jesus Christ to draw near to the throne of grace, I will dwell on it further, to leave a lasting impression of it on the souls of those who desire to walk humbly with God.

Consider, then, I say, to keep your heart in continual awe of the majesty of God, that those of the highest and most eminent attainments, of the nearest and most familiar communion with God, still know in this life only very little of him and his glory. God reveals his name to Moses, the most glorious attributes he has shown in the covenant of grace (Exod 34:5, 6); yet all of it is only God’s “back.” All that Moses knows by it is little and low compared with the perfection of God’s glory.

That is why it is said with special reference to Moses, “No one has ever seen God” (John 1:18). He speaks of Moses in comparison with Christ in verse 17, and of him it is said here, “No one,” not even Moses, the most eminent among them, “has ever seen God.” We speak much of God; we can talk of him, his ways, his works, his counsels, all day long. The truth is, we know very little of him. Our thoughts, our meditations, our expressions of him are low, many of them unworthy of his glory, none of them reaching his perfections.

You+ will say that Moses was under the law, when God wrapped himself in darkness, and his mind in types and clouds and dark ordinances. But under the glorious shining of the gospel, which has brought life and immortality to light, God has been revealed from his own bosom. So we now know him much more clearly, and as he is; we now see his face, and not only his back, as Moses did.

Ans. 1. I acknowledge a vast and almost inconceivable difference between the acquaintance we now have with God, since he has spoken to us by his own Son,10 and that which the saints in general had under the law. Their eyes were as good, sharp, and clear as ours, their faith and spiritual understanding not behind ours, and the object was as glorious to them as to us. Yet our day is clearer than theirs was: the clouds are blown away and scattered,11 the shadows of the night are gone and fled, the sun is risen, and the means of sight are made more excellent and clear than before. Yet:

(2.) The particular sight Moses had of God in Exod 34 was a gospel sight, a sight of God as “gracious,” and so on; and yet it is called only his “back,” that is, low and poor compared with his excellencies and perfections.

(3.) The apostle exalts to the utmost this glory of gospel light above that of the law, and shows that now the “veil” that caused darkness is taken away, so that with “unveiled” or uncovered “faces12 we all behold the glory of the Lord.” Yet he tells us how: “as in a mirror” (2 Cor 3:18). “In a mirror”: how is that? Clearly, perfectly? Alas, no! He tells you how it is in 1 Cor 13:12: “Now we see but a dim reflection as in a mirror,” he says. The apostle is not speaking of a telescope that helps us see things far off; and yet even such helps, how poor they are! How far we fall short of the truth of things, for all their help! It is a looking glass he alludes to (where there are only dim likenesses and images of things, not the things themselves), and he compares our knowledge to the sight we have in it. He also tells us that all we do see, δι’ ἐσόπτρου, “by” or “through this mirror,” is in αἰνίγματι, “in a riddle,” in darkness and obscurity. And speaking of himself, who was surely far more clear-sighted than anyone now living, he tells us that he saw only ἐκ μέρους, “in part.” He saw only the back of heavenly things (verse 12), and he compares all the knowledge of God he had attained to the knowledge he had of things when he was a child (verse 11). It is a μέρος [part], short of τὸ τέλειον [the perfect]; indeed, it is such as καταργηθήσεται, “it will be destroyed,” or done away. We know what weak, feeble, uncertain notions and ideas children have of anything hard to grasp; and how, when they grow up with some improvement in their gifts and abilities, those ideas vanish and they are ashamed of them. It is to a child’s credit that he loves, honors, believes, and obeys his father; but as for his knowledge and notions, his father knows their childishness and folly. For all our confidence in high attainments, all our notions of God are only childish compared with his infinite perfections. We lisp and babble, and for the most part say we know not what, in what we think are our most accurate conceptions and notions of God. We may love, honor, believe, and obey our Father, and with that he accepts our childish thoughts, for they are only childish. We see only his back; we know only a little of him. Hence comes the promise that so often supports and comforts us in our distress: “We will see Him as He is”; we will see him “face to face”; we will “know fully, even as” we are “fully known,” and “take hold of that for which” we were taken hold of (1 Cor 13:12; 1 John 3:2). And Scripture says expressly: “Now we do not see Him.” All of this shows that here we see only his back; not as he is, but in a dark, obscure representation; not in the perfection of his glory.

The queen of Sheba had heard much of Solomon, and had formed many great thoughts of his magnificence in her mind from it; but when she came and saw his glory, she had to confess that not even half the truth had been told her. We may suppose that here we have attained great knowledge, clear and high thoughts of God; but alas! when he brings us into his presence we will cry out, “We never knew him as he is; not a thousandth part of his glory, perfection, and blessedness ever entered our hearts.”

The apostle tells us in 1 John 3:2 that we do not know what we ourselves will be, what we will find ourselves to be in the end; much less will it enter our hearts to conceive what God is, and what we will find him to be. Consider either him who is to be known, or the way by which we know him, and this will appear further.

We Know So Little of God

1. First, we know so little of God because it is God who is to be known; that is, he who has described himself to us very much by this, that we cannot know him. What else does he mean when he calls himself invisible, incomprehensible, and the like? That is, he is one whom we do not and cannot know as he is. And our further progress consists more in knowing what he is not than what he is. So he is described as immortal, infinite; that is, he is not mortal, finite, and limited, as we are. Hence comes that glorious description of him in 1 Tim 6:16: “He alone is immortal and dwells in unapproachable light. No one has ever seen Him, nor can anyone see Him.” His light is such that no creature can approach it. He is not seen, not because he cannot be seen, but because we cannot bear the sight of him. The light of God, in whom there is no darkness, forbids all access to him by any creature whatever. We who cannot look at the sun in its glory are far too weak to bear the beams of infinite brightness.

On this account, as was said, the wise man professes himself “the most brutish of men,” no better than a beast, and says he lacks “the understanding of a man” (Prov 30:2). That is, he knew nothing compared with God, so that he seemed to have lost all his understanding once he came to consider him, his work, and his ways. Let our souls come down in this consideration to some particulars.

(1.) As for the being of God: we are so far from knowing it well enough to teach one another about it in words and expressions, that to form any conception of it in our minds, with such likenesses and impressions of things as we receive the knowledge of all other things by, is to make an idol for ourselves, and so to worship a god of our own making, and not the God who made us. We may as well and as lawfully carve him out of wood or stone as form a being for him in our minds, suited to our understanding.

The utmost of our best thoughts of the being of God is that we can have no thoughts of it. Our knowledge of a being is low indeed when it rises no higher than to know that we do not know it.

(2.) There are some things of God which he himself has taught us to speak of, and has given us rules for our expressions of them. But when we have done so, we do not see the things themselves; we do not know them. To believe and wonder is all we attain to. We profess, as we are taught, that God is infinite, omnipotent, eternal; and we know what disputes and notions there are about omnipresence, immensity, infiniteness, and eternity. We have, I say, words and notions about these things; but as for the things themselves, what do we know? What do we grasp of them? Can the mind of man do anything more than swallow itself up in an infinite abyss, which is as nothing, and give itself up to what it cannot conceive, much less express? Is not our understanding like a beast’s in the contemplation of such things, as if it were not there at all? Indeed, the perfection of our understanding is not to understand, and to rest there. It is only the back of eternity and infiniteness that we catch a glimpse of. What shall I say of the Trinity, the subsistence of distinct persons in the same individual essence? It is a mystery denied by many because understood by none, a mystery whose every letter is mysterious.

Who can declare the generation of the Son, the procession of the Spirit, or the difference between the one and the other? But I will not give further instances. The infinite and inconceivable distance between him and us keeps us in the dark as to any sight of his face or clear understanding of his perfections.

We know him by what he does rather than by what he is, by his doing us good rather than by his essential goodness; and, as Job says, how small a portion of him is revealed by this!

2. Secondly, we know little of God because it is by faith alone that we know him here. I will not now discuss the impressions that remain by nature on the hearts of all men that there is a God, nor what they may rationally be taught about that God from the works of his creation and providence, which they see and behold. It is admitted, and that from the sad experience of all ages, that this knowledge is so weak, low, dark, and confused that none ever glorified God as they ought on that account. Instead, for all their knowledge of God, they were in fact “without God in the world.”

The chief, and practically the only, acquaintance we have with God and the ways he makes himself known is by faith. “Anyone who comes to Him must believe that He exists and that He rewards those who earnestly seek Him” (Heb 11:6). Our knowledge of him and of his rewarding (the foundation of our obedience, or our coming to him) is believing. “We walk by faith, not by sight” (2 Cor 5:7): διὰ πίστεως οὐ δι’ εἴδους, by faith, and so by faith as not to have any distinct idea, image, or likeness of what we believe. Faith is all the evidence we have of “what we do not see” (Heb 11:1). I might dwell here on its nature, and show from all that goes with it and concerns it that we know only the back of what we know by faith alone. As to its origin, it is built purely on the testimony of him whom we have not seen; as the apostle says, “How can you+ love him whom you+ have not seen?” That is, he is one whom you+ know to exist only by faith. Faith receives everything on his testimony, and accepts that he exists on his testimony alone. As to its nature, it is assent upon testimony, not evidence upon demonstration; and its object is, as was said before, above us. That is why our faith, as was noted before, is called seeing “a dim reflection as in a mirror.” All that we know this way (and all that we know of God we know this way) is only low, dark, and obscure.

But you+ will say: “All this is true, but perhaps it is true only of those who do not know God as he is revealed in Jesus Christ. With those who do, it is otherwise. It is true that ‘no one has ever seen God,’ but ‘the one and only Son … has made Him known’ (John 1:17, 18); and ‘the Son of God has come and has given us understanding, so that we may know Him who is true’ (1 John 5:20).

“The light of ‘the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God,’ shines on believers (2 Cor 4:4). Indeed, ‘God, who said, “Let light shine out of darkness,” made His light shine in our hearts to give us the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ’ (verse 6). So though ‘we were once darkness,’ we are now ‘light in the Lord’ (Eph 5:8). And the apostle says, ‘We … with unveiled faces all behold the glory of the Lord’ (2 Cor 3:18). We are now so far from being in such darkness, or at such a distance from God, that our ‘fellowship … is with the Father and with His Son’ (1 John 1:3). The light of the gospel by which God is now revealed is glorious; not a star, but the sun in his beauty has risen on us, and the veil is taken from our faces. So though unbelievers, and perhaps some weak believers, may be in some darkness, those of any growth or considerable attainments have a clear sight and view of the face of God in Jesus Christ.”

Ans. 1. The truth is, we all know enough of him to love him more than we do, to delight in him, serve him, believe him, obey him, and trust in him beyond anything we have yet attained.

Our darkness and weakness are no excuse for our negligence and disobedience. Who has ever lived up to the knowledge he has had of the perfections, excellencies, and will of God? God’s purpose in giving us any knowledge of himself here is that we may “glorify Him as God”: that is, love him, serve him, believe and obey him, and give him all the honor and glory that poor sinful creatures owe to a sin-pardoning God and Creator. We must all acknowledge that we have never been thoroughly transformed into the image of the knowledge we have had. And had we used our talents well, we might have been trusted with more.

(2.) Comparatively, the knowledge we have of God by the revelation of Jesus Christ in the gospel is exceedingly eminent and glorious. It is so in comparison with any knowledge of God that might be attained otherwise, or that was delivered in the law under the Old Testament, which had only the shadow of good things, not their express image. The apostle pursues this at length in 2 Cor 3. In these last days Christ has revealed the Father from his own bosom, declared his name, and made known his mind, will, and counsel in a far clearer, more eminent, more distinct way than he did before, while he kept his people under the schooling of the law. And this, for the most part, is what the passages mentioned above mean. The clear, plain delivery and declaration of God and his will in the gospel is expressly exalted above every other way in which he has revealed himself.

(3.) The difference between believers and unbelievers in knowledge lies not so much in the matter of their knowledge as in the manner of knowing. Some unbelievers may know more, and be able to say more, of God, his perfections, and his will than many believers. But they know nothing as they ought, nothing in the right way, nothing spiritually and savingly, nothing with a holy, heavenly light. The excellency of a believer is not that he has a wide grasp of things, but that what he does grasp, which may perhaps be very little, he sees in the light of the Spirit of God, in a saving, soul-transforming light. And this is what gives us communion with God, not prying speculation or elaborately built notions.

(4.) By his word and Spirit, Jesus Christ reveals God to the hearts of all his people as a Father, as a God in covenant, as a rewarder, in every way enough to teach us to obey him here and to lead us to his bosom, to lie down there in the enjoyment of him for eternity. But still,

(5.) For all this, it is only a little portion of him that we know; we see only his back. For:

(1) The aim of all gospel revelation is not to unveil God’s essential glory so that we should see him as he is. It is only to declare as much of him as he knows to be enough as a foundation for our faith, love, obedience, and coming to him; that is, for the faith he expects from us here, and such service as befits poor creatures in the midst of temptations. But when he calls us to eternal wonder and contemplation, without interruption, he will make a new kind of revelation of himself, and the whole shape of things as it now lies before us will pass away like a shadow.

(2) We are dull and slow of heart to receive what is revealed in the word. God, through our weakness and infirmity, keeps us in continual dependence on him for teaching and for revelations of himself from his word, and never in this world brings any soul to the utmost of what can be drawn out of the word and discovered in it. So although the way of revelation in the gospel is clear and evident, we know little of the things themselves that are revealed.

Let us, then, return to the use and purpose of this consideration. Will not a right sense of this inconceivable greatness of God, and of the infinite distance at which we stand from him, fill the soul with a holy and reverent fear of him, and so keep it in a frame unsuited to the thriving or flourishing of any lust whatever? Let the soul be continually accustomed to reverent thoughts of God’s greatness and omnipresence, and it will be very much on its guard against any unfitting conduct. Consider him with whom you+ have to do: “our God is a consuming fire.” And in your+ deepest abasement at his presence and his eye, know that your+ very nature is too narrow to bear thoughts fit for his essential glory.

Chapter XIII

The ninth direction. When the heart is troubled by sin, speak no peace to it until God speaks it. Peace without detestation of sin is unsound; so is peace measured out to ourselves. How we may know when we measure out our peace to ourselves. Directions for that inquiry. The futility of speaking peace slightly. Also of doing it on one single account, not on all.

Ninth Direction: Speak No Peace Until God Speaks It

If God troubles the heart about the guilt of its sinful disorders, whether in respect of their root and indwelling or of any outbreaks of them, take care that you do not speak peace to yourself before God speaks it; but listen to what he says to your soul. This is our next direction. Without observing it, the heart will be greatly exposed to the deceitfulness of sin.

This is a matter of great importance. It is a sad thing for a man to deceive his own soul in this. All the warnings God gives us, in his tenderness to our souls, to test and examine ourselves, tend to prevent this great evil of speaking peace to ourselves without ground; for in the end that is to bless ourselves in opposition to God. It is not my business to dwell on the danger of it, but to help believers to avoid it, and to let them know when they are doing it.

To follow this direction rightly, observe:

(1) It is the great prerogative and sovereignty of God to give grace to whom he pleases (“He has mercy on whom He wants to have mercy,” Rom 9:18; and among all the sons of men he calls whom he will and sanctifies whom he will). In the same way, among those who are called and justified, and whom he will save, he still reserves this privilege to himself: to speak peace to whom he pleases, and in what degree he pleases, even among those on whom he has bestowed grace. He is “the God of all comfort” in a special way in his dealings with believers; that is, of the good things that he keeps locked up in his family and gives out to all his children at his pleasure. The Lord dwells on this in Isa 57:16–18. The very case we are considering is the one dealt with there. When God says he will heal their breaches and their sorrows, he claims this privilege for himself in a special way: “I create it,” verse 19. That is: “Even toward these poor wounded creatures I create it, and by my sovereignty I give it out as I please.”

So it is with God’s giving of grace to those who are in the state of nature: he does it with great exactness, and his ways in taking some and leaving others, as far as outward appearances go, are often quite beyond and even contrary to all likely expectation. It is the same with his imparting of peace and joy to those who are in the state of grace: he often gives them out quite beyond our expectations, as far as any visible grounds for his dealings are concerned.

(2) As God creates peace for whom he pleases, so it is the prerogative of Christ to speak it home to the conscience. When he speaks to the church of Laodicea, which had healed her wounds falsely and spoken peace to herself when she should not have, he takes this title to himself: “the Amen, the faithful and true Witness” (Rev 3:14). He bears witness to our condition as it really is. We may be mistaken, and trouble ourselves for nothing, or flatter ourselves on false grounds. But he is “the Amen, the faithful and true Witness”; and what he says of our state and condition is how it really is. In Isa 11:3 it is said that he “will not judge by what His eyes see,” that is, not by any outward appearance, or anything open to mistake, as we tend to judge. He will judge and settle every case as it really is.

Take these two preliminary observations, and I will give some rules by which men may know whether God is speaking peace to them, or whether they are only speaking peace to themselves.

(1) Men certainly speak peace to themselves when it is not accompanied by the greatest detestation imaginable of the sin they are speaking peace about, and by abhorrence of themselves for it. Suppose men are wounded by sin, troubled and perplexed. They know there is no remedy for them except in the mercies of God through the blood of Christ, and so they look to him and to the promises of the covenant in him. On that basis they quiet their hearts, telling themselves that it will be well with them, and that God will be exalted so that he may be gracious to them. Yet their souls have not been brought to the greatest detestation of the sin or sins that troubled them. This is to heal themselves, not to be healed by God. It is only a great and strong wind; the Lord is near it, but the Lord is not in the wind. When men truly look on Christ, “the One they have pierced,” without which there is no healing or peace, they will “mourn” (Zech 12:10). They will mourn for him, on this very account, and detest the sin that pierced him. When we go to Christ for healing, faith looks at him especially as one who was pierced. Faith takes several views of Christ, according to the occasions it has for coming to him and having communion with him. Sometimes it looks at his holiness, sometimes his power, sometimes his love, his favor with his Father. And when it goes for healing and peace, it looks especially at the blood of the covenant, at his sufferings; for “the punishment that brought us peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed” (Isa 53:5). When we look for healing, we must fix our eyes on his stripes, not on the outward story of them, which is the way of popish devotion, but on the love, kindness, mystery, and purpose of the cross. And when we look for peace, his punishment must be before our eyes. Now this, I say, if it is done according to the mind of God and in the strength of the Spirit who is poured out on believers, will produce a detestation of the sin or sins for which healing and peace are sought. So it is in Ezek 16:60, 61: “But I will remember the covenant I made with you in the days of your youth, and I will establish an everlasting covenant with you.” And what then? “Then you will remember your ways and be ashamed.” When God comes home to speak peace in a sure covenant, it fills the soul with shame for all the ways in which it has been estranged from him. And one of the things the apostle mentions as going with that godly sorrow which brings repentance leading to salvation, never to be regretted, is revenge: “what revenge!” (2 Cor 7:11). They looked back on their failures with indignation and revenge for their folly in them. When Job comes to a thorough healing, he cries, “I abhor myself” (Job 42:6); and until he did, he had no lasting peace. He might perhaps have patched himself up with the doctrine of free grace so excellently preached by Elihu (ch. 33, from verse 14 to the 29th); but then he would only have skinned over his wounds. He must come to self-abhorrence if he is to come to healing. So it was with those in Psalm 78:33, 35, in their great trouble and perplexity because of their sin. I do not doubt that when they came to God in Christ they spoke peace to themselves. That they did come to him in Christ is clear from the titles they gave him: they called him their Rock and their Redeemer, two words that everywhere point to the Lord Christ. But was their peace sound and lasting? No; it passed away like the early dew. God did not speak one word of peace to their souls. Why did they not have peace? Because in coming to God they flattered him. How does that appear? Verse 37: “Their hearts were disloyal to Him, and they were unfaithful to His covenant.” They had neither detested nor given up the sin about which they spoke peace to themselves. A man may seek healing and peace however he will. He may go to the true Physician, he may go the right way, he may quiet his heart in the promises of the covenant. Yet when peace is spoken, if it is not accompanied by detestation and abhorrence of the sin that was the wound and caused the trouble, this is no peace of God’s creating, but of our own purchasing. It is only a skinning over of the wound, while the core lies at the bottom. It will fester, and corrupt, and eat away, until it breaks out again with foulness, distress, and danger. There are poor souls who walk in such a path as this. They are more aware of the trouble of sin than of the pollution or uncleanness that goes with it. They seek mercy, and they seek it from the Lord in Christ, yet they will keep the sweet morsel of their sin under their tongue. Let them never think, I say, that they will have true and solid peace. For instance, you find your heart running out after the world, and it disturbs you in your communion with God. The Spirit speaks expressly to you: “If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him” (1 John 2:15). This drives you to deal with God in Christ for the healing of your soul and the quieting of your conscience. Yet a thorough detestation of the evil itself does not stay with you; perhaps you even like it well enough, and dislike it only for its consequences. Perhaps you may be saved, yet as through fire, and God will have some work with you before he is done. But you will have little peace in this life; you will be sick and fainting all your days (Isa 57:17). This is a deceit that lies at the root of the peace of many professing Christians and wastes it away. They wrestle with all their strength for mercy and pardon, and seem to have great communion with God in doing so. They lie before him and bewail their sins and follies, so that anyone would think, indeed they think so themselves, that surely they and their sins have now parted. And so they receive a mercy that satisfies their hearts for a little while. But when a thorough search comes to be made, there has been some secret reserve kept for the folly or follies they were dealing with; at the least, there has not been the thorough abhorrence of it that is necessary. And their whole peace is quickly found to be weak and rotten, hardly lasting longer than the words they used to beg for it are in their mouths.

(2) When men measure out peace to themselves on the conclusions their convictions and rational principles will carry them to, this is a false peace and will not last. I will explain a little what I mean. A man has been wounded by sin; he has a conviction of some sin on his conscience. He has not walked uprightly, as befits the gospel, and all is not well and right between God and his soul. He now considers what is to be done. He has light; he knows what path he must take, and how his soul was healed before. He considers that the promises of God are the outward means by which healing is applied to his sores and quiet to his heart. So he goes to them, searches them out, and finds one or more whose literal words directly suit his condition. He says to himself, “God speaks in this promise. Here I will take a dressing as long and as broad as my wound.” And so he applies the word of the promise to his condition and sits down in peace. This is another appearance on the mount: the Lord is near, but the Lord is not in it. It has not been the work of the Spirit, who alone can convict men “in regard to sin and righteousness and judgment,”13 but the mere workings of the intelligent, rational soul. We say there are three kinds of life: the vegetative, the sentient, and the rational or intelligent. Some things have only the vegetative. Some have the sentient as well, which includes the first. Some have the rational, which takes in and presupposes both the others. Now he who has the rational life does not act only in keeping with that principle, but also with both the others: he grows, and he feels. It is the same with men in the things of God. Some are merely natural and rational men. Some have, added to that, conviction with illumination. And some are truly regenerate. Now he who has the last has both the former as well, and so he acts sometimes on the principles of the rational man, sometimes on the principles of the enlightened man. His true spiritual life is not the source of all his stirrings. He does not always act in its strength, and not all his fruit comes from that root. In the case I am speaking of, he acts merely on the principle of conviction and illumination, by which his natural powers are heightened; but the Spirit does not breathe at all on these waters. Take an example. Suppose the wound and trouble of the soul come from relapses. Whatever the evil or folly may be, however small in itself, there are no wounds deeper than those given to the soul on that account, and no troubles greater. In the turmoil of his mind he finds the promise in Isa 55:7, that the Lord “will have compassion,” and our God “will abundantly pardon.” That is, he will multiply pardon, add to it, do it again and again. Or he finds the one in Hosea 14:4: “I will heal their backsliding; I will freely love them.” The man considers this and thereupon concludes peace for himself. Whether the Spirit of God makes the application or not, whether the Spirit gives life and power to the letter or not, the man does not care. He does not listen for whether God the Lord speaks peace. He does not wait on God. And God perhaps still hides his face, and sees the poor creature stealing peace and running away with it, knowing that the time will come when he will deal with him again and call him to a new reckoning (Hos 9:9), when the man will see that it is useless to go one step where God does not take him by the hand.

I see here, indeed, a number of other questions arising and pressing in. I cannot deal with them all; I will speak a little to one.

It may be said, then, “Since this seems to be the path the Holy Spirit leads us in for the healing of our wounds and the quieting of our hearts, how shall we know when we are going alone, and when the Spirit is also with us?”

Ans. 1. If any of you+ are off the path in this, God will soon let you+ know it. For besides having his promise that he “guides the humble in what is right and teaches them His way” (Ps 25:9), you+ will find he will not let you+ go on in error forever. He will not, I say, allow your+ nakedness to be covered with fig leaves. He will take them away, and all the peace you+ have in them, and will not let you+ settle on such dregs. You+ will soon know that your+ wound is not healed; that is, you+ will soon know by the outcome whether this is how it is with you+. The peace you+ get and obtain in this way will not last. While the mind is overpowered by its own convictions, troubles have nothing to fasten on. But wait a little, and all these reasonings will grow cold and vanish before the face of the first temptation that arises. But further:

(2.) This course is usually taken without waiting. Yet waiting is the grace, the particular exercise of faith, that God calls for in such a condition. I know God sometimes comes on the soul at once, in a moment as it were, wounding it and healing it. I am persuaded that this was so with David when he cut off the corner of Saul’s robe. But usually in such a case God calls for waiting and labor,14 attending on him as the eye of a servant on his master. The prophet Isaiah says (ch. 8:17), “I will wait for the Lord, who is hiding His face from the house of Jacob.” When his children have run from his house, God will have them lie a while at his door, and not rush in on him at once; unless he takes them by the hand and pulls them in, when they are so ashamed that they dare not come to him. Now self-healers, men who speak peace to themselves, usually make haste. They will not wait; they do not listen to what God says (Isa 28:16), but on they go to be healed.

(3.) Such a course may quiet the conscience and the mind, the reasoning part of the soul that draws conclusions; yet it does not sweeten the heart with rest and gracious contentment. The answer it receives is much like the one Elisha gave Naaman: “Go in peace” (2 Kgs 5:19). That quieted his mind; but I very much question whether it sweetened his heart, or gave him any joy in believing beyond the natural joy that was stirred in him by his healing. “Do not My words do good?” says the Lord (Mic 2:7). When God speaks, there is not only truth in his words, which may answer the conviction of our understanding; they also do good. They bring what is sweet and good and desirable to the will and the affections. By them the soul “returns to its rest” (Ps 116:7).

(4.) Worst of all, it does not amend the life. It does not heal the evil or cure the disorder. When God speaks peace, it guides and keeps the soul so that it does not “return to folly” (Ps 85:8). When we speak it ourselves, the heart is not drawn off from the evil. In fact, it is the quickest way in the world to bring a soul into a habit of backsliding. If, after dressing your own wound, you find yourself rather stirred up to the old fight again than wholly weaned from it, it is all too plain that you have been at work with your own soul, but Jesus Christ and his Spirit were not there. Indeed, often nature, having done its work, will come for its reward before many days are over; and having been busy in the work of healing, it will be ready to argue for a new wounding. When God speaks peace, there comes with it so much sweetness, and such a revelation of his love,15 that it lays a strong obligation on the soul to deal perversely no more.

(3) We speak peace to ourselves when we do it slightly. The prophet complains of this in some teachers: Jer 6:14, “They dress the wound of My people slightly.” And it is so with some people. They make the healing of their wounds a slight work: a look, a glance of faith at the promises does it, and the matter is ended. The apostle tells us that the word did not profit some, “since it was not mixed with faith” (Heb 4:2): μὴ συγκεκραμένος, “it was not well blended” and mingled with faith. It is not a mere look at the word of mercy in the promise. It must be mingled with faith until it is taken into the very nature of it; and then indeed it does the soul good. If you have had a wound on your conscience, which brought weakness and trouble, and you are now free of it, how did you come to be free? “I looked to the promises of pardon and healing, and so I found peace.” Yes, but perhaps you have made too much haste. You have done it superficially; you have not fed on the promise so as to mix it with faith, and to have all its power spread through your soul. You have done it only slightly. Before long you will find your wound breaking out again, and you will know that you are not cured.

(4) Whoever speaks peace to himself on any one account, while at the same time another evil of no less importance lies on his spirit about which he has had no dealings with God, that man cries “Peace” when there is none. To explain my meaning a little: A man has neglected a duty again and again, perhaps, when in all righteousness he owed it. His conscience is perplexed, his soul wounded; he has no rest in his bones because of his sin. He seeks healing and finds peace. Yet all the while, perhaps, worldliness or pride or some other folly, by which the Spirit of God is greatly grieved, may lie in that man’s bosom, and they do not disturb him, nor he them. Let that man not think that any of his peace is from God.

Then it will be well with men, when they have an equal regard for all God’s commandments. God will justify us from our sins, but he will not justify the least sin in us. He is a God of eyes too pure to look on iniquity.

(5) When men speak peace to their own consciences, God seldom speaks humbling to their souls. God’s peace is humbling peace, melting peace, as it was with David (Ps 51:1). He was never so deeply humbled as when Nathan brought him the news of his pardon.

Q. But you+ will say, “When may we take the comfort of a promise as our own, in relation to some particular wound, to quiet the heart?”

Ans. 1. In general, when God speaks it, whenever that may be, sooner or later. I told you+ before that he may do it at the very moment of the sin itself, and with such irresistible power that the soul cannot help receiving his mind in it. Sometimes he will make us wait longer. But when he speaks, sooner or later, whether we are sinning or repenting, whatever the condition of our souls, if God speaks, he must be received. There is nothing in our communion with him that the Lord is more troubled with us for, if I may say so, than our unbelieving fears, which keep us from receiving the strong consolation he is so willing to give us.

But you+ will say, “We are where we were. When God speaks it, we must receive it; that is true. But how shall we know when he speaks?”

Ans. 1. I wish we could all come up to this in practice: to receive peace when we are convinced that God speaks it, and that it is our duty to receive it. But further:

(2.) There is, if I may put it so, a secret instinct in faith by which it knows the voice of Christ when he really speaks. As the baby leaped in the womb when the blessed Virgin came to Elizabeth, so faith leaps in the heart when Christ really draws near to it. “My sheep,” says Christ, “know my voice” (John 10:4). They “know my voice”; they are used to the sound of it. And they know when his lips are opened to them and are full of grace. The bride was in a sad condition in Song 5:2, asleep in complacency. Yet as soon as Christ speaks, she cries, “It is the voice of my beloved!” She knew his voice, and was so well acquainted with communion with him that she recognized him at once. And so will you+. If you+ exercise yourselves+ in acquaintance and communion with him, you+ will easily tell his voice from the voice of a stranger. And take this κριτήριον [test] with you+: when he does speak, he speaks as no man ever spoke. He speaks with power, and one way or another he will make your+ “hearts burn within” you+, as he did to the disciples (Luke 24). He does it by putting his hand in through the opening of the door (Song 5:4), that is, his Spirit into your+ hearts, to take hold of you+.

He who has his senses trained to distinguish good from evil, having grown in judgment and experience by constantly observing the ways of Christ’s dealings with the soul, the manner of the Spirit’s workings, and the effects he usually produces, is the best judge for himself in this case.

(2.) If the word of the Lord does your+ souls good, he speaks it: if it humbles, if it cleanses, and if it serves the ends for which promises are given, namely, 1. to endear, 2. to cleanse, 3. to melt and bind to obedience, 4. to self-emptying, and so on. But this is not my business, and I will not turn aside any further in pursuing this direction. If it is not observed, sin will gain great advantages toward hardening the heart.

Chapter XIV

The general use of the foregoing directions. The great direction for accomplishing the work aimed at. Exercise faith on Christ; the various ways this may be done. Consideration of the fullness in Christ for relief proposed. Great expectations from Christ: grounds of these expectations. His mercifulness, his faithfulness. What comes of such expectations: on the part of Christ; on the part of believers. Faith to be exercised especially on the death of Christ: Rom 6:3, 4, 5, 6. The work of the Spirit in this whole business.

Now, the considerations I have dwelt on so far are things preparatory to the work aimed at, rather than things that will accomplish it. What I have aimed at so far is the heart’s proper preparation for the work itself, without which it will not be accomplished.

The directions for the work itself are very few; I mean those that belong particularly to it. They are these:

The Work Itself: Exercise Faith on Christ

1. First, set faith to work on Christ for the killing of your sin. His blood is the great sovereign remedy for sin-sick souls. Live in this, and you will die a conqueror; indeed, through the good providence of God, you will live to see your lust dead at your feet. But you will say, “How shall faith act on Christ for this end and purpose?” I say, in several ways.

(1.) By faith fill your soul with a proper consideration of the provision that is laid up in Jesus Christ for this end and purpose: that all your lusts, this very lust you are entangled with, may be mortified by faith. Ponder this: though you are in no way able in or by yourself to get the victory over your disorder, though you are even weary of fighting and utterly ready to faint, yet there is enough in Jesus Christ to give you relief (Phil 4:13). It held up the prodigal, when he was ready to faint (Luke 15:17), that there was still bread enough in his father’s house. Though he was far from it, it relieved him and held him up that it was there. In your greatest distress and anguish, consider the fullness of grace, the riches, the treasures of strength, might, and help that are laid up in him for our support16 (John 1:16; Col 1:19). Let them come into your mind and stay there. Consider that he is “exalted … as Prince and Savior, in order to grant repentance … to Israel” (Acts 5:31); and if to grant repentance, then to grant mortification, without which repentance does not and cannot exist. Christ tells us that we obtain purging grace by abiding in him (John 15:3). To exercise faith on the fullness that is in Christ for our supply is an outstanding way of abiding in Christ; for both our being grafted in and our abiding are by faith (Rom 11:19, 20). So let your soul be exercised by faith with thoughts and perceptions such as these: “I am a poor, weak creature. Unstable as water, I cannot excel. This corruption is too hard for me, and it is at the very point of ruining my soul; and I do not know what to do. My soul has become like parched ground, a haunt of jackals. I have made promises and broken them; vows and commitments have come to nothing. Many times I have been persuaded that I had won the victory and would be delivered, but I have been deceived. So I see plainly that without some great help and aid I am lost, and shall be brought at last to abandon God altogether. Yet, though this is my state and condition, let the hands that hang down be lifted up,17 and the feeble knees be strengthened. Look: the Lord Christ, who has all fullness of grace in his heart and all fullness of power in his hand, is able to slay all these enemies of his. There is enough provision in him for my relief and help. He can take my drooping, dying soul and make me more than a conqueror.18 ‘Why do you say, O my soul, “My way is hidden from the Lord, and my claim is ignored by my God”? Do you not know? Have you not heard? The Lord is the everlasting God, the Creator of the ends of the earth. He will not grow tired or weary; His understanding is beyond searching out. He gives power to the faint and increases the strength of the weak. Even youths grow tired and weary, and young men stumble and fall. But those who wait upon the Lord will renew their strength; they will mount up with wings like eagles; they will run and not grow weary, they will walk and not faint’ (Isa 40:27–31). He can make ‘the parched ground’ of my soul ‘become a pool,’ and my thirsty, barren heart ‘springs of water.’ Indeed, he can make this ‘haunt where jackals once lay,’ this heart so full of abominable lusts and fiery temptations, a place of ‘grass’ and fruit for himself” (Isa 35). So God held Paul up under his temptation with the thought of the sufficiency of his grace: “My grace is sufficient for you” (2 Cor 12:9). Though Paul was not at once given so much of it as to be freed from his temptation, yet its sufficiency in God for that end was enough to steady his spirit. I say, then: by faith, think much on the supply that is in Jesus Christ and its fullness, and how he can at any time give you strength and deliverance. Now if by this you do not find success to the point of victory, yet you will be kept upright in the chariot, so that you will not flee from the field until the battle is over. You will be kept from utter despondency, from lying down under your unbelief, or from turning aside to false means and remedies that in the end will not relieve you. The efficacy of this consideration will be found only in practice.

(2.) By faith, lift up your heart to expect relief from Christ. Relief from Christ in this case is like the prophet’s vision: “For the vision awaits an appointed time; it testifies of the end and does not lie. Though it lingers, wait for it, since it will surely come and will not delay” (Hab 2:3). Though it may seem long to you while you are in your trouble and perplexity, it will surely come at the time the Lord Jesus has appointed, which is the best time. So if you can lift your heart to a settled expectation of relief from Jesus Christ, if your eyes are on him “as the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master” (Ps 123:2) when they expect to receive something from him, your soul will be satisfied (Isa 7:8). He will surely deliver you. He will slay the lust, and your end will be peace. Only look for it from his hand, and wait for when and how he will do it. “If you+ do not stand firm in your+ faith, then you+ will not stand at all.”

Q. But you will say, “What ground do I have to build such an expectation on, so that I may expect not to be deceived?”

Ans. You have necessity to drive you on this course (John 6:68): you must be relieved and saved this way or not at all. To whom will you go? And there are also countless things in the Lord Jesus to encourage you and draw you to this expectation.

As for its necessity, I have partly shown it already, when I made clear that this is the work of faith and of believers only. “Apart from Me you+ can do nothing,” says Christ (John 15:5), speaking with special reference to the purging of the heart from sin (verse 2). The mortification of any sin must come by a supply of grace; of ourselves we cannot do it. Now God “was pleased to have all His fullness dwell in” Christ (Col 1:19), so that “from His fullness we have all received grace upon grace” (John 1:16). He is the head from whom the new man must receive the influences of life and strength, or it will decay every day. If we are strengthened with power in the inner being (Col 1:11), it is by Christ dwelling in our hearts through faith (Eph 3:16, 17). I have also shown before that this work is not to be done without the Spirit. From where, then, do we expect the Spirit? From whom do we look for him? Who has promised him to us, having obtained him for us? Ought not all our expectations in this to be on Christ alone? So let this be fixed in your heart: if you do not have relief from him, you will never have any. All ways, efforts, and strivings that are not driven by this expectation of relief from Christ, and from him only, are to no purpose and will do you no good. Indeed, if they are anything other than supports of your heart in this expectation, or means he himself has appointed for receiving help from him, they are in vain.

Now, to draw you further to this expectation.

(1.) Consider his mercifulness, tenderness, and kindness, as he is our great High Priest at the right hand of God. Certainly he pities you in your distress. He says, “As a mother comforts her son, so will I comfort you+” (Isa 66:13). He has the tenderness of a mother toward a nursing child. Heb 2:17, 18: “For this reason He had to be made like His brothers in every way, so that He might become a merciful and faithful high priest in service to God, in order to make atonement for the sins of the people. Because He Himself suffered when He was tempted, He is able to help those who are being tempted.” How is Christ’s ability, on account of his suffering, set before us? “Because He Himself suffered when He was tempted, He is able.” Did the sufferings and temptations of Christ add to his ability and power? Certainly not, considered absolutely and in itself. But the ability mentioned here is one that has readiness, inclination, and willingness to put itself forth going along with it; it is an ability of will against all that would dissuade. Having suffered and been tempted, he is able to break through everything that would dissuade him, to relieve poor tempted souls: δύναται βοηθῆσαι, “He is able to help.” It is a metonymy, naming the effect: he can now be moved to help, having been tempted himself. So in ch. 4:15, 16: “For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but we have one who was tempted in every way that we are, yet was without sin. Let us then approach the throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need.” The exhortation of verse 16 is the same one I am pressing: that we should hold on to expectations of relief from Christ. The apostle there calls it χάριν εἰς εὔκαιρον βοήθειαν, “grace for timely help.” “If ever help was timely,” says the soul, “it would be so for me in my present condition. This is what I long for: grace for timely help. I am ready to die, to perish, to be lost forever. Iniquity will prevail against me if help does not come.” The apostle says, “Expect this help, this relief, this grace from Christ.” But on what ground? The one he lays down in verse 15. And we may observe that the word in verse 16 that the older English translation renders “obtain” is λάβωμεν: ἵνα λάβωμεν ἔλεον, “That we may receive it”; suitable and timely help will come. I will say freely: this one thing, establishing the soul by faith in the expectation of relief from Jesus Christ,19 on account of his mercifulness as our high priest, will do more to ruin your lust and disorder, and bring a better and speedier result, than all the severest means of punishing the body that any of the sons of men ever bound themselves to. Indeed, let me add that no soul ever did or ever will perish by the power of any lust,20 sin, or corruption who could lift his soul by faith to an expectation of relief from Jesus Christ.

(2.) Consider the faithfulness of Him who has promised; this may lift you up and confirm you in waiting with an expectation of relief. He has promised to relieve in such cases, and he will fulfill his word to the utmost. God tells us that his covenant with us is like the “fixed order” of heaven, the sun, moon, and stars, which have their sure courses (Jer 31:36). So David said that he watched for relief from God “as watchmen wait for the morning,” something that will certainly come in its appointed time. So will your relief from Christ. It will come in its season, like the dew and rain on parched ground; for he who has promised is faithful. Particular promises to this purpose are countless. Let the soul always be furnished with some of them that seem especially to suit its condition.

Now there are two great advantages that always go with this expectation of help from Jesus Christ.

[1.] It binds him to give full and speedy help. Nothing binds a man’s heart more to be useful and helpful to another than that other’s expectation of help from him, if it has been rightly raised and encouraged by the one who is to give the relief. Our Lord Jesus has raised our hearts to this expectation by his kindness, care, and promises. Surely our rising up to it must be a strong obligation on him to help us accordingly. The Psalmist gives us this as a proven maxim: “You, O Lord, have never forsaken those who trust in You.” When the heart is once won to rest in God, to lay itself on him, he will certainly satisfy it. He will never be like water that fails; nor has he ever said to the descendants of Jacob, “Seek Me in vain.” If Christ is chosen as the foundation of our supply, he will not fail us.

[2.] It binds the heart to attend diligently to all the ways and means by which Christ usually imparts himself to the soul; and so it takes in the real help of all graces and ordinances whatever. He who expects anything from a man applies himself to the ways and means by which it may be obtained. The beggar who expects alms lies at the door, or in the way, of the man he expects it from. The way and the means by which Christ imparts himself are ordinarily his ordinances; he who expects anything from him must wait on him in them. It is the expectation of faith that sets the heart to work. It is not an idle, groundless hope I am speaking of. Now if there is any vigor, efficacy, and power in prayer or the sacraments for this end of mortifying sin, a man will certainly have his share in it all through this expectation of relief from Christ. For this reason I bring all particular exercises, such as prayer, meditation, and the like, under this head, and so I will not dwell on them further. When they are grounded on this foundation and spring from this root, they are of singular use for this purpose, and not otherwise.

Now for this direction for mortifying a prevailing disorder, you+ may have a thousand “probatum est”s [it is proved]. Who has walked with God under this temptation and not found its use and success? I dare leave the soul with it, without adding anything more. Only some particulars relating to it may be mentioned.

(1) Exercise faith especially on the death, blood, and cross of Christ; that is, on Christ as crucified and slain. The mortification of sin comes especially from the death of Christ. It is one particular, indeed outstanding, purpose of the death of Christ, and it will certainly be accomplished by it. He died to destroy the works of the devil. Whatever came on our natures through the devil’s first temptation, whatever gains strength in our persons by his daily suggestions, Christ died to destroy it all. “He gave Himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for Himself a people for His own possession, zealous for good deeds” (Titus 2:14). This was his aim and intention in giving himself for us, and in it he will not fail: his design was that we might be freed from the power of our sins and purified from all our defiling lusts. Christ “gave Himself up for” the church “to sanctify her, cleansing her …, and to present her to Himself as a glorious church, without stain or wrinkle or any such blemish, but holy and blameless” (Eph 5:25–27). And this will be accomplished, by virtue of his death, in various and different degrees. That is why our washing, purging, and cleansing are everywhere ascribed to his blood (1 John 1:7; Heb 1:3; Rev 1:5). Sprinkled on us, it will “purge our consciences from works of death, so that we may serve the living God” (Heb 9:14). This is what we aim at, what we are pursuing: that our consciences may be purged from works of death, that these may be rooted out and destroyed and have no more place in us. This will certainly be brought about by the death of Christ; power will go out from there for this purpose. Indeed, all supplies of the Spirit,21 all grace and power imparted to us, come from there, as I have shown elsewhere. The apostle sets it out like this. In Rom 6:2 the case we have in hand is put: “How can we who are dead to sin live in it any longer?” That is, dead to sin by what we profess; dead to sin by the obligation to be so; dead to sin by sharing in the power and strength for killing it; dead to sin by union with Christ and a share in him, in whom and by whom it is killed: how shall we live in it? He presses this in the verses that follow by several considerations, all taken from the death of Christ. This must not be: verse 3, “Or aren’t you+ aware that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into His death?” In baptism we have an evidence of our being planted into Christ; we are baptized into him. But into a share in what of his are we baptized? “His death,” he says. If indeed we are baptized into Christ, beyond an outward profession of faith, we are baptized into his death. The apostle explains what it is to be baptized into the death of Christ in verses 4, 5: “We were therefore buried with Him through baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may walk in newness of life. … We know that our old man was crucified with Him so that the body of sin might be rendered powerless, that we should no longer be slaves to sin.” This, he says, is our being baptized into the death of Christ: our conformity to it. It is to be dead to sin, to have our corruptions mortified, as he was put to death for sin; so that as he was raised up to glory, we may be raised up to grace and newness of life.

In verse 6 he tells us where this baptism into the death of Christ comes from; it comes from the death of Christ itself: “Our old man was crucified with Him so that the body of sin might be rendered powerless.” συνεσταυρώθη, “was crucified with Him,” not in respect of time, but of causality. We are crucified with him meritoriously, in that he obtained the Spirit for us to mortify sin; effectually, in that power comes from his death for our crucifying; and by way of representation and example, we shall certainly be crucified to sin, as he was for our sin. This is what the apostle intends. Christ by his death, destroying the works of the devil and obtaining the Spirit for us, has so killed sin as to its reign in believers that it will not achieve its end and dominion.

(2) Then exercise faith on the death of Christ, and that under these two aspects:22

  1. In expectation of power.
  2. In striving for conformity.

For the first, the general direction already given may be enough.

As for the second, the apostle’s words in Gal 3:1 may give us some light for our direction. Let faith look on Christ in the gospel as he is set forth dying and crucified for us.23 Look on him under the weight of our sins, praying, bleeding, dying. By faith bring him in that condition into your heart; apply his blood, so shed, to your corruptions. Do this daily. I could draw out this consideration at great length, in several particulars, but I must come to a close.

The Work of the Spirit

I have only, then, to add the main points of the work of the Spirit in this business of mortification, which is so especially ascribed to him.

In a word: this whole work, which I have described as our duty, is brought about, carried on, and accomplished by the power of the Spirit, in all its parts and degrees. For example:

1. He alone clearly and fully convicts the heart of the evil, guilt, and danger of the corruption, lust, or sin to be mortified. Without this conviction, or while it is so faint that the heart can wrestle with it or digest it, no thorough work will be done. An unbelieving heart (and in part we all have such a heart) will find a way around any consideration until it is overpowered by clear and evident convictions. Now this is the proper work of the Spirit: “He will … convict … in regard to sin” (John 16:8). He alone can do it. If men’s rational considerations, together with the preaching of the letter, could convict them of sin, we would perhaps see more convictions than we do. Through the preaching of the word, men come to recognize that they are sinners, that such and such things are sins, and that they themselves are guilty of them. But this light is not powerful. It does not take hold of the practical principles of the soul so as to conform the mind and will to them, and to produce effects suitable to such an awareness. That is why wise and knowing men who lack the Spirit do not think those things to be sins at all in which the chief movements and workings of lust consist. It is the Spirit alone who can do this work effectually, and who does it. And this is the first thing the Spirit does toward the mortification of any lust whatever. He convicts the soul of all the evil of the lust, cuts off all its pleas, exposes all its deceits, stops all its evasions, answers its excuses, and makes the soul acknowledge its abomination and lie down under the sense of it. Unless this is done, all that follows is in vain.

2. The Spirit alone reveals to us the fullness of Christ for our relief. It is this consideration that keeps the heart from false ways and from despairing despondency (1 Cor 2:8).

3. The Spirit alone establishes the heart in the expectation of relief from Christ, which, as has been shown, is the great sovereign means of mortification (2 Cor 1:21).

4. The Spirit alone brings the cross of Christ into our hearts with its sin-killing power; for it is by the Spirit that we are baptized into the death of Christ.

5. The Spirit is the author and finisher of our sanctification. He gives new supplies and influences of grace for holiness and sanctification when the contrary principle is weakened and brought low (Eph 3:16–18).

6. In all the soul’s approaches to God in this condition, it has support from the Spirit. Where do the power, life, and vigor of prayer come from? Where does its efficacy to prevail with God come from? Is it not from the Spirit? He is the Spirit of supplication, promised to those who look on him whom they have pierced (Zech 12:10), enabling them to pray “with groans too deep for words” (Rom 8:26). This is acknowledged to be the great means, or way, by which faith prevails with God. This is how Paul dealt with his temptation, whatever it was: “I pleaded with the Lord to take it away from me” (2 Cor 12:8). What the work of the Spirit in prayer is, from where and how he gives us help and makes us prevail, and what we must do to enjoy his help for that purpose, it is not my present intention to show.
